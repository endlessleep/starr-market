Part I — Choice Theory

1. Preferences

Preference relations, rationality, and their potential failures.

1.1 Preference Relations
Preferences only tell us how alternatives are ranked relative to each other ($x$ over $y$). They do not measure absolute satisfaction, emotional liking, or how strongly (intensity) one option is preferred over another.
Let $X$ be the set of alternatives.
Weak preference
$x \succeq y$ "$x$ is at least as good as $y$."
$\boxed{x \succ y \quad \text{or} \quad x \sim y}$
Strict preference
$x \succ y \iff x \succeq y \text{ and } y \not\succeq x$ "$x$ is strictly preferred to $y$."
Indifference
$x \sim y \iff x \succeq y \text{ and } y \succeq x$ "The agent is indifferent between $x$ and $y$."
$\succeq$ Weak preference — general relation
$\succ$ Strict preference only
$\sim$ Indifference only
1.2 Rationality

A preference relation is rational if it satisfies two axioms:

Completeness
For any $x, y \in X$:
$x \succeq y \quad \text{or} \quad y \succeq x \quad$ (or both)
Any pair of alternatives can be compared.  "Both" corresponds to $x \sim y$.
Transitivity
$x \succeq y \text{ and } y \succeq z \Rightarrow x \succeq z$
Rankings must be logically consistent. Transitivity rules out strict preference cycles.
$\boxed{\text{Rational preferences} = \text{Complete} + \text{Transitive}}$

An agent faces two options: bundle $A$ (more food, less leisure) and bundle $B$ (less food, more leisure).

Completeness requires that the agent can say one of: $A \succeq B$, $B \succeq A$, or both (indifference). The agent cannot simply be unable to compare them — that would be incompleteness, not indifference.

An agent prefers $A$ over $B$, and $B$ over $C$:

$A \succeq B \text{ and } B \succeq C$

Transitivity requires: $A \succeq C$

If instead the agent preferred $C$ over $A$, we would have a cycle $A \succ B \succ C \succ A$ — a contradiction with transitivity.

1.3 Incomplete Preferences

A preference relation is incomplete when some pairs of alternatives cannot be ranked.

That is, for some $x, y$:   neither $x \succeq y$  nor  $y \succeq x$ holds.

Potential reasons for incompleteness
  • Cognitive limitations — The alternatives are too complex or difficult for the agent to mentally process and rank.
  • Lack of information — The agent does not have enough information about the alternatives to form a decision.
Important distinction
Indifference  $x \sim y$
The agent can compare $x$ and $y$, and considers them equally good.
Incompleteness
The agent cannot form any ranking between $x$ and $y$.

Empirical evidence suggests that completeness may fail in practice.

Nielsen & Rigotti (2023) — participants choose between:

  • "I am indifferent" — the agent can compare but assigns equal value
  • "I don't know" — the agent genuinely cannot form a ranking

Goal: distinguish true indifference from genuine incompleteness. The experiment uses real monetary incentives — choices affect actual payment — to ensure the stated preferences are revealed, not hypothetical.

1.4 Intransitive Preferences

Preferences are intransitive if transitivity fails — there exist $x, y, z$ such that $x \succeq y$ and $y \succeq z$ but $x \not\succeq z$, creating a preference cycle.

1. Incomplete preferences
When an agent cannot compare certain items in a chain, a complete ranking cannot be established, breaking logical transitivity.
2. Just perceptible differences
Tiny pairwise differences may be individually imperceptible ($x \sim y$ and $y \sim z$), but compounding them across multiple steps creates a noticeable difference ($x \succ z$), producing a cycle.

Consider a sequence of nearby grey shades: $g_1, g_2, \dots, g_{10}$, each slightly darker than the previous.

  • Each adjacent pair $g_i \sim g_{i+1}$ — the difference is imperceptible.
  • But $g_1 \succ g_{10}$ — the first and last are clearly different.

Chaining the indifferences gives $g_1 \sim g_{10}$ by transitivity — which contradicts the direct ranking $g_1 \succ g_{10}$.

3. Framing
Presenting economically identical options in different contexts or frames can alter choices, creating apparent inconsistencies in rankings.

A stereo costs \$125, a calculator costs \$15. A nearby store offers a \$5 discount.

  • Most people travel to get the discount when it applies to the calculator:  $\text{Calculator-Discount} \succ \text{No Discount}$
  • Most people do not bother when it applies to the stereo:  $\text{No Discount} \succ \text{Stereo-Discount}$
  • Yet the direct comparison often gives:  $\text{Calculator-Discount} \sim \text{Stereo-Discount}$

This is inconsistent with transitivity — the saved amount is identical (\$5) in both cases, yet the framing reverses the choice.

4. Condorcet Paradox
Majority voting across individuals can generate cyclic social preferences, even if every individual voter is strictly rational and transitive.

Three agents vote over: Opera ($O$), Rock ($R$), Ice-skating ($I$).

  • Mom: $O \succ R \succ I$
  • Dad: $I \succ O \succ R$
  • Child: $R \succ I \succ O$

Pairwise majority votes:

$O \succ R, \quad R \succ I, \quad I \succ O$

Hence: $O \succ R \succ I \succ O$ — a cycle. The intransitivity arises at the collective level, not the individual level.

5. Changes in preferences over time
Preferences fluctuate or reverse between decision moments, making choices made at different times appear as an intransitive cycle.

A smoker's ranking after just finishing a cigarette:

$\text{NoCig} \succ \text{OneCig} \succ \text{TenCig}$

After a period of abstinence:

$\text{TenCig} \succ \text{NoCig} \succ \text{OneCig}$

The ranking is not stable — it reverses over time. What looks like an intransitive cycle is in fact a preference that has shifted between observations.
