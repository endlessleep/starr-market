Exercise 1

Exercise 1. General Equilibrium

Thomas and Juliette consume radishes \(R\) and carrots \(C\). Their utility functions are:

\[\begin{gathered} u_t(R,C)=R^\alpha C^{1-\alpha},\qquad0<\alpha<1,\\ u_j(R,C)=R^\beta C^{1-\beta},\qquad0<\beta<1. \end{gathered}\]

Initially, Thomas has 1 radish and 9 carrots, while Juliette has 9 radishes and 1 carrot:

\[\begin{gathered} (\overline R_t,\overline C_t)=(1,9),\\ (\overline R_j,\overline C_j)=(9,1). \end{gathered}\]

Questions

  1. What are the initial utility levels of Thomas and Juliette?
  2. Draw an Edgeworth box representing Thomas' and Juliette's indifference curves and the endowment allocation. For this question only, assume \(\alpha=\beta=1/2\). Shade the allocations that would be Pareto improvements over the endowment allocation.
  3. Thomas and Juliette sell or buy vegetables at prices \(p_R\) and \(p_C\). What relative price \(p^*=p_C/p_R\) clears both markets?
  4. Assume \(\alpha=\beta\) and normalize prices so that \(p_R^*=1\) and \(p_C^*=p_C/p_R=p^*\). Show that allowing trade leads to a Pareto improvement compared with the initial situation.

    Hint: for \(0<\alpha=\beta<1\),

    \[\begin{gathered} 9-8\alpha>9^{1-\alpha},\\ 8\beta+1>9^\beta. \end{gathered}\]
  5. Still assuming \(\alpha=\beta\), describe the change in the normalized equilibrium price \(p_C^*\) and equilibrium demands when \(\alpha=\beta\) increases. What is the intuition for these changes?
  6. Now assume \(\alpha\neq\beta\) and change the initial endowments: Thomas has 9 radishes and 9 carrots, while Juliette has 1 radish and 1 carrot. Does allowing trade lead to a Pareto improvement?

Initial utility levels

Goal: Find each person’s utility before trade.

Start: Use each utility function with that person’s own initial endowment.

Thomas starts with (1,9), while Juliette starts with (9,1):

\[\begin{aligned} u_t(1,9)&=1^\alpha9^{1-\alpha}=\boxed{9^{1-\alpha}},\\ u_j(9,1)&=9^\beta1^{1-\beta}=\boxed{9^\beta}. \end{aligned}\]

The power of 1 is 1. These are the starting utility levels. Later, compare each person’s final utility with their own starting level to measure gains from trade.

The Edgeworth box and gains from trade

Goal: Draw the initial allocation and shade the trades that improve on it.

Start: For this question only, \(\alpha=\beta=1/2\), so utility is \(\sqrt{RC}\).

Total resources are 10 radishes and 10 carrots, so the box has width 10 and height 10. Both starting utilities equal \(\sqrt{1\times9}=3\). The number 3 is a utility level, not a coordinate in the box.

Thomas’s origin \(O_t\) is at the bottom-left. Juliette’s origin \(O_j\) is at the top-right. Every point represents two bundles: \((R_t,C_t)\) for Thomas and \((10-R_t,10-C_t)\) for Juliette.

To draw the two initial indifference curves, set each person’s utility equal to 3. Squaring gives \(RC=9\). Write both curves using Thomas’s coordinates:

\[\begin{gathered} R_tC_t=9\quad\Longrightarrow\quad C_t=\frac9{R_t},\\ (10-R_t)(10-C_t)=9\\ \Longrightarrow\quad C_t=10-\frac9{10-R_t}. \end{gathered}\]

Read the two intersections from the correct origin

To locate both intersections, solve the two curve equations together. Since \(R_tC_t=9\), expanding \((10-R_t)(10-C_t)=9\) gives \(R_t+C_t=10\). Therefore:

\[R_t(10-R_t)=9 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad(R_t-1)(R_t-9)=0.\]

The intersections are \((1,9)\) and \((9,1)\) in Thomas’s coordinates. The first is the initial allocation \(\omega\); call the second \(Z\):

PointThomas — from \(O_t\)Juliette — from \(O_j\)
Initial \(\omega\)\((1,9)\)\((9,1)\)
Other intersection \(Z\)\((9,1)\)\((1,9)\)

Juliette’s initial bundle \((9,1)\) is read from her origin. The label \(Z=(9,1)\) is read from Thomas’s origin. They are different allocations, even though the written pair is the same.

Read the diagram — Thomas and Juliette

  • Thomas’ indifference curve through the endowment.
  • Juliette’s indifference curve through the endowment.
  • Area where neither person is worse off than at the start.
  1. Read the opposite origins. Right and up mean more goods for Thomas. Left and down mean more goods for Juliette. Each point represents what both people consume at the same time.
  2. Use \(\omega\) as the reference. Both coloured curves pass through the initial endowment. Moving along either curve leaves that consumer’s utility unchanged.
  3. Read the green lens. Thomas prefers points above the blue curve; Juliette prefers points below the red curve. Inside the green area, both are better off. On an edge belonging to just one curve, one person gains and the other keeps the same utility.
  4. Distinguish improvement from efficiency. The lens shows improvements relative to the initial allocation. The contract curve contains efficient allocations, where no further Pareto improvement is possible. It is not drawn here: being inside the lens does not by itself mean being efficient.

A Pareto improvement is a feasible allocation satisfying:

\[\begin{gathered} \sqrt{R_tC_t}\geq3,\\ \sqrt{(10-R_t)(10-C_t)}\geq3, \end{gathered}\]

with at least one strict inequality. Shade the region between the curves from \(\omega\) to \(Z\). Its interior makes both people strictly better off. On either curved edge, one gains and the other keeps the same utility. Exclude \(\omega\) and \(Z\) themselves: both utilities remain 3 there.

From the maximization problems to equilibrium prices

Goal: Find the relative price at which both markets clear.

Start: Derive the two consumers’ demands from their budgets, then set total demand equal to total resources.

1. Write Thomas’ choice and budget

Thomas chooses how many radishes \(R_t\) and carrots \(C_t\) to consume. He takes the positive prices \(p_R,p_C\) as given. His initial goods are worth \(p_R\times1+p_C\times9=p_R+9p_C\). This value is his budget:

\[\begin{gathered} \max_{R_t,C_t\geq0}\quad R_t^\alpha C_t^{1-\alpha}\\ \text{subject to}\quad p_RR_t+p_CC_t\leq p_R+9p_C. \end{gathered}\]

The left side is what he spends. The right side is what he can spend by selling his initial goods. He does not have to sell everything first: this budget also allows him to keep some of his goods.

Both goods give him utility because \(0<\alpha<1\). At the best choice, both quantities are positive: a zero amount of either good would give zero utility, while he can afford some of both. He also spends the full budget. Otherwise, he could buy more and increase utility.

2. Build Thomas’ Lagrangian

Write utility plus a multiplier times the unused budget. Keep the original utility function:

\[\begin{aligned} \mathcal L_t={}&R_t^\alpha C_t^{1-\alpha}\\ &+\lambda_t\big(p_R+9p_C-p_RR_t-p_CC_t\big). \end{aligned}\]

The expression in brackets is income minus spending. The multiplier \(\lambda_t\) measures how much extra utility a small increase in the budget would bring at the optimum.

For this interior choice, take the derivative with respect to each quantity and to the multiplier, then set them equal to zero:

\[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\mathcal L_t}{\partial R_t} &=\alpha R_t^{\alpha-1}C_t^{1-\alpha}-\lambda_t p_R=0,\\ \frac{\partial\mathcal L_t}{\partial C_t} &=(1-\alpha)R_t^\alpha C_t^{-\alpha}-\lambda_t p_C=0,\\ \frac{\partial\mathcal L_t}{\partial\lambda_t} &=p_R+9p_C-p_RR_t-p_CC_t=0. \end{aligned}\]

Use the power rule: the derivative of \(R_t^\alpha\) is \(\alpha R_t^{\alpha-1}\). When differentiating with respect to \(R_t\), hold \(C_t\) fixed, and hold radishes fixed when taking the carrot derivative. The last equation gives the full-budget condition.

3. Remove the multiplier and find Thomas’ demands

Divide the carrot condition by the radish condition. This cancels \(\lambda_t\). The ratio is \(MU_C/MU_R\): radishes given up for a little more carrots. This is the MRS convention used again in Question 6:

\[\begin{aligned} \frac{(1-\alpha)R_t^\alpha C_t^{-\alpha}} {\alpha R_t^{\alpha-1}C_t^{1-\alpha}} &=\frac{\lambda_t p_C}{\lambda_t p_R},\\ \frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\frac{R_t}{C_t} &=\frac{p_C}{p_R},\\ p_CC_t&=\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}p_RR_t. \end{aligned}\]

The last line links spending on carrots to spending on radishes. Put it into the budget:

\[\begin{aligned} p_RR_t+\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}p_RR_t&=p_R+9p_C,\\ p_RR_t\left(1+\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\right)&=p_R+9p_C,\\ \frac{p_RR_t}{\alpha}&=p_R+9p_C,\\ p_RR_t&=\alpha(p_R+9p_C). \end{aligned}\]

Thomas spends the share \(\alpha\) of his budget on radishes. The remaining share \(1-\alpha\) goes to carrots. Divide spending by the price of each good to get quantities:

\[\begin{gathered} \boxed{R_t=\frac{\alpha(p_R+9p_C)}{p_R} =\alpha\left(1+9\frac{p_C}{p_R}\right)},\\ \boxed{C_t=\frac{(1-\alpha)(p_R+9p_C)}{p_C} =(1-\alpha)\left(\frac{p_R}{p_C}+9\right)}. \end{gathered}\]

4. Write and solve Juliette’s problem

Juliette owns 9 radishes and 1 carrot, so her budget is \(9p_R+p_C\). Her preference parameter is \(\beta\):

\[\begin{gathered} \max_{R_j,C_j\geq0}\quad R_j^\beta C_j^{1-\beta}\\ \text{subject to}\quad p_RR_j+p_CC_j\leq9p_R+p_C. \end{gathered}\]

Her best choice also uses both goods and spends the full budget. Her Lagrangian is:

\[\begin{aligned} \mathcal L_j={}&R_j^\beta C_j^{1-\beta}\\ &+\lambda_j(9p_R+p_C-p_RR_j-p_CC_j). \end{aligned}\]
\[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\mathcal L_j}{\partial R_j} &=\beta R_j^{\beta-1}C_j^{1-\beta}-\lambda_j p_R=0,\\ \frac{\partial\mathcal L_j}{\partial C_j} &=(1-\beta)R_j^\beta C_j^{-\beta}-\lambda_j p_C=0,\\ \frac{\partial\mathcal L_j}{\partial\lambda_j} &=9p_R+p_C-p_RR_j-p_CC_j=0. \end{aligned}\]

Divide the second equation by the first, then use the budget as for Thomas:

\[\begin{gathered} \frac{1-\beta}{\beta}\frac{R_j}{C_j}=\frac{p_C}{p_R} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p_CC_j=\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}p_RR_j,\\ p_RR_j\left(1+\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}\right)=9p_R+p_C,\\ p_RR_j=\beta(9p_R+p_C),\qquad p_CC_j=(1-\beta)(9p_R+p_C),\\ \boxed{R_j=\beta\left(9+\frac{p_C}{p_R}\right)},\qquad \boxed{C_j=(1-\beta)\left(9\frac{p_R}{p_C}+1\right)}. \end{gathered}\]

For both consumers, these conditions give the unique best bundle. With these Cobb–Douglas preferences, the budget set is convex and the interior tangency gives a maximum.

5. Choose the price ratio that clears the radish market

Total radishes are fixed at 10. At equilibrium, the quantities Thomas and Juliette want must add to 10:

\[\begin{aligned} R_t+R_j&=10,\\ \alpha\left(1+9\frac{p_C}{p_R}\right) +\beta\left(9+\frac{p_C}{p_R}\right)&=10,\\ \alpha+9\beta+(9\alpha+\beta)\frac{p_C}{p_R}&=10,\\ (9\alpha+\beta)\frac{p_C}{p_R}&=10-\alpha-9\beta. \end{aligned}\]
\[\boxed{p^*=\frac{p_C}{p_R}=\frac{10-\alpha-9\beta}{9\alpha+\beta}}.\]

The denominator is positive. The numerator is also positive because \(\alpha+9\beta<10\). This determines the price of carrots relative to radishes. Multiplying both prices by the same positive number would not change the choices.

Both budgets are fully spent and prices are positive. By Walras’ law, clearing the radish market also clears the carrot market. The boxed ratio is the equilibrium relative price.

Optional calculation — Check the carrot market

Walras’ law says that, when both budgets are fully spent and prices are positive, clearing one of the two markets also clears the other. We can check this directly:

\[\begin{aligned} C_t+C_j &=(1-\alpha)\left(\frac1{p^*}+9\right) +(1-\beta)\left(\frac9{p^*}+1\right)\\ &=\frac{10-\alpha-9\beta}{p^*}+10-9\alpha-\beta\\ &=(9\alpha+\beta)+(10-9\alpha-\beta)=10. \end{aligned}\]

Demand equals the available amount for both vegetables. The relative price therefore clears both markets.

Equal preferences: both consumers gain

Goal: Show that each consumer is better off after trade.

Start: Use the demands and equilibrium price ratio from Question 3, now with \(\alpha=\beta\).

1. Find the relative price, then normalize

Equal parameters mean identical preferences. They do not by themselves mean that trade occurs: we must still compare the chosen bundles with the initial goods. Here, Thomas and Juliette start with different bundles.

First, put \(\beta=\alpha\) into the market-clearing ratio from Question 3. Then measure prices in units of radishes by setting \(p_R^*=1\):

\[p_R^*=1,\qquad p_C^*=\frac{10(1-\alpha)}{10\alpha}=\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}.\]

Normalization only chooses the price units. Market clearing has already determined the ratio. To find consumption after trading is allowed, evaluate each demand at these equilibrium prices. The demand functions give the best affordable bundles, and market clearing makes the two choices fit together. For carrot demands, use \(1/p_C^*=\alpha/(1-\alpha)\):

\[\begin{aligned} R_t^*&=\alpha+9(1-\alpha)=9-8\alpha,\\ C_t^*&=(1-\alpha)\left(\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}+9\right)=9-8\alpha,\\ R_j^*&=9\alpha+(1-\alpha)=1+8\alpha,\\ C_j^*&=(1-\alpha)\left(\frac{9\alpha}{1-\alpha}+1\right)=1+8\alpha. \end{aligned}\]

The star marks consumption at equilibrium, rather than the initial endowment. For \(0<\alpha<1\), Thomas’s radishes increase from 1 while his carrots fall from 9; Juliette makes the opposite trade. Each good still adds to 10.

2. Compare equilibrium and initial utilities

Since each consumer consumes equal amounts of both goods, the exponents add to one:

\[\begin{aligned} u_t^*&=(9-8\alpha)^\alpha(9-8\alpha)^{1-\alpha}\\ &=9-8\alpha>9^{1-\alpha}=u_t(1,9),\\ u_j^*&=(1+8\alpha)^\alpha(1+8\alpha)^{1-\alpha}\\ &=1+8\alpha>9^\alpha=u_j(9,1). \end{aligned}\]

The hints compare each final utility with that person’s own initial utility. Both inequalities are strict for a parameter between 0 and 1. The allocation is possible because each vegetable still adds to 10. Trade makes both consumers strictly better off.

A stronger preference for radishes

Goal: Find how the price and each person’s consumption change when the common parameter rises.

Start: Differentiate the equilibrium formulas from Question 4 with respect to the common value of α and β.

Increase the common preference parameter, keeping \(\alpha=\beta\). Using \(\alpha\) to write this common value:

\[p_C^*=\frac1\alpha-1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\boxed{\frac{d p_C^*}{d\alpha}=-\frac1{\alpha^2}<0}.\]

The derivative is negative, so the price of carrots falls compared with radishes. To find how quantities change, differentiate the four demands from Question 4:

\[\begin{gathered} R_t^*=C_t^*=9-8\alpha,\qquad \frac{dR_t^*}{d\alpha}=\frac{dC_t^*}{d\alpha}=-8,\\ R_j^*=C_j^*=1+8\alpha,\qquad \frac{dR_j^*}{d\alpha}=\frac{dC_j^*}{d\alpha}=8. \end{gathered}\]
Intuition. Both consumers put more weight on radishes, so carrots become cheaper relative to radishes. The price change favors Juliette’s radish-rich endowment relative to Thomas’s carrot-rich endowment. At the new equilibrium, Thomas consumes less of both goods and Juliette consumes more. The total stays at 10 units of each: the change in preferences affects prices and the sharing of goods, not the available quantities.

Can trade make Thomas and Juliette better off?

Goal: Determine whether a trade can make both consumers better off.

Start: Compare their MRS at the new initial endowments.

In this question, the starting goods have changed:

\[\omega_t=(9,9),\qquad\omega_j=(1,1),\qquad\alpha\neq\beta.\]

There are still 10 radishes and 10 carrots in total. Both people start with positive amounts of both goods. We check whether this starting allocation is Pareto efficient: can a trade help both people?

Main method — Compare the initial MRS

Step 1. Write the efficiency condition. For these smooth preferences, at an efficient point inside the box, the two MRS must be equal:

\[\boxed{MRS_t=MRS_j}.\]

Here we use the marginal utility of carrots divided by the marginal utility of radishes. This measures how many radishes a person is willing to give up for a little more carrots:

\[\begin{gathered} MRS_t=\frac{\partial u_t/\partial C_t}{\partial u_t/\partial R_t} =\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\frac{R_t}{C_t},\\ MRS_j=\frac{\partial u_j/\partial C_j}{\partial u_j/\partial R_j} =\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}\frac{R_j}{C_j}. \end{gathered}\]

Step 2. Use the starting quantities. Thomas has \((9,9)\) and Juliette has \((1,1)\). For both people, the ratio of radishes to carrots equals 1:

\[\begin{aligned} MRS_t&=\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\times\frac99 =\boxed{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}},\\ MRS_j&=\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}\times\frac11 =\boxed{\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}}. \end{aligned}\]

Step 3. Compare. These two values could be equal only if:

\[\begin{aligned} \frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}=\frac{1-\beta}{\beta} &\Longleftrightarrow\beta(1-\alpha)=\alpha(1-\beta)\\ &\Longleftrightarrow\beta-\alpha\beta=\alpha-\alpha\beta\\ &\Longleftrightarrow\beta=\alpha. \end{aligned}\]

But the question says \(\alpha\neq\beta\). Therefore:

\[\boxed{MRS_t\neq MRS_j}.\]

Why can both gain? Choose an exchange rate between their two MRS. The person who values carrots more is willing to give more radishes for them than the other person needs to receive. A small trade at a rate between the two therefore benefits both.

Answer to Question 6

Yes. The initial allocation is not Pareto efficient, so mutually beneficial trade is possible. At a competitive equilibrium, each person can still afford their initial bundle. Optimal choice therefore cannot leave either person worse off; because their initial MRS differ, at least one must strictly gain.

Optional proof — Test (9,9) on the contract curve

The contract curve contains the Pareto-efficient allocations. This method checks whether the starting point \((R_t,C_t)=(9,9)\) is on that curve.

Step 1. Write the contract curve. Since total resources are fixed, Juliette receives what Thomas does not consume:

\[R_j=10-R_t,\qquad C_j=10-C_t.\]

Put these quantities into the condition \(MRS_t=MRS_j\):

\[\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\frac{R_t}{C_t} =\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}\frac{10-R_t}{10-C_t}.\]

Cross-multiply, then collect all terms containing \(R_t\):

\[\begin{gathered} \beta(1-\alpha)R_t(10-C_t) =\alpha(1-\beta)(10-R_t)C_t,\\ R_t\big[10\beta(1-\alpha)+(\alpha-\beta)C_t\big] =10\alpha(1-\beta)C_t. \end{gathered}\]

The bracket equals \(\beta(1-\alpha)(10-C_t)+\alpha(1-\beta)C_t\), which is positive inside the box. Divide by it to get the interior contract curve:

\[\boxed{R_t= \frac{10\alpha(1-\beta)C_t} {10\beta(1-\alpha)+(\alpha-\beta)C_t}}.\]

Step 2. Test the starting point. If \((9,9)\) belongs to this curve, replacing \(R_t\) and \(C_t\) by 9 must satisfy the equation:

\[9= \frac{90\alpha(1-\beta)} {10\beta(1-\alpha)+9(\alpha-\beta)}.\]

Multiply both sides by the denominator, then divide by 9:

\[10\beta(1-\alpha)+9(\alpha-\beta) =10\alpha(1-\beta).\]

Expand the brackets:

\[10\beta-10\alpha\beta+9\alpha-9\beta =10\alpha-10\alpha\beta.\]

The terms \(-10\alpha\beta\) cancel on both sides. On the left, \(10\beta-9\beta=\beta\). We get:

\[\begin{aligned} \beta+9\alpha&=10\alpha,\\ \beta&=\alpha. \end{aligned}\]
\[\boxed{\alpha=\beta}.\]

Step 3. Interpret. Membership would require \(\alpha=\beta\), contrary to the question. Thus \((9,9)\) is outside the contract curve, confirming the main answer.

Exercise 2

Exercise 2. General Equilibrium

Consider a two-person, two-good exchange economy. Alice's and Benjamin's preferences over their consumption bundles \((x_1,x_2)\) are represented by:

\[\begin{gathered} u^A(x_1^A,x_2^A)=x_1^A+\ln(x_2^A),\\ u^B(x_1^B,x_2^B)=x_1^B+2\ln(x_2^B). \end{gathered}\]

There are initially 5 units of good 1 and 3 units of good 2. The initial distribution of these goods between Alice and Benjamin is unknown.

Questions

  1. Determine the set of interior Pareto-optimal allocations.
  2. Show that any interior Pareto-optimal allocation \(x>0\) is a general equilibrium associated with a price vector \(p\) and initial endowments \(\omega\).

    Hint: normalize \(p_1=1\).

  3. Determine the Pareto-optimal allocation \(x\) for which the distribution of endowments is egalitarian, \(\omega^A=\omega^B\), assuming that the allocation is supported as a general equilibrium with normalized prices.
  4. Now suppose Alice has 4 units of good 1 and 2 units of good 2, while Benjamin has 1 unit of each good. What income transfer from Alice to Benjamin leads to the egalitarian equilibrium?

Interior Pareto-optimal allocations

Goal: Find all interior allocations where no further mutually beneficial trade is possible.

Start: Calculate marginal utilities, equate the MRS, and use the total quantities available.

1. Calculate and equate the MRS

An interior allocation means that both people consume positive amounts of both goods. Since both utilities increase with consumption, an efficient allocation uses all 5 units of good 1 and all 3 units of good 2. First calculate the marginal utilities:

\[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial u^A}{\partial x_1^A}&=1,& \frac{\partial u^A}{\partial x_2^A}&=\frac1{x_2^A},\\ \frac{\partial u^B}{\partial x_1^B}&=1,& \frac{\partial u^B}{\partial x_2^B}&=\frac2{x_2^B}. \end{aligned}\]

Here we use \(MRS=MU_1/MU_2\): units of good 2 given up for a little more good 1. With good 1 horizontal and good 2 vertical, the indifference-curve slope is \(-MRS\). This reverses the orientation used in Exercise 1, where \(MU_C/MU_R\) measured radishes per carrot. Equality of the two consumers’ MRS remains the efficiency condition. We obtain:

\[MRS^A=\frac{1}{1/x_2^A}=x_2^A,\qquad MRS^B=\frac{1}{2/x_2^B}=\frac{x_2^B}{2}.\]

At an interior optimum, \(MRS^A=MRS^B\). Combine this with feasibility:

\[\begin{gathered} x_2^A=\frac{x_2^B}{2},\qquad x_2^A+x_2^B=3,\\ 3x_2^A=3\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\boxed{x_2^A=1,\quad x_2^B=2}. \end{gathered}\]

2. Share good 1 while keeping both bundles interior

\[\begin{gathered} x^A=(x_1^A,1),\\ x^B=(5-x_1^A,2),\qquad0<x_1^A<5. \end{gathered}\]

The MRS depend only on good 2, so their equality fixes its allocation: 1 for Alice and 2 for Benjamin. Changing the sharing of good 1 does not change either MRS. Any \(0<x_1^A<5\) therefore gives an interior efficient allocation, with Benjamin receiving the remaining \(5-x_1^A\). The utilities are concave, so the tangency condition and feasibility are sufficient here.

Read the diagram — the interior contract curve

  • Alice’s indifference curves, read from the bottom-left.
  • Benjamin’s indifference curves, read from the top-right.
  • Dashed line: the interior contract curve.
  • Black points: three tangencies illustrating the same condition.
  1. Read the box from the two origins. Alice receives more when we move right or up from \(O_A\). Benjamin receives more when we move left or down from \(O_B\). The width is 5 and the height is 3: these are the economy’s total resources.
  2. Find the tangencies, as in the lecture. At each black point, one blue curve and one red curve touch with the same slope. Their MRS are equal, so there is no small exchange that makes both consumers better off. The three points illustrate a whole set; they are not three computed equilibria.
  3. Connect the tangencies. Every interior efficient allocation has \(x_2^A=1\) and \(x_2^B=3-1=2\). Read this as 1 unit up from Alice’s origin, or 2 units down from Benjamin’s origin. All tangencies therefore lie at the same height. This is why the contract curve is horizontal in this exercise, although the general illustration in the lecture is rising. Its shape depends on the utility functions.
  4. Move along the dashed line. Moving right gives Alice more good 1 and leaves Benjamin less. Good 2 stays fixed for both. Each point remains efficient, but the distribution changes. The empty endpoints are excluded because one consumer would have zero good 1.
  5. Do not select an equilibrium yet. Question 1 gives the whole interior efficient set. We need prices and initial endowments to select a competitive equilibrium. Question 3 will give those endowments.

Support every interior optimum as an equilibrium

Goal: Find prices and endowments that support any interior efficient allocation as an equilibrium.

Start: Take any allocation from Question 1. We may choose its initial endowments; they are not fixed in this question.

1. Alice’s problem at \(p_1=1\)

We want to find prices and starting goods that make any allocation from Question 1 an equilibrium. Set the price of good 1 to 1. Alice’s initial goods are worth \(\omega_1^A+p_2\omega_2^A\). Her choice problem is:

\[\begin{gathered} \max_{x_1^A\geq0,\;x_2^A>0}\quad x_1^A+\ln x_2^A\\ \text{s.t.}\quad x_1^A+p_2x_2^A\leq\omega_1^A+p_2\omega_2^A. \end{gathered}\]

Good 2 must be positive because its logarithm must be defined. Utility increases with both goods, so Alice spends her full budget. For the interior choice asked for here, good 1 is positive too. Add the unused budget to utility, multiplied by a Lagrange multiplier:

\[\begin{aligned} \mathcal L^A={}&x_1^A+\ln x_2^A\\ &+\lambda_A(\omega_1^A+p_2\omega_2^A-x_1^A-p_2x_2^A). \end{aligned}\]
\[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\mathcal L^A}{\partial x_1^A}&=1-\lambda_A=0,\\ \frac{\partial\mathcal L^A}{\partial x_2^A}&=\frac1{x_2^A}-\lambda_Ap_2=0,\\ \frac{\partial\mathcal L^A}{\partial\lambda_A} &=\omega_1^A+p_2\omega_2^A-x_1^A-p_2x_2^A=0. \end{aligned}\]

The first equation gives \(\lambda_A=1\). The second then gives \(1/x_2^A=p_2\), so \(x_2^A=1/p_2\). Spending on good 2 is therefore \(p_2x_2^A=1\). Use the budget to find what remains for good 1:

\[\boxed{x_2^A=\frac1{p_2}},\qquad\boxed{x_1^A=\omega_1^A+p_2\omega_2^A-1}.\]

2. Benjamin’s problem and demand

Benjamin has his own budget, and the coefficient on the logarithm is 2:

\[\begin{gathered} \max_{x_1^B\geq0,\;x_2^B>0}\quad x_1^B+2\ln x_2^B\\ \text{s.t.}\quad x_1^B+p_2x_2^B\leq\omega_1^B+p_2\omega_2^B. \end{gathered}\]

Reuse Alice’s reasoning. Benjamin also has \(MU_1=1\), so the good-1 condition gives \(\lambda_B=1\). Only the good-2 marginal utility changes:

\[\frac{2}{x_2^B}=\lambda_Bp_2=p_2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p_2x_2^B=2.\]

He spends 2 on good 2 and the rest of his budget on good 1:

\[\boxed{x_2^B=\frac2{p_2}},\qquad\boxed{x_1^B=\omega_1^B+p_2\omega_2^B-2}.\]

These interior demands require Alice’s wealth to exceed 1 and Benjamin’s to exceed 2. The endowments below satisfy these conditions. Concavity of utility makes the first-order conditions sufficient for their best choices.

3. Clear markets and choose starting endowments

There are 3 units of good 2. Add the two interior demands and set their sum equal to 3:

\[\frac1{p_2}+\frac2{p_2}=3\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\boxed{p^*=(1,1)}.\]

For any target from Question 1, choose endowments equal to that target:

\[\begin{gathered} \omega^A=(x_1^A,1),\\ \omega^B=(5-x_1^A,2),\qquad0<x_1^A<5. \end{gathered}\]

Check the demands at these prices and endowments:

\[\begin{aligned} \text{Alice’s good 1 demand}&=x_1^A+1-1=x_1^A,\\ \text{Alice’s good 2 demand}&=1,\\ \text{Benjamin’s good 1 demand}&=(5-x_1^A)+2-2=5-x_1^A,\\ \text{Benjamin’s good 2 demand}&=2. \end{aligned}\]

Each person chooses exactly their initial bundle. The demands add to \((5,3)\), and both good-1 demands are positive because \(0<x_1^A<5\). Individual optimality and market clearing therefore hold: this is a valid equilibrium with no trade.

The prices support the target: given these budgets, both consumers choose it. Efficiency concerns possible gains from reallocating goods. An equilibrium also requires individual choices and market clearing at given prices and endowments. This construction meets all those conditions without trade.

Equilibrium with equal initial endowments

Goal: Find the equilibrium consumption bundles when initial endowments are equal.

Start: Keep \(p_1=1\) and use the interior demand functions from Question 2.

Equal endowments means splitting each good equally between the two people. It does not yet tell us what they choose to consume:

\[\omega^A=\omega^B=\left(\frac52,\frac32\right).\]

The good-2 demands from Question 2 add to \(3/p_2\). Equating this to the available 3 units gives \(p_2=1\). Each person’s wealth is then \(5/2+3/2=4\). Alice spends 1 on good 2, Benjamin spends 2, and the rest buys good 1:

\[\begin{aligned} x_2^{A*}&=1,&\quad x_1^{A*}&=4-1=3,\\ x_2^{B*}&=2,&\quad x_1^{B*}&=4-2=2. \end{aligned}\]
\[\boxed{x^{A*}=(3,1)},\qquad\boxed{x^{B*}=(2,2)}.\]

Both bundles are interior, and \((3,1)+(2,2)=(5,3)\). Equal endowments give equal wealth, not identical consumption: Benjamin values good 2 more strongly.

We can now name this particular equilibrium \(E=(3,1)\) in Alice’s coordinates. Read from Benjamin’s opposite origin, the same point gives \((5-3,3-1)=(2,2)\). The diagrams in Question 4 use this point as their target.

The income transfer from Alice to Benjamin

Goal: Find the income transfer that leads to the equal-wealth equilibrium from Question 3.

Start: Value the original endowments at the target prices \(p^*=(1,1)\), then compare each wealth with 4.

1. Compare initial wealth with the target wealth

At the target prices \(p^*=(1,1)\), initial endowments \((4,2)\) and \((1,1)\) are worth:

\[\begin{aligned} \text{Alice: }&4+2=6,\\ \text{Benjamin: }&1+1=2. \end{aligned}\]

The total wealth is \(6+2=8\). The target from Question 3 gives each person half, so each needs wealth 4. A positive transfer means income received, and a negative one means income paid:

\[T_A=4-6=-2,\qquad T_B=4-2=2.\]

Alice transfers 2 units of income to Benjamin. The transfer balances: \(-2+2=0\). Alice’s budget becomes \(6-2=4\), and Benjamin’s becomes \(2+2=4\). Their constraints are now \(x_1^A+x_2^A\leq4\) and \(x_1^B+x_2^B\leq4\). Applying their demands gives the bundles from Question 3:

\[x^{A*}=(3,1),\qquad x^{B*}=(2,2).\]

This redistribution is not a Pareto improvement over the original endowment. Alice moves from \((4,2)\) to \((3,1)\), so she has less of both goods and loses utility. Benjamin moves from \((1,1)\) to \((2,2)\), so he gains. An efficient final allocation need not benefit everyone relative to the initial allocation.

Exercise 3. General Equilibrium with Production

Consider an economy with two consumers \(i\in\{A,B\}\), one firm, and two goods \(n\in\{1,2\}\). Utilities and endowments are:

Consumer A

\[\begin{gathered} u^A(x_1^A,x_2^A)=\ln(x_1^A)+\ln(x_2^A),\\ \omega^A=\left(\frac12,\frac12\right). \end{gathered}\]

Consumer B

\[\begin{gathered} u^B(x_1^B,x_2^B)=(x_1^B)^{1/4}(x_2^B)^{3/4},\\ \omega^B=\left(\frac12,\frac12\right). \end{gathered}\]

The firm

The firm produces good 2 using good 1 as an input:

\[y_2=\sqrt{y_1}.\]

Consumer B owns the firm. Here, \(y_1\geq0\) denotes the amount of good 1 used as an input, and \(y_2\) the output of good 2.

Questions

  1. Determine the demand for good 1 of each consumer and of the firm.
  2. From now on, normalize \(p_2=1\). Determine the equilibrium price \(p_1\).
  3. For this question, replace the production function by \(y_2=y_1\), with constant returns to scale. Determine the equilibrium price \(p_1\) and allocation \((x,y)\).
  4. For this question, consider the exchange economy without production consisting of consumers A and B. Determine the equilibrium price and allocation.

Consumers’ demands and the firm’s input demand

Goal: Find the consumers’ demands for good 1 and the firm’s input demand.

Start: Consumers maximize utility within their budgets; the firm maximizes revenue minus input cost. Only B receives its profit.

1. Consumer A

A owns half a unit of each good and no share of the firm. The initial goods are worth \(p_1/2+p_2/2\), which is A’s entire budget. A chooses consumption at the given positive prices:

\[\begin{gathered} \max_{x_1^A,x_2^A>0}\quad\ln x_1^A+\ln x_2^A\\ \text{s.t.}\quad p_1x_1^A+p_2x_2^A\leq\frac12(p_1+p_2). \end{gathered}\]

Both quantities must be positive for the logarithms. Utility increases with each good, so the budget is fully spent. Write utility plus the multiplier times income minus spending:

\[\begin{aligned} \mathcal L^A={}&\ln x_1^A+\ln x_2^A\\ &+\lambda_A\left[\frac12(p_1+p_2)-p_1x_1^A-p_2x_2^A\right]. \end{aligned}\]
\[\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\mathcal L^A}{\partial x_1^A}&=\frac1{x_1^A}-\lambda_Ap_1=0,\\ \frac{\partial\mathcal L^A}{\partial x_2^A}&=\frac1{x_2^A}-\lambda_Ap_2=0,\\ \frac{\partial\mathcal L^A}{\partial\lambda_A}&=\frac12(p_1+p_2)-p_1x_1^A-p_2x_2^A=0. \end{aligned}\]

Divide the first quantity condition by the second: \(x_2^A/x_1^A=p_1/p_2\). Multiply by \(p_2x_1^A\) to get equal spending on both goods. Put this into the budget:

\[p_1x_1^A=p_2x_2^A,\qquad2p_1x_1^A=\frac12(p_1+p_2).\]
\[\begin{gathered} \boxed{x_1^A(p_1,p_2)=\frac14\left(1+\frac{p_2}{p_1}\right)},\\ x_2^A(p_1,p_2)=\frac14\left(1+\frac{p_1}{p_2}\right). \end{gathered}\]

2. Consumer B

B has the same initial goods as A and owns the firm. Add the firm’s profit \(\Pi(p_1,p_2)\) to B’s budget; we calculate it in Step 3 below. B takes this income and the prices as given when choosing consumption:

\[\begin{gathered} \max_{x_1^B,x_2^B\geq0}\quad (x_1^B)^{1/4}(x_2^B)^{3/4}\\ \text{s.t.}\quad p_1x_1^B+p_2x_2^B \leq\frac12(p_1+p_2)+\Pi(p_1,p_2). \end{gathered}\]

The best bundle has both quantities positive and spends the full budget. Use the Cobb–Douglas utility directly in the Lagrangian:

\[\begin{aligned} \mathcal L^B={}&(x_1^B)^{1/4}(x_2^B)^{3/4}\\ &+\lambda_B\left[\frac12(p_1+p_2)+\Pi(p_1,p_2)\right.\\ &\hspace{3.5em}\left.-p_1x_1^B-p_2x_2^B\right]. \end{aligned}\]
\[\begin{gathered} \frac{\partial\mathcal L^B}{\partial x_1^B} =\frac14(x_1^B)^{-3/4}(x_2^B)^{3/4}-\lambda_Bp_1=0,\\ \frac{\partial\mathcal L^B}{\partial x_2^B} =\frac34(x_1^B)^{1/4}(x_2^B)^{-1/4}-\lambda_Bp_2=0,\\ \frac{\partial\mathcal L^B}{\partial\lambda_B} =\frac12(p_1+p_2)+\Pi(p_1,p_2)-p_1x_1^B-p_2x_2^B=0. \end{gathered}\]

Divide the first condition by the second:

\[\frac{p_1}{p_2}=\frac{x_2^B}{3x_1^B}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad p_2x_2^B=3p_1x_1^B.\]

The multiplier cancels in the ratio. B spends three times as much on good 2 as on good 1. Replace \(p_2x_2^B\) by \(3p_1x_1^B\) in the budget:

\[4p_1x_1^B=\frac12(p_1+p_2)+\Pi(p_1,p_2).\]
\[\begin{aligned} x_1^B(p_1,p_2)&=\frac18\left(1+\frac{p_2}{p_1}\right)+\frac{\Pi(p_1,p_2)}{4p_1},\\ x_2^B(p_1,p_2)&=\frac38\left(1+\frac{p_1}{p_2}\right)+\frac{3\Pi(p_1,p_2)}{4p_2}. \end{aligned}\]

3. The firm’s input demand and output supply

The firm chooses the input \(y_1\). Its technology then gives output \(y_2=\sqrt{y_1}\). Revenue is the output price times output, and cost is the input price times input:

\[\max_{y_1\geq0}\;p_2\sqrt{y_1}-p_1y_1.\]

Differentiate profit with respect to the input. The derivative of \(\sqrt{y_1}\) is \(1/(2\sqrt{y_1})\). At an interior maximum, the extra revenue from the input equals its price:

\[\frac{p_2}{2\sqrt{y_1}}-p_1=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\boxed{y_1(p_1,p_2)=\frac{p_2^2}{4p_1^2}}.\]

The second derivative is negative, confirming the unique maximum:

\[\frac{d^2}{dy_1^2}\left(p_2\sqrt{y_1}-p_1y_1\right)=-\frac{p_2}{4y_1^{3/2}}<0\quad(y_1>0).\]
\[y_2(p_1,p_2)=\sqrt{y_1(p_1,p_2)}=\frac{p_2}{2p_1}.\]

The second derivative concerns profit before choosing the input. The symbol Π below means the maximum profit after making that choice. Revenue minus cost at the optimal input gives:

\[\begin{aligned} \Pi(p_1,p_2)&=p_2\frac{p_2}{2p_1}-p_1\frac{p_2^2}{4p_1^2}\\ &=\boxed{\frac{p_2^2}{4p_1}}. \end{aligned}\]

4. Collect all demands for good 1

Substitute profit into B's demand:

\[\begin{aligned} x_1^A(p_1,p_2)&=\frac14\left(1+\frac{p_2}{p_1}\right),\\ x_1^B(p_1,p_2)&=\frac18\left(1+\frac{p_2}{p_1}\right)+\frac{p_2^2}{16p_1^2},\\ y_1(p_1,p_2)&=\frac{p_2^2}{4p_1^2}. \end{aligned}\]

Here \(y_1\) is a positive physical input quantity. Add it to consumption when measuring total use of good 1. In a net production vector, the same input is negative: \((-y_1,y_2)\). These are two ways to record the same production activity.

The equilibrium price when \(p_2=1\)

Goal: Find the positive equilibrium price of good 1.

Start: Set \(p_2=1\) in Question 1’s demands. Consumption plus the firm’s input must equal the initial stock of good 1.

1. Clear the market for good 1

The total endowment of good 1 is \(1/2+1/2=1\). Consumers and the firm share it:

\[x_1^A+x_1^B+y_1=1.\]

Set \(p_2=1\) in the demands from Question 1:

\[\begin{aligned} &\frac14\left(1+\frac1{p_1}\right) +\frac18\left(1+\frac1{p_1}\right)\\ &\qquad+\frac1{16p_1^2}+\frac1{4p_1^2}=1. \end{aligned}\]

Add the fractions with the same type of term: \(1/4+1/8=3/8\), and \(1/16+1/4=5/16\). This gives:

\[\frac38+\frac3{8p_1}+\frac5{16p_1^2}=1.\]

Multiplying by \(16p_1^2\) gives:

\[6p_1^2+6p_1+5=16p_1^2.\]
\[10p_1^2-6p_1-5=0.\]

2. Keep the positive root

\[p_1=\frac{6\pm\sqrt{36+200}}{20}.\]
\[\boxed{p_1^*=\frac{3+\sqrt{59}}{10}\approx1.0681},\qquad p_2^*=1.\]

The two roots come from the quadratic formula with coefficients \(10,-6,-5\). Since \(\sqrt{59}>3\), the root \((3-\sqrt{59})/10\) is negative and cannot be a positive equilibrium price. Both consumer budgets are fully spent and the firm’s profit is paid to B. Walras’ law then implies that clearing good 1 also clears good 2.

Optional calculation — Check the good-2 market

For a direct check, substitute \(p_1^*\) into:

\[\underbrace{\frac58(1+p_1^*)+\frac3{16p_1^*}}_{x_2^{A*}+x_2^{B*}} =\underbrace{1+\frac1{2p_1^*}}_{1+y_2^*}.\]

To see why the equality holds, subtract the right side from the left:

\[\frac{10(p_1^*)^2-6p_1^*-5}{16p_1^*}=0.\]

The numerator is zero because it is exactly the price equation solved above. Good 2 is consumed from both the initial stock and the firm’s new output.

Constant returns to scale

Goal: Find equilibrium prices, consumption and production with the new technology.

Start: Use \(y_2=y_1\) and \(p_2=1\). Consumer preferences are unchanged, but the firm’s problem must be solved again.

1. Use the firm’s problem to find the price

Now \(y_2=y_1\), with \(p_2=1\), so:

\[\max_{y_1\geq0}\quad y_1-p_1y_1=(1-p_1)y_1.\]

The technology has changed. Do not reuse the square-root firm’s input or profit formula from Question 1. Now one unit of input produces one unit of output, so check whether the coefficient of production is positive, negative, or zero:

  • If \(p_1<1\), profit increases without bound as production rises: there is no finite optimum.
  • If \(p_1>1\), the firm chooses \(y_1=0\). Consumer demand for good 1 is then \(\frac38(1+1/p_1)<1\), so that market cannot clear.
  • If \(p_1=1\), every production scale yields zero profit. Market clearing determines the scale.
\[\boxed{p_1^*=p_2^*=1},\qquad\boxed{\Pi^*=0}.\]

2. Compute consumption

Each consumer’s endowment is worth 1, and B receives zero profit. Both budgets are therefore \(x_1^i+x_2^i\leq1\), for \(i=A,B\).

The consumer conditions from Question 1 still apply: A spends half on each good; B spends one quarter on good 1 and three quarters on good 2. Both prices equal 1, so spending equals the quantity bought:

\[\boxed{x^{A*}=\left(\frac12,\frac12\right)},\qquad \boxed{x^{B*}=\left(\frac14,\frac34\right)}.\]

Total consumption is \((3/4,5/4)\).

3. Choose the production scale that clears both markets

\[\begin{aligned} \frac34+y_1^*&=1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad y_1^*=\frac14,\\ y_2^*&=y_1^*=\frac14,\\ x_2^{A*}+x_2^{B*}&=\frac54=1+y_2^*. \end{aligned}\]

The firm uses 1/4 unit of good 1 and produces 1/4 unit of good 2. In the lecture's net-output convention, its production vector is \((-1/4,1/4)\).

Zero profit does not imply zero production. At equal prices, every unit of output earns exactly what its input costs. Profit is zero at every scale, including positive scales; market clearing selects \(y_1=y_2=1/4\).

The pure exchange economy

Goal: Find the equilibrium price and consumption allocation without a firm.

Start: Keep the same preferences and endowments, but remove input demand and profit income.

1. Remove production and profit income

Each consumer owns \((1/2,1/2)\), so each budget is \(p_1x_1^i+p_2x_2^i\leq(p_1+p_2)/2\). Reuse the consumer conditions from Question 1 with \(\Pi=0\): A spends half on each good; B spends one quarter on good 1 and three quarters on good 2.

\[\begin{aligned} x_1^A&=\frac14\left(1+\frac{p_2}{p_1}\right),& x_2^A&=\frac14\left(1+\frac{p_1}{p_2}\right),\\ x_1^B&=\frac18\left(1+\frac{p_2}{p_1}\right),& x_2^B&=\frac38\left(1+\frac{p_1}{p_2}\right). \end{aligned}\]

2. Clear good 1

Without production, only the two consumers use good 1. Their demands must add to its initial stock of 1:

\[\begin{aligned} x_1^A+x_1^B&=1,\\ \frac38\left(1+\frac{p_2}{p_1}\right)&=1,\\ \frac{p_2}{p_1}&=\frac53. \end{aligned}\]

With \(p_2=1\):

\[\boxed{p_1^*=\frac35},\qquad\boxed{p^*=\left(\frac35,1\right)}.\]

3. Compute the equilibrium allocation

\[\begin{aligned} x_1^{A*}&=\frac14\left(1+\frac53\right)=\frac23,\\ x_2^{A*}&=\frac14\left(1+\frac35\right)=\frac25,\\ x_1^{B*}&=\frac18\left(1+\frac53\right)=\frac13,\\ x_2^{B*}&=\frac38\left(1+\frac35\right)=\frac35. \end{aligned}\]
\[\boxed{x^{A*}=\left(\frac23,\frac25\right)},\qquad \boxed{x^{B*}=\left(\frac13,\frac35\right)}.\]

The good-1 demands add to \(2/3+1/3=1\), and the good-2 demands add to \(2/5+3/5=1\). Both people make their best affordable choice and both markets clear. There is no production vector to choose in this exchange economy.

Exercise 4. First Welfare Theorem with Production

First Welfare Theorem. Let utility functions \(u\) be continuous and strictly increasing in \(x\). If an allocation \((x,y)\) and a price vector \(p\) form a Walrasian equilibrium, then \((x,y)\) is Pareto efficient.

Question

  1. Taking inspiration from the proof in the slides for the exchange economy, prove the First Welfare Theorem in the productive economy.

Proof of the First Welfare Theorem with production

Goal: Prove that a Walrasian equilibrium with production is Pareto efficient.

Start: Use consumer optimization, firm profit maximization, and feasibility at the same equilibrium prices.

The proof is by contradiction: a feasible Pareto improvement would require consumption worth more than the economy can provide.

Here, \(y_k\) is a net production vector: an input has a negative component and an output has a positive component. Thus \(p^*\cdot y_k\) already means revenue minus input cost. In Exercise 3, the input amount \(y_1\) was written as a positive quantity instead.

1. Suppose a feasible Pareto improvement exists

Let \((x^*,y^*,p^*)\) be a Walrasian equilibrium. A star marks the equilibrium choice. Its price vector satisfies \(p^*\geq0\) and \(p^*\neq0\). A tilde marks the alternative we want to test. Assume this alternative is feasible: each production plan is possible, and consumption does not exceed the available goods. Also assume:

\[\begin{gathered} u_i(\widetilde x_i)\geq u_i(x_i^*)\quad\text{for every }i,\\ u_{i_0}(\widetilde x_{i_0})>u_{i_0}(x_{i_0}^*)\quad\text{for at least one }i_0. \end{gathered}\]

2. Use consumer optimization

At the equilibrium prices, consumer \(i\) solves the following problem. The ownership share \(\theta_{ki}\geq0\) is the fraction of firm \(k\)’s profit received by consumer \(i\); each firm is fully owned, so \(\sum_i\theta_{ki}=1\):

\[\begin{gathered} \max_{x_i\geq0}\quad u_i(x_i)\\ \text{s.t.}\quad p^*\cdot x_i\leq p^*\cdot\omega_i+\sum_k\theta_{ki}\Pi_k^*,\\ \Pi_k^*=p^*\cdot y_k^*. \end{gathered}\]

A strictly preferred bundle must cost more than the budget, otherwise the consumer would choose it. A weakly preferred bundle cannot cost less: with money left over, the consumer could afford a small increase in all goods. Strictly increasing utility would make this bundle strictly better, contradicting optimality. Therefore:

\[\begin{gathered} p^*\cdot\widetilde x_i\geq p^*\cdot\omega_i+\sum_k\theta_{ki}\Pi_k^* \quad\text{for every }i,\\ p^*\cdot\widetilde x_{i_0}>p^*\cdot\omega_{i_0}+\sum_k\theta_{ki_0}\Pi_k^*. \end{gathered}\]

3. Sum across consumers

Add the inequalities for all consumers. No one has a lower value and at least one has a strictly higher value, so the total inequality is strict. For every firm, the ownership shares add to 1: \(\sum_i\theta_{ki}=1\). This means we count the firm’s profit exactly once when adding all incomes:

\[\begin{aligned} \sum_i p^*\cdot\widetilde x_i &>\sum_i p^*\cdot\omega_i+\sum_i\sum_k\theta_{ki}\Pi_k^*\\ &=\sum_i p^*\cdot\omega_i+\sum_k\Pi_k^*. \end{aligned}\]

4. Use firm profit maximization

At the same prices, firm \(k\) solves:

\[\max_{y_k\in Y_k}\quad p^*\cdot y_k.\]

The set \(Y_k\) contains the firm’s possible production plans. The equilibrium plan \(y_k^*\) earns the highest profit among them. The alternative plan \(\widetilde y_k\) belongs to this set, so it cannot have a greater value at these prices:

\[\Pi_k^*=p^*\cdot y_k^*\geq p^*\cdot\widetilde y_k.\]

Combine this inequality with the strict inequality from Step 3:

\[\begin{aligned} p^*\cdot\sum_i\widetilde x_i &>p^*\cdot\sum_i\omega_i+\sum_k\Pi_k^*\\ &\geq p^*\cdot\left(\sum_i\omega_i+\sum_k\widetilde y_k\right). \end{aligned}\]

5. Contradict feasibility

The proposed change is supposed to be possible. For each good, total consumption must be no greater than the initial stock plus net production:

\[\sum_i\widetilde x_i\leq\sum_i\omega_i+\sum_k\widetilde y_k.\]

Multiply the quantity inequality for each good by its equilibrium price, then add across goods. All prices are nonnegative, so this keeps the inequality in the same direction:

\[p^*\cdot\sum_i\widetilde x_i \leq p^*\cdot\left(\sum_i\omega_i+\sum_k\widetilde y_k\right).\]

Step 4 says that consumption is worth strictly more than the available goods. Step 5 says that it is worth no more. Both statements cannot hold. Our assumed Pareto improvement therefore cannot exist, so the equilibrium allocation and production plan are Pareto efficient.

The extra step relative to pure exchange is Step 4: use profit maximization to compare the equilibrium and alternative production plans.
