Exercise 1

Exercise 1. General Equilibrium

Thomas and Juliette consume radishes \(R\) and carrots \(C\). Their utility functions are:

\[\begin{gathered} u_t(R,C)=R^\alpha C^{1-\alpha},\qquad0<\alpha<1,\\ u_j(R,C)=R^\beta C^{1-\beta},\qquad0<\beta<1. \end{gathered}\]

Initially, Thomas has 1 radish and 9 carrots, while Juliette has 9 radishes and 1 carrot:

\[\begin{gathered} (\overline R_t,\overline C_t)=(1,9),\\ (\overline R_j,\overline C_j)=(9,1). \end{gathered}\]

Questions

  1. What are the initial utility levels of Thomas and Juliette?
  2. Draw an Edgeworth box representing Thomas' and Juliette's indifference curves and the endowment allocation. For this question only, assume \(\alpha=\beta=1/2\). Shade the allocations that would be Pareto improvements over the endowment allocation.
  3. Thomas and Juliette sell or buy vegetables at prices \(p_R\) and \(p_C\). What relative price \(p^*=p_C/p_R\) clears both markets?
  4. Assume \(\alpha=\beta\) and normalize prices so that \(p_R^*=1\) and \(p_C^*=p_C/p_R=p^*\). Show that allowing trade leads to a Pareto improvement compared with the initial situation.

    Hint: for \(0<\alpha=\beta<1\),

    \[\begin{gathered} 9-8\alpha>9^{1-\alpha},\\ 8\beta+1>9^\beta. \end{gathered}\]
  5. Still assuming \(\alpha=\beta\), describe the change in the normalized equilibrium price \(p_C^*\) and equilibrium demands when \(\alpha=\beta\) increases. What is the intuition for these changes?
  6. Now assume \(\alpha\neq\beta\) and change the initial endowments: Thomas has 9 radishes and 9 carrots, while Juliette has 1 radish and 1 carrot. Does allowing trade lead to a Pareto improvement?

Initial utility levels

Substitute each consumer's own endowment into their utility function:

\[\begin{aligned} u_t(1,9)&=1^\alpha9^{1-\alpha}=\boxed{9^{1-\alpha}},\\ u_j(9,1)&=9^\beta1^{1-\beta}=\boxed{9^\beta}. \end{aligned}\]

The Edgeworth box and gains from trade

With \(\alpha=\beta=1/2\), both consumers initially obtain utility \(3\). Total resources are 10 radishes and 10 carrots.

Thomas' origin is at the bottom-left; Juliette's is at the top-right. A point \((R_t,C_t)\) gives Juliette \((10-R_t,10-C_t)\).

The indifference curves through the endowment satisfy:

\[\begin{gathered} R_tC_t=9\quad\Longrightarrow\quad C_t=\frac9{R_t},\\ (10-R_t)(10-C_t)=9\\ \Longrightarrow\quad C_t=10-\frac9{10-R_t}. \end{gathered}\]

Read the diagram — Thomas and Juliette

  • Thomas: initial indifference curve, utility 3.
  • Juliette: initial indifference curve, utility 3.
  • Green lens: both are at least as well off.
  • E: one allocation that makes both strictly better off.
  1. Read the two origins. From \(O_t\), right means more radishes and up means more carrots for Thomas. From \(O_j\), read left and down for Juliette. The box contains 10 units of each vegetable.
  2. Read the initial point \(\omega\). Thomas has \((1,9)\); Juliette gets the remainder \((9,1)\). The dashed guides locate this point. Both initial utilities equal 3.
  3. Find the better side of each curve. Above the blue curve, Thomas is better off. Below the red curve, Juliette is better off. Their overlap is the green lens.
  4. Check the boundaries. On a blue-only boundary, Thomas stays at utility 3 and Juliette gains; on a red-only boundary, the reverse holds. At \(\omega\) and \(Z=(9,1)\), both utilities are still 3, so neither point is a Pareto improvement.

A concrete trade: \(\omega\rightarrow E\)

At \(E=(5,5)\), both receive 5 radishes and 5 carrots. Thomas buys 4 radishes and sells 4 carrots; Juliette makes the opposite trade.

\[u_t(E)=u_j(E)=\sqrt{5\times5}=5>3.\]

This is one strict improvement. The green lens contains many others; being in the lens does not, by itself, imply Pareto efficiency.

A Pareto improvement is a feasible allocation satisfying:

\[\begin{gathered} \sqrt{R_tC_t}\geq3,\\ \sqrt{(10-R_t)(10-C_t)}\geq3, \end{gathered}\]

with at least one strict inequality. Both consumers strictly gain inside the lens. Its two intersection points, \((1,9)\) and \((9,1)\), leave both utilities unchanged.

Demands and the equilibrium relative price

1. Thomas: utility maximization

Use the original Cobb–Douglas utility directly:

\[\begin{aligned} \mathcal L_t={}&R_t^\alpha C_t^{1-\alpha}\\ &+\lambda_t(p_R+9p_C-p_RR_t-p_CC_t). \end{aligned}\]

The first-order conditions are:

\[\begin{gathered} \alpha R_t^{\alpha-1}C_t^{1-\alpha}=\lambda_t p_R,\\ (1-\alpha)R_t^\alpha C_t^{-\alpha}=\lambda_t p_C. \end{gathered}\]

Divide the second condition by the first:

\[\frac{p_C}{p_R}=\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\frac{R_t}{C_t}.\]

Thus \(p_CC_t=\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}p_RR_t\). Substitution into the binding budget gives:

\[\frac{p_RR_t}{\alpha}=p_R+9p_C.\]
\[\begin{gathered} \boxed{R_t(p_R,p_C)=\alpha\left(1+9\frac{p_C}{p_R}\right)},\\ \boxed{C_t(p_R,p_C)=(1-\alpha)\left(\frac{p_R}{p_C}+9\right)}. \end{gathered}\]

2. Juliette: the same method with her own endowment

Her budget is \(p_RR_j+p_CC_j=9p_R+p_C\). The first-order conditions give:

\[p_CC_j=\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}p_RR_j.\]

Therefore:

\[\begin{gathered} \boxed{R_j(p_R,p_C)=\beta\left(9+\frac{p_C}{p_R}\right)},\\ \boxed{C_j(p_R,p_C)=(1-\beta)\left(9\frac{p_R}{p_C}+1\right)}. \end{gathered}\]

Positive prices and exponents strictly between 0 and 1 imply interior, unique optimal bundles.

3. Clear the radish market

\[\begin{aligned} R_t+R_j&=10,\\ \alpha+9\beta+(9\alpha+\beta)\frac{p_C}{p_R}&=10. \end{aligned}\]
\[\boxed{p^*=\frac{p_C}{p_R}=\frac{10-\alpha-9\beta}{9\alpha+\beta}}.\]

Both numerator and denominator are positive. The carrot market also clears by Walras' law. Directly:

\[\begin{aligned} C_t+C_j&=\frac{10-\alpha-9\beta}{p^*}+10-9\alpha-\beta\\ &=(9\alpha+\beta)+(10-9\alpha-\beta)=10. \end{aligned}\]

Equal preferences: both consumers gain

1. Normalize prices and use \(\alpha=\beta\)

\[p_R^*=1,\qquad p_C^*=\frac{10(1-\alpha)}{10\alpha}=\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}.\]

Substitute into the demands from Question 3:

\[\begin{aligned} R_t^*&=\alpha+9(1-\alpha)=9-8\alpha,\\ C_t^*&=\alpha+9(1-\alpha)=9-8\alpha,\\ R_j^*&=9\alpha+(1-\alpha)=1+8\alpha,\\ C_j^*&=9\alpha+(1-\alpha)=1+8\alpha. \end{aligned}\]

For each good, the two demands add to 10.

2. Compare equilibrium and initial utilities

Since each consumer consumes equal amounts of both goods, the exponents add to one:

\[\begin{aligned} u_t^*&=(9-8\alpha)^\alpha(9-8\alpha)^{1-\alpha}\\ &=9-8\alpha>9^{1-\alpha}=u_t(1,9),\\ u_j^*&=(1+8\alpha)^\alpha(1+8\alpha)^{1-\alpha}\\ &=1+8\alpha>9^\alpha=u_j(9,1). \end{aligned}\]

The strict inequalities are the supplied hints. Trade makes both consumers strictly better off.

A stronger preference for radishes

Increase the common preference parameter, keeping \(\alpha=\beta\). Using \(\alpha\) to write this common value:

\[p_C^*=\frac1\alpha-1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\boxed{\frac{d p_C^*}{d\alpha}=-\frac1{\alpha^2}<0}.\]

Question 4 also gives:

\[\begin{gathered} R_t^*=C_t^*=9-8\alpha,\qquad \frac{dR_t^*}{d\alpha}=\frac{dC_t^*}{d\alpha}=-8,\\ R_j^*=C_j^*=1+8\alpha,\qquad \frac{dR_j^*}{d\alpha}=\frac{dC_j^*}{d\alpha}=8. \end{gathered}\]
Intuition. Both consumers put more weight on radishes, so carrots become cheaper relative to radishes. Thomas owns mostly carrots and loses purchasing power; Juliette owns mostly radishes and gains purchasing power. Thomas consumes less of both goods, Juliette more; aggregate consumption stays at 10 units of each good.

Different preferences and proportional endowments

1. Derive the contract curve

At a smooth interior efficient allocation, the two consumers have the same marginal rate of substitution:

\[\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\frac{R_t}{C_t} =\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}\frac{10-R_t}{10-C_t}.\]

Cross-multiply and collect the terms in \(R_t\), keeping the full coefficients:

\[\begin{gathered} \beta(1-\alpha)R_t(10-C_t)\\ =\alpha(1-\beta)(10-R_t)C_t,\\[6pt] R_t\big[10\beta(1-\alpha)+(\alpha-\beta)C_t\big]\\ =10\alpha(1-\beta)C_t. \end{gathered}\]
\[\boxed{R_t=\frac{10\alpha(1-\beta)C_t}{10\beta(1-\alpha)+(\alpha-\beta)C_t}}.\]

At the initial bundles \((9,9)\) and \((1,1)\), both ratios \(R/C\) equal one, but:

\[\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\neq\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}.\]

The initial allocation is therefore not Pareto efficient: mutually beneficial trades exist.

2. Show that competitive trade makes both strictly better off

Normalize \(p_R=1\). With the new endowments, radish-market clearing is:

\[9\alpha(1+p_C)+\beta(1+p_C)=10.\]
\[\boxed{p_C^*=\frac{10-9\alpha-\beta}{9\alpha+\beta}}.\]

When \(\alpha\neq\beta\), this price lies strictly between \((1-\alpha)/\alpha\) and \((1-\beta)/\beta\). Thus neither initial bundle satisfies its consumer's optimality condition at the equilibrium prices.

Both initial bundles remain affordable. Since each consumer has a unique optimum and chooses a different bundle, both achieve strictly higher utility. Trade is a strict Pareto improvement.

Exercise 2

Exercise 2. General Equilibrium

Consider a two-person, two-good exchange economy. Alice's and Benjamin's preferences over their consumption bundles \((x_1,x_2)\) are represented by:

\[\begin{gathered} u^A(x_1^A,x_2^A)=x_1^A+\ln(x_2^A),\\ u^B(x_1^B,x_2^B)=x_1^B+2\ln(x_2^B). \end{gathered}\]

There are initially 5 units of good 1 and 3 units of good 2. The initial distribution of these goods between Alice and Benjamin is unknown.

Questions

  1. Determine the set of interior Pareto-optimal allocations.
  2. Show that any interior Pareto-optimal allocation \(x>0\) is a general equilibrium associated with a price vector \(p\) and initial endowments \(\omega\).

    Hint: normalize \(p_1=1\).

  3. Determine the Pareto-optimal allocation \(x\) for which the distribution of endowments is egalitarian, \(\omega^A=\omega^B\), assuming that the allocation is supported as a general equilibrium with normalized prices.
  4. Now suppose Alice has 4 units of good 1 and 2 units of good 2, while Benjamin has 1 unit of each good. What income transfer from Alice to Benjamin leads to the egalitarian equilibrium?

Interior Pareto-optimal allocations

1. Equate the slopes of the indifference curves

Holding utility constant gives:

\[\begin{gathered} du^A=dx_1^A+\frac{dx_2^A}{x_2^A}=0\\ \Longrightarrow\quad-\left.\frac{dx_2}{dx_1}\right|_A=x_2^A,\\[6pt] du^B=dx_1^B+\frac{2\,dx_2^B}{x_2^B}=0\\ \Longrightarrow\quad-\left.\frac{dx_2}{dx_1}\right|_B=\frac{x_2^B}{2}. \end{gathered}\]

At an interior optimum, \(MRS^A=MRS^B\). Combine this with feasibility:

\[\begin{gathered} x_2^A=\frac{x_2^B}{2},\qquad x_2^A+x_2^B=3,\\ 3x_2^A=3\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\boxed{x_2^A=1,\quad x_2^B=2}. \end{gathered}\]

2. Share good 1 while keeping both bundles interior

\[\begin{gathered} x^{A*}=(x_1^A,1),\\ x^{B*}=(5-x_1^A,2),\qquad0<x_1^A<5. \end{gathered}\]

These are all the interior Pareto optima. Preferences are increasing and convex, so the common tangency supports efficiency.

Read the diagram — the interior contract curve

  • Alice’s indifference curve through E.
  • Benjamin’s indifference curve through E.
  • Interior contract curve: all tangency allocations.
  • E: the equal-wealth equilibrium found in Question 3.
  1. Read a point from both sides. Alice starts at \(O_A\), bottom-left. Benjamin starts at \(O_B\), top-right. If Alice has \((x_1^A,x_2^A)\), Benjamin has \((5-x_1^A,3-x_2^A)\).
  2. Why is the line horizontal? Efficiency requires \(x_2^A=x_2^B/2\). Since good 2 totals 3, Alice must receive 1 and Benjamin 2, whatever the division of good 1.
  3. Read the tangency at \(E\). Alice receives \((3,1)\) and Benjamin \((2,2)\). Both indifference curves have slope \(-1\) in Alice’s coordinates: their marginal rates of substitution agree.
  4. Move along the line. At \(P=(1,1)\), Alice receives \((1,1)\) and Benjamin \((4,2)\). Moving from \(P\) to \(E\) gives Alice 2 units of good 1 and takes 2 from Benjamin. Both points are efficient, but the move is not a Pareto improvement.

Efficiency does not select a unique distribution

Good 2 is fixed at \((1,2)\), while good 1 can be shared in any interior way:

\[x_2^A=1,\qquad x_2^B=2,\qquad0<x_1^A<5.\]

The hollow endpoints are excluded because the question asks for interior allocations: at either endpoint, one person has no good 1. The two coloured curves show the tangency at \(E\); other points on the green line have their own tangent indifference curves.

Support every interior optimum as an equilibrium

1. Alice's demand at \(p_1=1\)

\[\begin{aligned} \mathcal L^A={}&x_1^A+\ln x_2^A\\ &+\lambda_A(\omega_1^A+p_2\omega_2^A-x_1^A-p_2x_2^A). \end{aligned}\]
\[1-\lambda_A=0,\qquad\frac1{x_2^A}-\lambda_Ap_2=0.\]

For an interior optimum, \(\lambda_A=1\), hence:

\[\boxed{x_2^A=\frac1{p_2}},\qquad\boxed{x_1^A=\omega_1^A+p_2\omega_2^A-1}.\]

2. Benjamin's demand

\[\begin{aligned} \mathcal L^B={}&x_1^B+2\ln x_2^B\\ &+\lambda_B(\omega_1^B+p_2\omega_2^B-x_1^B-p_2x_2^B). \end{aligned}\]
\[1-\lambda_B=0,\qquad\frac2{x_2^B}-\lambda_Bp_2=0.\]
\[\boxed{x_2^B=\frac2{p_2}},\qquad\boxed{x_1^B=\omega_1^B+p_2\omega_2^B-2}.\]

These expressions apply when the resulting demands for good 1 are positive, as required for the interior allocations considered here.

3. Clear markets and choose supporting endowments

\[\frac1{p_2}+\frac2{p_2}=3\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\boxed{p^*=(1,1)}.\]

For any target from Question 1, choose endowments equal to that target:

\[\begin{gathered} \omega^A=(x_1^A,1),\\ \omega^B=(5-x_1^A,2),\qquad0<x_1^A<5. \end{gathered}\]

The demand formulas return exactly those bundles: Alice spends \(x_1^A+1\), Benjamin \(7-x_1^A\). Both markets clear and both consumers optimize, so this is a Walrasian equilibrium.

The claim allows us to choose the endowments; it does not say that every efficient allocation is an equilibrium for the same original endowments.

Equilibrium with equal initial endowments

Divide each total endowment equally:

\[\omega^A=\omega^B=\left(\frac52,\frac32\right).\]

At \(p^*=(1,1)\), each consumer's wealth is \(5/2+3/2=4\). Substituting into the interior demands:

\[\begin{aligned} x_2^{A*}&=1,&\quad x_1^{A*}&=4-1=3,\\ x_2^{B*}&=2,&\quad x_1^{B*}&=4-2=2. \end{aligned}\]
\[\boxed{x^{A*}=(3,1)},\qquad\boxed{x^{B*}=(2,2)}.\]

Both bundles are interior, and \((3,1)+(2,2)=(5,3)\). Equal endowments give equal wealth, not identical consumption: Benjamin values good 2 more strongly.

The income transfer from Alice to Benjamin

1. Compare initial wealth with the target wealth

At the target prices \(p^*=(1,1)\), initial endowments \((4,2)\) and \((1,1)\) are worth:

\[\begin{aligned} \text{Alice: }&4+2=6,\\ \text{Benjamin: }&1+1=2. \end{aligned}\]

The target equilibrium requires wealth 4 for each:

\[T_A=4-6=-2,\qquad T_B=4-2=2.\]

Alice transfers 2 units of income to Benjamin. Transfers sum to zero. After the transfer, both face a budget of 4 and choose the equilibrium bundles from Question 3:

\[x^{A*}=(3,1),\qquad x^{B*}=(2,2).\]

At these prices, transferring 2 units of good 1 has the same wealth effect. Redistributing the endowments to \((5/2,3/2)\) each is another implementation.

Read the diagram — gains from trade versus redistribution

  • Alice’s initial utility: \(4+\ln2\).
  • Benjamin’s initial utility: 1.
  • Green lens: feasible gains from the initial endowment.
  • E: the equal-wealth target, outside that lens.
  1. Start at \(\omega=(4,2)\). Alice has \((4,2)\), so Benjamin has \((1,1)\). The two initial indifference curves intersect there.
  2. Locate a mutually beneficial exchange. At \(G=(4.5,1.5)\), Alice buys 0.5 unit of good 1 and sells 0.5 unit of good 2. Benjamin makes the opposite exchange and receives \((0.5,1.5)\). Both gain, so \(G\) belongs to the green lens.
  3. Read the target \(E=(3,1)\). Alice receives \((3,1)\), Benjamin \((2,2)\). This point lies below Alice’s blue initial indifference curve, so Alice is worse off. Benjamin is better off.
  4. Separate the two comparisons. Trade can improve on \(\omega\) for both people. The particular choice to equalize their wealth benefits Benjamin at Alice’s expense.

Read the diagram — redistribute first, then trade

  • Before: Alice’s budget is \(x_1^A+x_2^A=6\).
  • After: Alice’s budget is \(x_1^A+x_2^A=4\).

Why is this a common budget line?

Alice can afford the points on or below the orange line. In Alice’s coordinates, Benjamin’s budget becomes:

\[\begin{gathered} (5-x_1^A)+(3-x_2^A)\leq4\\ \Longleftrightarrow\quad x_1^A+x_2^A\geq4. \end{gathered}\]

Benjamin can afford the points on or above it. Both can afford an allocation only on the orange line. Their demands select \(E\) on that line; Question 2’s first-order conditions establish that each bundle is optimal.

Two different steps: the wealth transfer is a distributional choice; the subsequent voluntary exchange takes consumers to their preferred affordable bundles.
Exercise 3

Exercise 3. General Equilibrium with Production

Consider an economy with two consumers \(i\in\{A,B\}\), one firm, and two goods \(n\in\{1,2\}\). Utilities and endowments are:

Consumer A

\[\begin{gathered} u^A(x_1^A,x_2^A)=\ln(x_1^A)+\ln(x_2^A),\\ \omega^A=\left(\frac12,\frac12\right). \end{gathered}\]

Consumer B

\[\begin{gathered} u^B(x_1^B,x_2^B)=(x_1^B)^{1/4}(x_2^B)^{3/4},\\ \omega^B=\left(\frac12,\frac12\right). \end{gathered}\]

The firm

The firm produces good 2 using good 1 as an input:

\[y_2=\sqrt{y_1}.\]

Consumer B owns the firm. Here, \(y_1\geq0\) denotes the amount of good 1 used as an input, and \(y_2\) the output of good 2.

Questions

  1. Determine the demand for good 1 of each consumer and of the firm.
  2. From now on, normalize \(p_2=1\). Determine the equilibrium price \(p_1\).
  3. For this question, replace the production function by \(y_2=y_1\), with constant returns to scale. Determine the equilibrium price \(p_1\) and allocation \((x,y)\).
  4. For this question, consider the exchange economy without production consisting of consumers A and B. Determine the equilibrium price and allocation.

Consumers’ demands and the firm’s input demand

1. Consumer A

A receives no profit, so the budget is \(p_1x_1^A+p_2x_2^A=\frac12(p_1+p_2)\).

\[\begin{aligned} \mathcal L^A={}&\ln x_1^A+\ln x_2^A\\ &+\lambda_A\left[\frac12(p_1+p_2)-p_1x_1^A-p_2x_2^A\right]. \end{aligned}\]
\[\frac1{x_1^A}=\lambda_Ap_1,\qquad\frac1{x_2^A}=\lambda_Ap_2.\]

The two expenditures are equal. Substituting into the budget:

\[p_1x_1^A=p_2x_2^A,\qquad2p_1x_1^A=\frac12(p_1+p_2).\]
\[\begin{gathered} \boxed{x_1^A(p_1,p_2)=\frac14\left(1+\frac{p_2}{p_1}\right)},\\ x_2^A(p_1,p_2)=\frac14\left(1+\frac{p_1}{p_2}\right). \end{gathered}\]

2. Consumer B

B receives the firm's entire maximized profit \(\Pi(p_1,p_2)\). Use the original Cobb–Douglas utility:

\[\begin{aligned} \mathcal L^B={}&(x_1^B)^{1/4}(x_2^B)^{3/4}\\ &+\lambda_B\left[\frac12(p_1+p_2)+\Pi(p_1,p_2)\right.\\ &\hspace{3.5em}\left.-p_1x_1^B-p_2x_2^B\right]. \end{aligned}\]
\[\begin{gathered} \frac14(x_1^B)^{-3/4}(x_2^B)^{3/4}=\lambda_Bp_1,\\ \frac34(x_1^B)^{1/4}(x_2^B)^{-1/4}=\lambda_Bp_2. \end{gathered}\]

Divide the first condition by the second:

\[\frac{p_1}{p_2}=\frac{x_2^B}{3x_1^B}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad p_2x_2^B=3p_1x_1^B.\]

Substitute into B's budget:

\[4p_1x_1^B=\frac12(p_1+p_2)+\Pi(p_1,p_2).\]
\[\begin{aligned} x_1^B(p_1,p_2)&=\frac18\left(1+\frac{p_2}{p_1}\right)+\frac{\Pi(p_1,p_2)}{4p_1},\\ x_2^B(p_1,p_2)&=\frac38\left(1+\frac{p_1}{p_2}\right)+\frac{3\Pi(p_1,p_2)}{4p_2}. \end{aligned}\]

3. The firm's input demand and output supply

\[\max_{y_1\geq0}\;p_2\sqrt{y_1}-p_1y_1.\]

For positive prices, the first-order condition gives:

\[\frac{p_2}{2\sqrt{y_1}}-p_1=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\boxed{y_1(p_1,p_2)=\frac{p_2^2}{4p_1^2}}.\]

The second derivative is negative, confirming the unique maximum:

\[\frac{d^2\Pi}{dy_1^2}=-\frac{p_2}{4y_1^{3/2}}<0\quad(y_1>0).\]
\[y_2(p_1,p_2)=\sqrt{y_1(p_1,p_2)}=\frac{p_2}{2p_1}.\]

Maximum profit is:

\[\begin{aligned} \Pi(p_1,p_2)&=p_2\frac{p_2}{2p_1}-p_1\frac{p_2^2}{4p_1^2}\\ &=\boxed{\frac{p_2^2}{4p_1}}. \end{aligned}\]

4. Collect all demands for good 1

Substitute profit into B's demand:

\[\begin{aligned} x_1^A(p_1,p_2)&=\frac14\left(1+\frac{p_2}{p_1}\right),\\ x_1^B(p_1,p_2)&=\frac18\left(1+\frac{p_2}{p_1}\right)+\frac{p_2^2}{16p_1^2},\\ y_1(p_1,p_2)&=\frac{p_2^2}{4p_1^2}. \end{aligned}\]

The firm's demand uses good 1 as an input; it must be added to consumer demand when clearing that market.

The equilibrium price when \(p_2=1\)

1. Clear the market for good 1

The total endowment of good 1 is \(1/2+1/2=1\). Consumers and the firm share it:

\[x_1^A+x_1^B+y_1=1.\]

Set \(p_2=1\) in the demands from Question 1:

\[\begin{aligned} &\frac14\left(1+\frac1{p_1}\right) +\frac18\left(1+\frac1{p_1}\right)\\ &\qquad+\frac1{16p_1^2}+\frac1{4p_1^2}=1. \end{aligned}\]

Collect the constant, reciprocal-price, and squared reciprocal-price terms:

\[\frac38+\frac3{8p_1}+\frac5{16p_1^2}=1.\]

Multiplying by \(16p_1^2\) gives:

\[6p_1^2+6p_1+5=16p_1^2.\]
\[10p_1^2-6p_1-5=0.\]

2. Keep the positive root

\[p_1=\frac{6\pm\sqrt{36+200}}{20}.\]
\[\boxed{p_1^*=\frac{3+\sqrt{59}}{10}\approx1.0681},\qquad p_2^*=1.\]

The other root is negative. Walras' law ensures that good 2 clears as well.

For a direct check, substitute \(p_1^*\) into:

\[\underbrace{\frac58(1+p_1^*)+\frac3{16p_1^*}}_{x_2^{A*}+x_2^{B*}} =\underbrace{1+\frac1{2p_1^*}}_{1+y_2^*}.\]

Constant returns to scale

1. The firm's problem pins down prices

Now \(y_2=y_1\), with \(p_2=1\), so:

\[\Pi=(1-p_1)y_1.\]
  • If \(p_1<1\), profit increases without bound as production rises: there is no finite optimum.
  • If \(p_1>1\), the firm chooses \(y_1=0\). Consumer demand for good 1 is then \(\frac38(1+1/p_1)<1\), so that market cannot clear.
  • If \(p_1=1\), every production scale yields zero profit. Market clearing determines the scale.
\[\boxed{p_1^*=p_2^*=1},\qquad\boxed{\Pi^*=0}.\]

2. Compute consumption

Each consumer's endowment is worth 1, and B receives zero profit. Applying the spending shares:

\[\boxed{x^{A*}=\left(\frac12,\frac12\right)},\qquad \boxed{x^{B*}=\left(\frac14,\frac34\right)}.\]

Total consumption is \((3/4,5/4)\).

3. Choose the production scale that clears both markets

\[\begin{aligned} \frac34+y_1^*&=1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad y_1^*=\frac14,\\ y_2^*&=y_1^*=\frac14,\\ x_2^{A*}+x_2^{B*}&=\frac54=1+y_2^*. \end{aligned}\]

The firm uses 1/4 unit of good 1 and produces 1/4 unit of good 2. In the lecture's net-output convention, its production vector is \((-1/4,1/4)\).

Zero profit does not imply zero production. At equal prices, the firm is indifferent over scales; only the scale \(1/4\) clears the markets.

The pure exchange economy

1. Remove production and profit income

Each consumer still owns \((1/2,1/2)\). With no firm, the demands are:

\[\begin{aligned} x_1^A&=\frac14\left(1+\frac{p_2}{p_1}\right),& x_2^A&=\frac14\left(1+\frac{p_1}{p_2}\right),\\ x_1^B&=\frac18\left(1+\frac{p_2}{p_1}\right),& x_2^B&=\frac38\left(1+\frac{p_1}{p_2}\right). \end{aligned}\]

2. Clear good 1

\[\begin{aligned} x_1^A+x_1^B&=1,\\ \frac38\left(1+\frac{p_2}{p_1}\right)&=1,\\ \frac{p_2}{p_1}&=\frac53. \end{aligned}\]

With \(p_2=1\):

\[\boxed{p_1^*=\frac35},\qquad\boxed{p^*=\left(\frac35,1\right)}.\]

3. Compute the equilibrium allocation

\[\begin{aligned} x_1^{A*}&=\frac14\left(1+\frac53\right)=\frac23,\\ x_2^{A*}&=\frac14\left(1+\frac35\right)=\frac25,\\ x_1^{B*}&=\frac18\left(1+\frac53\right)=\frac13,\\ x_2^{B*}&=\frac38\left(1+\frac35\right)=\frac35. \end{aligned}\]
\[\boxed{x^{A*}=\left(\frac23,\frac25\right)},\qquad \boxed{x^{B*}=\left(\frac13,\frac35\right)}.\]

The bundles add to \((1,1)\), exactly the total endowment.

Exercise 4. First Welfare Theorem with Production

First Welfare Theorem. Let utility functions \(u\) be continuous and strictly increasing in \(x\). If an allocation \((x,y)\) and a price vector \(p\) form a Walrasian equilibrium, then \((x,y)\) is Pareto efficient.

Question

  1. Taking inspiration from the proof in the slides for the exchange economy, prove the First Welfare Theorem in the productive economy.

Proof of the First Welfare Theorem with production

1. Suppose a feasible Pareto improvement exists

Let \((x^*,y^*,p^*)\) be a Walrasian equilibrium. Suppose another feasible pair \((\widetilde x,\widetilde y)\) satisfies:

\[\begin{gathered} u_i(\widetilde x_i)\geq u_i(x_i^*)\quad\text{for every }i,\\ u_{i_0}(\widetilde x_{i_0})>u_{i_0}(x_{i_0}^*)\quad\text{for at least one }i_0. \end{gathered}\]

2. Use consumer optimization

Consumer \(i\)'s equilibrium wealth is the value of their endowment plus their share of each firm's profit:

\[p^*\cdot\omega_i+\sum_k\theta_{ki}\Pi_k^*,\qquad\Pi_k^*=p^*\cdot y_k^*.\]

A strictly preferred bundle must cost more than that wealth, or it would have been chosen. A weakly preferred bundle cannot cost less: the remaining budget and strict monotonicity would allow a strictly better affordable bundle.

\[\begin{gathered} p^*\cdot\widetilde x_i\geq p^*\cdot\omega_i+\sum_k\theta_{ki}\Pi_k^* \quad\text{for every }i,\\ p^*\cdot\widetilde x_{i_0}>p^*\cdot\omega_{i_0}+\sum_k\theta_{ki_0}\Pi_k^*. \end{gathered}\]

3. Sum across consumers

At least one inequality is strict. Since \(\sum_i\theta_{ki}=1\) for each firm, all profit is distributed:

\[\begin{aligned} \sum_i p^*\cdot\widetilde x_i &>\sum_i p^*\cdot\omega_i+\sum_i\sum_k\theta_{ki}\Pi_k^*\\ &=\sum_i p^*\cdot\omega_i+\sum_k\Pi_k^*. \end{aligned}\]

4. Use firm profit maximization

For every alternative feasible production plan \(\widetilde y_k\), profit maximization implies:

\[\Pi_k^*=p^*\cdot y_k^*\geq p^*\cdot\widetilde y_k.\]

Combine this inequality with the strict inequality from Step 3:

\[\begin{aligned} p^*\cdot\sum_i\widetilde x_i &>p^*\cdot\sum_i\omega_i+\sum_k\Pi_k^*\\ &\geq p^*\cdot\left(\sum_i\omega_i+\sum_k\widetilde y_k\right). \end{aligned}\]

5. Contradict feasibility

Feasibility requires, component by component:

\[\sum_i\widetilde x_i\leq\sum_i\omega_i+\sum_k\widetilde y_k.\]

Multiplying by the nonnegative equilibrium prices and summing gives:

\[p^*\cdot\sum_i\widetilde x_i \leq p^*\cdot\left(\sum_i\omega_i+\sum_k\widetilde y_k\right).\]

This contradicts Step 4. No feasible Pareto improvement exists, so the equilibrium allocation and production plan are Pareto efficient.

The extra step relative to pure exchange: firm profit maximization bounds the value of any alternative production plan. The contradiction compares values at the same prices; it does not infer a componentwise quantity inequality from a value inequality.
