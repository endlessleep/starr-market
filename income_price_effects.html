3. Income and Price Effects

Slutsky decomposition, elasticities, research focuses, and welfare effects of a price change.

Slutsky Equation

Assume that $u$ is a continuous utility function representing a locally non-satiated preference relation $\succeq$, that $p\gg0$ and $y=e(p,\bar u)$, and that $h(p,\bar u)$ and $x(p,y)$ are singleton-valued and differentiable at $(p,\bar u,y)$.

Then, for every $i,j$:

$$\frac{\partial x_i(p,y)}{\partial p_j}=\frac{\partial h_i(p,\bar u)}{\partial p_j}-\left[\frac{\partial x_i(p,y)}{\partial y}\right]x_j(p,y).$$

An intuition

Start from the same-problem identity:

$$h_i(p,\bar u)=x_i(p,e(p,\bar u)).$$

When $p_j$ changes, how much does $e$ change? It is $\partial e/\partial p_j$. We differentiate $e$ with respect to $p_j$ because $e$ contains $p_j$.

When $e$ changes, how much does $x_i$ change? It is $\partial x_i/\partial y$. We differentiate $x_i$ with respect to its income slot because this is where $e$ enters.

Differentiate with respect to $p_j$:

$$\frac{\partial h_i(p,\bar u)}{\partial p_j}=\frac{\partial x_i(p,y)}{\partial p_j}+\left[\frac{\partial x_i(p,y)}{\partial y}\right]\left[\frac{\partial e(p,\bar u)}{\partial p_j}\right],\qquad e(p,\bar u)=y.$$

By Shephard's Lemma, $\partial e(p,\bar u)/\partial p_j=h_j(p,\bar u)$. The same-problem identity gives $h_j(p,\bar u)=x_j(p,y)$. Substitution yields the Slutsky equation displayed above.

Read the derivation step by step

The identity starts with the Hicksian demand for good $i$. Its right-hand side is the Marshallian demand evaluated at the expenditure needed to attain $\bar u$.

$$h_i(p,\bar u)=x_i(p,e(p,\bar u)).$$

When $p_j$ changes, how much does $e$ change? It is $\partial e/\partial p_j$. We differentiate $e$ with respect to $p_j$ because $e$ contains $p_j$.

When $e$ changes, how much does $x_i$ change? It is $\partial x_i/\partial y$. We differentiate $x_i$ with respect to its income slot because this is where $e$ enters.

When $p_j$ changes, the right-hand side changes directly through $p_j$ and indirectly through $e(p,\bar u)$. This gives the two terms in the derivative:

$$\frac{\partial h_i(p,\bar u)}{\partial p_j}=\frac{\partial x_i(p,y)}{\partial p_j}+\left[\frac{\partial x_i(p,y)}{\partial y}\right]\left[\frac{\partial e(p,\bar u)}{\partial p_j}\right].$$

Shephard's Lemma replaces the last derivative by Hicksian demand for good $j$:

$$\frac{\partial e(p,\bar u)}{\partial p_j}=h_j(p,\bar u).$$

At the same problem, $h_j(p,\bar u)=x_j(p,y)$. Therefore:

$$\frac{\partial h_i(p,\bar u)}{\partial p_j}=\frac{\partial x_i(p,y)}{\partial p_j}+\left[\frac{\partial x_i(p,y)}{\partial y}\right]x_j(p,y).$$

Moving the final term to the other side gives:

$$\frac{\partial x_i(p,y)}{\partial p_j}=\frac{\partial h_i(p,\bar u)}{\partial p_j}-\left[\frac{\partial x_i(p,y)}{\partial y}\right]x_j(p,y).$$

Two remarks about the Slutsky equation

  • It gives a relationship between the Marshallian and Hicksian demands for good $i$.
  • It allows us to analyze the response of the Marshallian demand for good $i$ to a price change in good $j$.
  • It can also be used to analyze the response to an own-price change, when $i=j$.

Substitution and Income Effects

Total effect
$$\frac{\partial x_i(p,y)}{\partial p_j}$$
Substitution effect
$$\frac{\partial h_i(p,\bar u)}{\partial p_j}$$
Income effect
$$-\left[\frac{\partial x_i(p,y)}{\partial y}\right]x_j(p,y)$$

Substitution effect

When $p_j$ varies, the substitution effect is the change in demand for good $i$ holding utility constant. A change in $p_j$ changes the optimal bundle $(x_i^*,x_j^*)$. The resulting change in demand for good $i$ depends on whether $i$ is a substitute or complement for $j$.

Income effect

When $p_j$ varies, the income effect is the change in demand for good $i$ holding income constant. A change in $p_j$ changes the consumer's purchasing power. The resulting change in demand for good $i$ depends on whether good $i$ is normal or inferior.

Own-price change

For an own-price change, $i=j$, the substitution effect is always negative:

$$\frac{\partial h_i(p,\bar u)}{\partial p_i}\leq0.$$

Income and Price Elasticities

Income-expansion path

The income-expansion path represents the consumer's optimal bundles for different income levels, keeping prices constant.

As income increases, the budget line shifts outward in parallel because prices remain unchanged. The consumer therefore reaches different optimal bundles. Connecting these optimal choices shows how the quantities of goods 1 and 2 respond to changes in income.

Income elasticity of demand

The income elasticity measures the percentage change in the quantity of $x_i$ demanded per 1% change in income:

$$\eta_i=\frac{\%\,\text{change in Marshallian demand}}{\%\,\text{change in income}}=\left[\frac{\partial x_i(p,y)}{\partial y}\right]\frac{y}{x_i(p,y)}.$$

Price-expansion path

The price-expansion path represents the optimal choices for different $p_1$, keeping $p_2$ and income constant.

As the price of good 1 changes, the budget line rotates because income and the price of good 2 remain unchanged. The consumer therefore chooses different optimal bundles. Connecting these optimal choices shows how the quantities of goods 1 and 2 respond to changes in the price of good 1.

Price elasticity of demand

The price elasticity measures the percentage change in the quantity of $x_i$ demanded per 1% change in price $p_j$:

$$\varepsilon_{ij}=\frac{\%\,\text{change in Marshallian demand for }x_i}{\%\,\text{change in price }p_j}=\left[\frac{\partial x_i(p,y)}{\partial p_j}\right]\frac{p_j}{x_i(p,y)}.$$

If $i=j$, $\varepsilon_{ii}$ measures own-price elasticity. If $i\neq j$, $\varepsilon_{ij}$ measures cross-price elasticity.

Slutsky equation in elasticity form

$$\varepsilon_{ij}=\varepsilon_{ij}^h-\eta_i\left(\frac{p_jx_j}{y}\right).$$
  • $\varepsilon_{ij}$: Marshallian price elasticity;
  • $\varepsilon_{ij}^h$: Hicksian price elasticity;
  • $\eta_i$: income elasticity;
  • $p_jx_j/y$: share of good $j$ in total expenditure.

How to read this table

$\eta_i$ — Income elasticity

What happens to demand for good $i$ when income changes?

  • $\eta_i>0$: Normal good → income $\uparrow$ ⇒ demand for good $i$ $\uparrow$.
  • $\eta_i<0$: Inferior good → income $\uparrow$ ⇒ demand for good $i$ $\downarrow$.
  • $\eta_i>1$: Luxury good → demand increases more than proportionally with income.

$\varepsilon_{ii}$ — Own-price elasticity

What happens to demand for good $i$ when its own price changes?

  • $\varepsilon_{ii}<0$: Ordinary good → price of $i$ $\uparrow$ ⇒ demand for $i$ $\downarrow$.
  • $\varepsilon_{ii}>0$: Giffen good → price of $i$ $\uparrow$ ⇒ demand for $i$ $\uparrow$.

$\varepsilon_{ij}$ — Cross-price elasticity

What happens to demand for good $i$ when the price of another good $j$ changes?

  • $\varepsilon_{ij}>0$: Gross substitutes → price of $j$ $\uparrow$ ⇒ demand for $i$ $\uparrow$.
  • $\varepsilon_{ij}<0$: Gross complements → price of $j$ $\uparrow$ ⇒ demand for $i$ $\downarrow$.
Research Focus

Giffen Goods

Intuition Behind Giffen Behavior

Consider a poor household near a subsistence level of nutrition, whose diet consists of only two foods: a staple food with a high calorie-to-price ratio and bad taste, and a “fancy” food with a low calorie/price ratio and good taste.

Question. What happens to food consumption when the price of the staple food increases?

Answer. Staple food consumption increases: Giffen behavior.

If the price of bread increases, the household can no longer afford the original bundle of foods. If it increases its consumption of meat, it will fall below its required caloric intake. It must instead increase its consumption of bread, which is still the cheapest source of calories, and cut back on meat.

Overview

A Giffen good is a good for which an increase in its price causes an increase in its quantity:

$$\varepsilon_{ii}>0.$$

The positive own-price elasticity arises when a negative income effect dominates the substitution effect:

$$\varepsilon_{ii}=\varepsilon_{ii}^h-\eta_i\left(\frac{p_i}{y}\right)x_i>0.$$
  • own price $\varepsilon$;
  • substitution effect $<0$;
  • income effect $<0$.

Giffen goods are named after Sir Robert Giffen, who is said to have observed this phenomenon with bread during the Irish potato famine (allegedly). Better terminology would emphasize that the Giffen property is attached to the good for a particular consumer in a particular situation.

Conditions for Giffen Behavior

  • The staple good is an inferior good: $\eta_i<0$.
  • The staple good comprises a large part of the household budget: $p_ix_i/y$.
  • The staple good has no ready substitute.
  • Households are concerned about their subsistence: not too rich and do not consume only the staple; not too poor.

Giffen goods are always inferior: they are consumed less as income rises. However, not all inferior goods are Giffen.

  • Income does not change the shape of indifference curves.
  • It changes where on them the household makes choices: at different tangency points, the relative strength of the income effect changes.
The Giffen effect depends on the household’s initial situation. A very poor household already consumes almost only the staple good, so it has little fancy food left to cut. A standard household reacts normally to a staple-price increase by consuming less of the staple. The Giffen effect is most likely for a poor household in between: when the staple price rises, the household becomes effectively poorer, cuts its consumption of the fancy good, and increases its consumption of the staple good. The negative income effect is strong enough to dominate the substitution effect.
Empirical evidence

Jensen & Miller (2008)

Jensen, R. T., & Miller, N. H. (2008). “Giffen behavior and subsistence consumption.” American Economic Review, 98(4), 1553–1577.

Research question

The authors test whether Giffen behavior actually exists in real households, rather than being only a theoretical possibility. They study urban-poor households in China whose diets rely heavily on a staple food.

Hunan

Main staple: rice.
Meat is the “fancy” food.
Rice: 64% of calorie intake; meat: 7%.

Gansu

Main staple: wheat.
Wheat: 69% of calorie intake; meat: 1%.

The experiment changes the effective price of the staple food through subsidies and observes how staple consumption responds.

Why only some poor households?

For a Giffen good, a price increase must lead to:

$$p_{\text{staple}}\uparrow\quad\Rightarrow\quad x_{\text{staple}}\uparrow.$$

This requires a very strong negative income effect that dominates the substitution effect. The household must be poor, but neither too poor nor too rich:

  • Very poor: almost all calories already come from the staple. There is very little fancy food left to reduce, so the household has little possibility of shifting expenditure from fancy food toward more staple.
  • Poor / subsistence household: the staple represents a large part of the diet, but the household still consumes some fancy food. When the staple becomes more expensive, purchasing power falls strongly. The household cuts fancy food and relies more on the cheap calorie source: staple consumption can increase.
  • Standard / richer household: the household is less dependent on the staple for subsistence. The income effect is weaker, so the usual substitution effect dominates: staple price $\uparrow$ ⇒ staple consumption $\downarrow$.
Dependent enough on the staple, but still having other consumption to cut.

Experimental design

Treated households received subsidies for staple food (rice/wheat) for 5 months. Control households did not receive the subsidy. Consumption surveys were conducted during and after the subsidy.

Estimation strategy

$$\%\Delta \text{staple}_{i,t}=\alpha+\beta\,\%\Delta p_{i,t}+\sum\gamma\,\%\Delta Z_{i,t}+\sum\delta\,Y_{i,t}+c\,\Delta e_{i,t}.$$
  • $\%\Delta p_{i,t}$: % change in price of staple food ($<0$ in $t=2$, $<0$ in $t=3$);
  • $\%\Delta Z_{i,t}$: % change in control variables, including income;
  • $Y_{i,t}$: county and time fixed effects.

Household categories

Households are divided into three categories. An initial calorie share of staple food $>0.8$ identifies subsistence and calorie-deprived households. An initial calorie share $<0.2$ identifies standard households.

Results

Hunan. The evidence is strong. For subsistence and calorie-deprived households:

$$1\%\uparrow\text{ in rice price}\quad\Rightarrow\quad0.45\%\uparrow\text{ in rice consumption.}$$

Rice therefore behaves as a Giffen good for these households. For standard households:

$$1\%\uparrow\text{ in rice price}\quad\Rightarrow\quad0.61\%\downarrow\text{ in rice consumption.}$$

They display the ordinary response to a price increase. The relationship has an inverted-U shape: price elasticity is positive for initial staple shares from 0.53 to 0.84, and peaks at a 0.70 calorie share, with a price elasticity of 0.85.

Why is the evidence weaker in Gansu?

  1. Wheat has more substitution possibilities. Households can switch away from wheat more easily, strengthening the usual substitution response.
  2. Meat represents only about 1% of calories. There is almost no fancy food to cut, so households have little scope to finance additional wheat consumption by reducing meat.
Research Focus

Conspicuous Consumption

What is the question?

Conspicuous consumption means purchasing goods or services to display wealth, status, or social prestige, rather than only for their intrinsic utility or functionality. The idea, originating with Veblen, is that demand for visible goods may increase disproportionately with income.

Do more visible goods have higher income elasticities?

The course first establishes a standard benchmark. It then adds social-status concerns, obtains the prediction $\eta_v>1$ for the visible good, and finally tests this prediction in the data.

1. BenchmarkWithout status concerns, the share spent on $v$ is constant.
2. InformationOthers observe $v$, but not income $y$.
3. PredictionStatus concerns make the visible-good share rise with income.
4. TestCompare visibility with income elasticities.

Step 1 — The standard benchmark

Start with two goods, $v$ and $w$. Income is $y$, prices are normalized to 1, and the budget constraint is $v+w=y$.

$$f(v,w)=\beta_v\ln(v)+\beta_w\ln(w),\qquad \beta=\frac{\beta_v}{\beta_w}.$$

The standard utility-maximization solution is:

$$v(y)=\left[\frac{\beta}{1+\beta}\right]y.$$

This is the Engel curve for $v$: it tells us how consumption of $v$ changes when income changes, holding prices fixed. Dividing by $y$ gives the share spent on $v$:

$$\frac{v}{y}=\frac{\beta}{1+\beta}.$$

The share is constant, so richer consumers spend the same share of income on $v$. This gives the benchmark:

$$\eta_v=\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}\right)\left(\frac{y}{v}\right)=1.$$

Step 2 — Why add social status?

We now ask what changes if part of utility comes from how other people perceive the consumer. The utility function becomes:

$$u=(1-\alpha)f(v,w)+\alpha f(\hat v,\hat w).$$
  • $\hat v,\hat w$ are society's beliefs about one's consumption;
  • $\alpha$ measures the consumer's concern for social status;
  • $v$ is visible: $\hat v=v$;
  • $w$ is private: $\hat w=\hat w(v)$;
  • income $y$ is private.

Because other people observe $v$ but not $y$, they can use visible consumption to form beliefs about income and private consumption. The consumer derives utility from these beliefs.

Step 3 — Read the Engel curve from what is observable

The usual Engel curve asks: “If I know income $y$, how much $v$ will the consumer buy?” When $\alpha=0$:

$$v=\left[\frac{\beta}{1+\beta}\right]y.$$

Here the useful question is the inverse one: “If I observe $v$, what income $y$ do I infer?” Without status concerns:

$$y=\left[\frac{1+\beta}{\beta}\right]v.$$

This inverse form matters because $v$ is observable while income is not.

Step 4 — The prediction with social-status concerns

With social-status concerns, $\alpha>0$, and the Engel curve becomes:

$$y=\left[\frac{1+\beta}{\alpha+\beta}\right]v+C\,v^{-\beta/\alpha},\qquad C>0.$$

The supplied course material states this result; it does not provide the intermediate derivation from the social-status utility model.

Divide by $v$ to read the budget share:

$$\frac{y}{v}=\frac{1+\beta}{\alpha+\beta}+C\,v^{-(1+\beta/\alpha)}.$$

Read this expression step by step:

  1. As visible consumption $v$ increases, $C\,v^{-(1+\beta/\alpha)}$ decreases because $v$ is raised to a negative power.
  2. The first term, $(1+\beta)/(\alpha+\beta)$, is constant. Therefore, when the second term decreases, the whole ratio $y/v$ decreases.
  3. If $y/v$ decreases, its reciprocal $v/y$ increases.
  4. Since prices are normalized to 1, $v/y$ is the share of income spent on the visible good.

For example, moving from $v/y=20\%$ to $v/y=30\%$ means that the consumer is not merely buying more of the visible good because income is higher: the visible good now represents a larger fraction of total income.

Consumers therefore devote a larger share of income to the visible good as income and visible consumption increase. Visible consumption increases more than proportionally with income, so:

$$\eta_v>1.$$

Richer consumers increasingly shift their budget toward visible consumption, which allows them to distinguish themselves from lower-income consumers.

Empirical application

Heffetz (2011): testing the prediction

Heffetz, O. (2011). “A test of conspicuous consumption: Visibility and income elasticities.” Review of Economics and Statistics, 93(4), 1101–1117.

Data

The Consumer Expenditure Survey records household spending on categories such as clothing, food, housing, transportation, and luxury items. It also contains household total income and demographic data.

Research strategy

To test Veblen's intuition, the study derives income elasticities, builds a Visibility Index, and examines the relationship between visibility and income elasticities.

Visibility Index

The Visibility Index is based on a survey of about 500 US citizens. It asks whether, and how quickly, a respondent would notice that a similar household spends more than average on a given category.

“Imagine that you meet a new person who lives in a household similar to yours. Imagine that their household is not different from other similar households, except that they like to, and do, spend more than average on [XXX].

Would you notice this about them, and if so, for how long would you have to have known them, to notice it?

Would you notice it almost immediately upon meeting them for the first time, a short while after, a while after, only a long while after, or never?”

Results

Goods that are more visible to others have higher income elasticities. This is less true for poorer households who have no one to distinguish from.

Implications

  • Social factors are important in shaping consumer demand beyond basic utility maximization $\rightarrow$ Behavioral Economics.
  • Taxation on luxury goods could consider the social visibility aspect to address issues related to inequality $\rightarrow$ Jenkins (2016).

Welfare Effects of a Price Change

Main Question

By how much better- or worse-off is the consumer as a result of a price change?

Why Not Compare Utility Directly?

Look directly at the utility function? No. The answer would depend on which utility function $u$ is chosen to represent the consumer preferences.

While all utility functions give the same answer to the question of whether the consumer is better or worse off, they give different answers to the question of by how much the consumer is better or worse off. The answer would be in utils, which have no real meaning.

Use the Expenditure Function

Let $v$ be an arbitrary indirect utility function and $\bar p$ an arbitrary reference price vector. Recall that:

$$e(\bar p,v(p,w))$$

is the minimum income required to reach utility level $v(p,w)$ at prices $\bar p$. We can compare two utility levels in monetary terms:

$$e(\bar p,v(p',w))-e(\bar p,v(p,w)).$$

If this difference is greater than zero, then at the same reference prices $\bar p$, more income is required to reach $v(p',w)$ than $v(p,w)$. Since expenditure is increasing in utility, the consumer is therefore better off under $p'$.

Choosing the Reference Price

  • use the new prices $p'$ $\rightarrow$ Compensating Variation;
  • use the old prices $p$ $\rightarrow$ Equivalent Variation.

For the following slides, suppose the price change makes the consumer worse off:

$$u'=v(p',w)<v(p,w)=u.$$

CV and EV are expressed as positive monetary measures of this welfare loss.

Compensating Variation

Definition

Suppose the price of something the consumer buys increases:

$$p\longrightarrow p'.$$

Income $w$ stays the same, so the consumer can buy less. Utility falls:

$$u=v(p,w)\longrightarrow u'=v(p',w),\qquad u'

At the new prices $p'$, how much money must we give the consumer so that she is as well off as before the price increase? That amount is the Compensating Variation.

$$CV=e(p',v(p,w))-e(p',v(p',w)).$$

Using $e(p',v(p',w))=w$:

$$CV=e(p',u)-e(p',u')=e(p',u)-w.$$
Read the calculation step by step

Start with the definition at the new prices $p'$:

$$CV=e(p',v(p,w))-e(p',v(p',w)).$$

The earlier utility level is $u=v(p,w)$ and the utility after the price change is $u'=v(p',w)$. Replacing these two values gives:

$$CV=e(p',u)-e(p',u').$$

The course expenditure identity is $e(p',v(p',w))=w$. Since $u'=v(p',w)$, this is $e(p',u')=w$. Hence:

$$CV=e(p',u)-w.$$

For a harmful price change, $CV>0$. CV is the minimum compensation the consumer would require to accept the harmful change.

Equivalent Variation

Definition

Consider the same harmful price increase:

$$p\longrightarrow p',\qquad u=v(p,w)\longrightarrow u'=v(p',w),\qquad u'

This time, stand before the price increase, when prices are still $p$.

At the old prices $p$, how much income could we take away from the consumer so that she is as well off as she would be after the price increase? That amount is the Equivalent Variation.

$$EV=e(p,v(p,w))-e(p,v(p',w)).$$

Using $e(p,v(p,w))=w$:

$$EV=e(p,u)-e(p,u')=w-e(p,u').$$
Read the calculation step by step

Start with the definition at the old prices $p$:

$$EV=e(p,v(p,w))-e(p,v(p',w)).$$

The two utility levels are $u=v(p,w)$ and $u'=v(p',w)$. Replacing them gives:

$$EV=e(p,u)-e(p,u').$$

The course expenditure identity is $e(p,v(p,w))=w$. Since $u=v(p,w)$, this is $e(p,u)=w$. Hence:

$$EV=w-e(p,u').$$

For a harmful price change, $EV>0$. EV is the maximum amount the consumer would be willing to pay to avoid the harmful change.

Compensating and Equivalent Variation

What Do CV and EV Measure?

  • CV: compensation required after the harmful change.
  • EV: willingness to pay before the harmful change to avoid it.

For a harmful price change: $CV>0$ and $EV>0$.

Comparing CV and EV for a Harmful Price Change

For the price increase considered in the graphs, assume that $x_1$ is a normal good.

If $x_1$ Is a Normal Good

$$CV>EV.$$

Compensating the consumer after the price increase raises her demand for $x_1$, while taking income away before the price increase lowers her demand for $x_1$. Hence, restoring the old utility requires more money than the consumer would be willing to pay to avoid the price increase.

If $x_1$ Is an Inferior Good

$$EV>CV.$$

If There Is No Income Effect for $x_1$

$$CV=EV.$$

CV and EV Using the Indirect Utility Function

CV: be as well off today as she was yesterday.

$$v(p',w+CV)=u.$$

We give the consumer CV after the harmful price change.

EV: be as well off yesterday as she is today.

$$v(p,w-EV)=u'.$$

The consumer would be willing to give up EV before the price change to avoid it.

Relating Welfare to Hicksian Demand

Compensating Variation and Hicksian Demand

Assume only $p_1$ changes, with $p_1'>p_1$.

$$CV=e(p',u)-w=e(p',u)-e(p,u).$$
$$CV=\int_{p_1}^{p_1'}\left[\frac{\partial e(p,u)}{\partial p_1}\right]dp_1=\int_{p_1}^{p_1'}h_1(p,u)\,dp_1.$$

Using Shephard's Lemma, $\partial e(p,u)/\partial p_1=h_1(p,u)$.

Read the calculation step by step

From the definition of CV:

$$CV=e(p',u)-w.$$

At the original prices, the expenditure needed to attain $u$ is the original income, so $w=e(p,u)$:

$$CV=e(p',u)-e(p,u).$$

Because only $p_1$ changes, this expenditure difference is written as:

$$CV=\int_{p_1}^{p_1'}\left[\frac{\partial e(p,u)}{\partial p_1}\right]dp_1.$$

Using Shephard's Lemma, the derivative inside the integral is $h_1(p,u)$:

$$CV=\int_{p_1}^{p_1'}h_1(p,u)\,dp_1.$$

Equivalent Variation and Hicksian Demand

Assume only $p_1$ changes, with $p_1'>p_1$.

$$EV=w-e(p,u')=e(p',u')-e(p,u').$$
$$EV=\int_{p_1}^{p_1'}\left[\frac{\partial e(p,u')}{\partial p_1}\right]dp_1=\int_{p_1}^{p_1'}h_1(p,u')\,dp_1.$$

Using Shephard's Lemma.

Read the calculation step by step

From the definition of EV:

$$EV=w-e(p,u').$$

At the new prices, the expenditure needed to attain $u'$ is the income $w$, so $w=e(p',u')$:

$$EV=e(p',u')-e(p,u').$$

Because only $p_1$ changes, this expenditure difference is written as:

$$EV=\int_{p_1}^{p_1'}\left[\frac{\partial e(p,u')}{\partial p_1}\right]dp_1.$$

Using Shephard's Lemma gives:

$$EV=\int_{p_1}^{p_1'}h_1(p,u')\,dp_1.$$

Relating Welfare to Marshallian Demand

Change in Consumer Surplus

The change in Consumer Surplus following a price increase is:

$$\Delta CS=-\int_{p_1}^{p_1'}x_1(p,y)\,dp_1<0.$$

Equivalently, the Consumer Surplus loss is:

$$-\Delta CS=\int_{p_1}^{p_1'}x_1(p,y)\,dp_1>0.$$

Relating CV, EV, and Consumer Surplus

Available Welfare Measures

To measure by how much the consumer is better- or worse-off as a result of a price change, we can use the Compensating Variation, the Equivalent Variation, or the $\Delta$ in Consumer Surplus.

Which measure should we use to look at the welfare effects of a change in prices?

Compensating Variation

CV answers a compensation question. Imagine a highway has been built and cuts a neighbourhood. What is the minimum compensation residents would require after the highway is built to restore their original welfare?

Equivalent Variation

EV answers a willingness-to-pay question. Imagine the highway has not yet been built. What is the maximum amount residents would be willing to pay to prevent it from being built?

Consumer Surplus

Consumer Surplus is an intermediate measure between EV and CV without a direct interpretation in terms of welfare measured as money.

For a normal good and a price increase:

$$EV<-\Delta CS<CV.$$

With no income effect:

$$EV=-\Delta CS=CV.$$
