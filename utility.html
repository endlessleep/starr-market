2. Utility

Representation, ordinality, transformations, and continuous representations.

2.1 Utility Representation

Let $X$ be the set of alternatives.

A utility function $u: X \to \mathbb{R}$ represents a preference relation $\succeq$ if:

$$\boxed{x \succeq y \iff u(x) \ge u(y)}$$
A utility function assigns numbers to alternatives in a way that preserves their ranking.
  • Utility represents preferences — it does not create them.
  • Utility representations are generally not unique.
Ordinal vs. Cardinal
Utility is ordinal, not cardinal. The numerical values themselves do not measure the intensity of preference — only the ranking matters.
Invariance to Monotonic Transformations
If $u$ represents $\succeq$, then for any strictly increasing function $v: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, the composition $v \circ u$ also represents $\succeq$:
$$u(x) \ge u(y) \iff v(u(x)) \ge v(u(y))$$
Strictly increasing transformations preserve the order of numbers, so they preserve the preference ranking.

By definition of utility representation:

$$x \succeq y \iff u(x) \ge u(y)$$

Since $v: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is strictly increasing ($a \ge b \iff v(a) \ge v(b)$):

$$u(x) \ge u(y) \iff v(u(x)) \ge v(u(y))$$

Therefore: $x \succeq y \iff v(u(x)) \ge v(u(y))$, which means $(v \circ u)$ represents $\succeq$. $\blacksquare$

2.2 Representability Implies Rationality
$$\boxed{\text{Representable by utility} \implies \text{Rational (Complete + Transitive)}}$$

Real numbers are naturally complete (any two numbers can be compared via $\ge$) and transitive. A preference relation represented by a real-valued utility function inherits these exact properties.

Part 1: Completeness

Since $u(x), u(y) \in \mathbb{R}$, either $u(x) \ge u(y)$ or $u(y) \ge u(x)$.

Therefore, $x \succeq y$ or $y \succeq x$. Hence $\succeq$ is complete.

Part 2: Transitivity

Suppose $x \succeq y$ and $y \succeq z$.

Then $u(x) \ge u(y)$ and $u(y) \ge u(z)$, which implies $u(x) \ge u(z)$ by transitivity of $\ge$ on $\mathbb{R}$.

Therefore, $x \succeq z$. Hence $\succeq$ is transitive. $\blacksquare$

2.3 Rationality and Utility Representation
Does rationality imply utility representation?

Recall:

$$\text{Rational preferences} = \text{complete} + \text{transitive}$$

and:

$$x \succeq y \iff u(x) \ge u(y)$$

means that $u$ represents the preference relation.

Finite Choice Set

If $X$ is finite:

$$\boxed{\text{Rational} \implies \text{Representable}}$$
With finitely many alternatives, a complete and transitive ranking can always be encoded by utility numbers.

Example: $a \succ b \succ c$ can be represented by:

$$u(a) = 3, \quad u(b) = 2, \quad u(c) = 1$$
Infinite Choice Set

If $X$ is infinite:

$$\boxed{\text{Rational} \not\implies \text{Representable in general}}$$
“Not in general” does not mean that rational preferences on infinite sets are never representable. Some are representable, some are not.

Let $X = \mathbb{R}_+$ and define:

$$x \succeq y \iff x \ge y$$

Then:

$$u(x) = x$$

represents these preferences.

The utility function preserves exactly the same ranking as the numerical order.

Why can representation fail on an infinite set?
Rationality only imposes completeness and transitivity. On an infinite set, this may still allow preference relations that behave discontinuously or exhibit “jumps”.
This motivates continuity as an additional condition.
2.4 Continuity
Continuity of preferences avoids “jumps” in preferences.
$$\boxed{\text{Rational + Continuous} \implies \text{Representable by a continuous utility function}}$$

(when $X \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$)

Take a sequence of bundles $y^k$ that gets closer and closer to a bundle $y$:

$$y^k \to y$$

If each $y^k$ is at least as good as a fixed bundle $x$:

$$y^k \succeq x \qquad \forall k$$

then continuity requires:

$$y \succeq x$$

If all nearby bundles are at least as good as $x$, the limiting bundle should not suddenly become worse than $x$.

Lexicographic Preferences on $\mathbb{R}^2_+$:

For $x = (x_1, x_2)$ and $y = (y_1, y_2)$,

$$x \succ y \iff x_1 > y_1 \quad \text{or} \quad (x_1 = y_1 \text{ and } x_2 > y_2)$$

The first coordinate has absolute priority over the second.

Illustration of the “jump at the limit”:

Consider $x = (0.5, 0.5)$ and the sequence $y^k = \left(0.5 + \frac{1}{k}, 0\right)$.

  • For every finite $k$: $y^k \succ x$ because $0.5 + \frac{1}{k} > 0.5$.
  • As $k \to \infty$: $y^k \to (0.5, 0)$.
  • At the limit: $x \succ (0.5, 0)$ because the first coordinates are equal ($0.5 = 0.5$) and $0.5 > 0$.

The ranking jumps at the limit, so lexicographic preferences are not continuous.

This provides a rational preference relation on an infinite set for which rationality alone is not enough.
