Part II

Preamble

Before setting up the consumer’s optimization problem, we specify the environment in which choices are made. The following assumptions explain what consumers know, how they face prices, and which quantities they can choose.

Perfect information

The consumer knows the relevant goods, their prices, and the budget available for consumption. The choice problem is formulated with this information available.

Price-taking consumers

The consumer takes market prices as given. She chooses how much to buy at these prices; her own purchase does not determine the price she faces.

Linear prices

Each unit of good $i$ costs $p_i$. Buying $x_i$ units therefore costs $p_ix_i$, and total expenditure is the sum of these costs across goods.

Divisible goods

Quantities need not be whole numbers. Consumption can be adjusted continuously, which is why bundles are represented by nonnegative real-valued coordinates.

Setting up the UMP

The consumer’s utility-maximization problem

Let $x=(x_1,\ldots,x_L)$ be a bundle, $p=(p_1,\ldots,p_L)$ the price vector, and $y$ income. Here, $y$ denotes money available to spend. Total expenditure is $p\cdot x=\sum_{i=1}^L p_ix_i$. The number of goods is denoted by $L$ here and by $n$ in the later formulas.

The setup assumes that $u$ is continuous, differentiable, and strictly quasi-concave, and that the budget set $B(p,y)$ is compact. Continuity rules out jumps in utility; differentiability allows us to work with marginal utilities. Strict quasi-concavity is the utility representation of the strict convexity of preferences used below.

The consumer chooses a nonnegative bundle that gives the highest utility while remaining affordable:

$$\max_{x\in\mathbb R_+^L}u(x)\quad\text{s.t. }p\cdot x\leq y.$$

“Subject to” means that the maximization is restricted by the budget. A bundle with a higher utility is not an available choice if its cost exceeds income.

Marshallian demand: $x(p,y)$

The solution to the UMP: the bundle, or set of bundles, chosen at the given prices and income.

Indirect utility: $v(p,y)$

The value of the UMP: the highest utility attainable at those prices and that income.

Budget set and budget line

With two goods, the budget set contains every bundle that costs at most $y$. It includes bundles that leave some income unspent:

$$p_1x_1+p_2x_2\leq y.$$

The budget line contains the bundles that cost exactly $y$. On this line, the whole budget is spent:

$$p_1x_1+p_2x_2=y.$$

To express the line in terms of $x_2$, subtract $p_1x_1$ from both sides, then divide by $p_2>0$:

$$\begin{aligned} p_2x_2&=y-p_1x_1,\\ x_2&=\frac{y}{p_2}-\frac{p_1}{p_2}x_1. \end{aligned}$$

The vertical intercept $y/p_2$ is the amount of good 2 the consumer can buy when all income goes to that good. Similarly, the horizontal intercept $y/p_1$ is the amount of good 1 affordable when nothing is spent on good 2.

The slope is $-p_1/p_2$. Along the budget line, buying more of good 1 leaves less income for good 2. The negative sign records this trade-off; the price ratio determines how much good 2 must be given up.

Marshallian Demand

Marshallian demand identifies the utility-maximizing choices within the budget set:

$$x(p,y)=\underset{x\in B(p,y)}{\arg\max}\;u(x).$$

The notation $\arg\max$ returns the maximizing bundles. Two questions must be distinguished: does an optimal bundle exist, and is there only one? We then consider whether the optimum exhausts the budget and how it responds when all prices and income are scaled together.

Existence

If preferences are rational and continuous and $B(p,y)$ is compact, an optimal bundle exists. With strictly positive prices and nonnegative income:

$$p\gg0,\quad y\geq0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x(p,y)\neq\varnothing.$$

The notation $p\gg0$ means $p_i>0$ for every good. The conclusion $x(p,y)\neq\varnothing$ means that there is at least one utility-maximizing affordable bundle.

A compact set is closed, so it includes its boundary points, and bounded, so it does not extend indefinitely in any direction.

Proof sketch

The feasible set is nonempty and compact. The zero bundle is affordable because it costs zero and $y\geq0$. Thus the budget set contains at least one bundle.

Preferences admit a continuous utility representation. Rationality and continuity allow us to represent their ranking by a continuous function $u$, as in choice theory.

Apply the Weierstrass Theorem. A continuous function on a nonempty compact set attains a maximum. Applying this result to $u$ on $B(p,y)$ gives at least one optimal bundle, so $x(p,y)$ is nonempty.

A single bundle or a set of bundles?

If preferences are strictly convex and the budget set is convex, the optimum is unique, provided it exists. Marshallian demand is then single-valued: a given pair $(p,y)$ determines one bundle.

The budget set is convex: if two bundles are affordable, any mixture of them is affordable as well. Strict convexity of preferences rules out two distinct bundles both being optimal.

Proof by contradiction

Suppose two distinct bundles are optimal. Let $x$ and $x'$ both maximize preferences over $B(p,y)$, with $x\neq x'$. Neither can be strictly better than the other, since both are optimal. Hence $x\sim x'$.

Take a genuine mixture. For $\lambda\in(0,1)$, convexity of the budget set gives:

$$\lambda x+(1-\lambda)x'\in B(p,y).$$

Use strict convexity of preferences. The endpoints are distinct and indifferent, and neither weight is zero. Their mixture is strictly preferred to either endpoint:

$$\lambda x+(1-\lambda)x'\succ x.$$

This is a contradiction. We have found an affordable bundle strictly preferred to $x$, although $x$ was assumed optimal. Two distinct optima are therefore impossible: it must be the case that $x=x'$.

Walras’ Law

If preferences are locally non-satiated, an optimal bundle uses the whole income. For every $p\gg0$, $y\geq0$, and $x\in x(p,y)$:

$$p\cdot x=y.$$

This is Walras’ Law. At the optimum, expenditure equals income: the budget constraint holds with equality. Recall that strict monotonicity, commonly assumed in consumer theory, implies local non-satiation.

Proof by contradiction

Suppose an optimum leaves money unspent. Take $x\in x(p,y)$ and assume $p\cdot x<y$.

Use local non-satiation. There is a bundle $x'$ strictly preferred to $x$ as close to $x$ as required. Because expenditure at $x$ is strictly below income, a sufficiently small change still leaves expenditure below $y$. We can therefore choose $x'$ so that:

$$x'\succ x,\qquad p\cdot x'<y.$$

Contradiction. The bundle $x'$ is affordable and strictly preferred to the supposed optimum. Thus an optimum cannot satisfy $p\cdot x<y$. Since it is affordable, it must satisfy $p\cdot x=y$.

Homogeneity of degree zero

Scaling all prices and income by the same positive factor leaves Marshallian demand unchanged. For $\lambda>0$:

$$x(\lambda p,\lambda y)=x(p,y).$$

If every price rises by the factor $\lambda$ while income rises by exactly the same factor, affordability is unchanged. The consumer faces the same choices and therefore has the same demand. This also means that the currency used to express prices and income does not affect demand.

The budget set is unchanged

At the scaled prices and income, affordability requires $(\lambda p)\cdot x\leq\lambda y$. Since $\lambda>0$, division by $\lambda$ preserves the inequality:

$$\begin{aligned} (\lambda p)\cdot x\leq\lambda y &\iff \lambda(p\cdot x)\leq\lambda y\\ &\iff p\cdot x\leq y. \end{aligned}$$

Therefore $B(\lambda p,\lambda y)=B(p,y)$. Preferences and the feasible set are unchanged, so the consumer solves the same maximization problem and obtains the same demand.

Solving the UMP

Setting up the Lagrangian

Using the binding budget constraint, the Lagrangian combines utility with the difference between income and expenditure:

$$\mathcal L(x,\lambda)=u(x)+\lambda(y-p\cdot x).$$

Equivalently, $\mathcal L(x,\lambda)=u(x)-\lambda(p\cdot x-y)$. The two expressions are identical: changing the order inside the parentheses changes its sign.

With two goods, this becomes:

$$\mathcal L(x_1,x_2,\lambda)=u(x_1,x_2)+\lambda(y-p_1x_1-p_2x_2).$$
$$\begin{gathered} \mathcal L(x_1,x_2,\lambda)=u(x_1,x_2)\\ {}+\lambda(y-p_1x_1-p_2x_2). \end{gathered}$$

For a differentiable interior optimum $x^*=(x_1^*,x_2^*)$, with $x_1^*>0$ and $x_2^*>0$, the first-order conditions are:

Differentiate with respect to $x_1$

$$\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial x_1} =\frac{\partial u(x^*)}{\partial x_1}-\lambda p_1=0.$$

An additional unit of good 1 contributes its marginal utility and reduces the unspent budget by $p_1$.

Differentiate with respect to $x_2$

$$\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial x_2} =\frac{\partial u(x^*)}{\partial x_2}-\lambda p_2=0.$$

The same reasoning applies to good 2, using its own marginal utility and price.

Differentiate with respect to $\lambda$

$$\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\lambda} =y-p_1x_1^*-p_2x_2^*=0.$$

This condition restores the budget equality: the chosen bundle costs exactly $y$.

Interior solutions: MRS and the price ratio

At an interior solution, the first two conditions give $MU_1=\lambda p_1$ and $MU_2=\lambda p_2$, where $MU_i=\partial u(x^*)/\partial x_i$. Taking their ratio, with positive marginal utilities, cancels the common multiplier:

$$\frac{MU_1}{MU_2}=\frac{\lambda p_1}{\lambda p_2}=\frac{p_1}{p_2}.$$
$$MRS_{12}(x^*)= \frac{\partial u(x^*)/\partial x_1}{\partial u(x^*)/\partial x_2} =\frac{p_1}{p_2}.$$

The marginal rate of substitution describes the consumer’s willingness to trade good 2 for good 1. The price ratio describes the trade-off required by the market. At the interior optimum, these two ratios coincide.

The Lagrange multiplier: marginal utility of income

The Envelope Theorem lets us evaluate the effect of a parameter on the optimized value by differentiating the Lagrangian with respect to that parameter, evaluated at the optimum.

Here, the value function is indirect utility:

$$v(p,y)=\max_{x\in B(p,y)}u(x).$$

Income $y$ enters $\mathcal L=u(x)-\lambda(p\cdot x-y)$ through the term $+\lambda y$. Holding the choice and multiplier at their optimal values, differentiation with respect to $y$ gives:

$$\frac{\partial v(p,y)}{\partial y} =\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial y} =\lambda.$$

Thus $\lambda$ is the marginal utility of income: the rate at which the consumer’s maximum attainable utility increases when income increases. It is also called the shadow value of income.

Corner solutions

At a corner solution, one good is not consumed: $x_1^*=0$ or $x_2^*=0$. To understand how this can arise, consider the comparison developed in the course:

$$MRS_{12}=\frac{MU_1}{MU_2}<\frac{p_1}{p_2}.$$

The consumer is willing to give up less of good 2 for an additional unit of good 1 than the market requires. Good 1 is therefore relatively too expensive for her willingness to substitute. She wants to consume less of good 1 and more of good 2.

As consumption adjusts, there are two possibilities:

  • The MRS reaches $p_1/p_2$ while both quantities remain positive. The adjustment reaches an interior solution.
  • The MRS remains below $p_1/p_2$ all the way to $x_1=0$. The adjustment ends at the corner $x_1^*=0$.

Indirect Utility Function

The indirect utility function records the most utility a consumer can obtain at prices $p$ and income $y$:

$$v(p,y)=\max_{x\in B(p,y)}u(x).$$

The input is the price vector and income; the output is the utility reached after making the best affordable choice. Roy’s identity links this optimized utility to Marshallian demand.

Roy’s identity

Roy’s Identity → Marshallian demand.

$$x_i(p,y)=-\frac{\partial v(p,y)/\partial p_i}{\partial v(p,y)/\partial y}.$$

Take the function $v(p,y)$:

  1. Differentiate $v(p,y)$ with respect to $p_i$.
  2. Differentiate $v(p,y)$ with respect to $y$.
  3. Take the negative of the first derivative divided by the second derivative.
$$v(p,y)\longrightarrow\text{Roy’s Identity}\longrightarrow x_i(p,y)\text{ = Marshallian demand.}$$

The EmP

The consumer’s expenditure-minimization problem

The consumer is now given a utility level $\bar u$ to reach. The expenditure-minimization problem chooses a bundle that reaches at least this level at the lowest possible cost:

$$\min_{x\in\mathbb R_+^n}p\cdot x \quad\text{s.t. }u(x)\geq\bar u.$$

The objective is expenditure $p\cdot x$. The constraint $u(x)\geq\bar u$ requires the bundle to provide the specified utility level or more.

Hicksian demand: $h(p,u)$

The solution to the EmP: the bundle that minimizes expenditure at the given prices and required utility.

Expenditure function: $e(p,u)$

The value of the EmP: the minimum expenditure needed to meet that utility requirement.

In $h(p,u)$ and $e(p,u)$, the second argument $u$ is the required utility level. We also write this target as $\bar u$ to distinguish it from the utility function $u(x)$.

The expenditure function

The expenditure function gives the smallest amount the consumer must spend at prices $p$ to reach the target:

$$e(p,\bar u)=\min_{x\in\mathbb R_+^n} \{p\cdot x:\ u(x)\geq\bar u\}.$$

For attainable utility targets, the course states the following properties. In the first four, the target utility is held fixed.

Continuous in prices
Small changes in prices lead to small changes in the minimum expenditure required. The expenditure function does not jump as prices vary.
Increasing in prices
Raising prices cannot reduce the minimum cost of attaining the target. “Increasing” here means non-decreasing; it does not require a strict rise for every price change.
Concave in prices
At a weighted average of two price vectors, minimum expenditure is at least the same weighted average of the two minimum expenditures. This is a property of how $e$ depends on prices, with required utility fixed.
Homogeneous of degree one in prices
Multiplying all prices by the same positive factor multiplies the minimum expenditure by that factor: $e(\lambda p,u)=\lambda e(p,u)$ for $\lambda>0$.
Increasing in required utility
With prices fixed, requiring a higher utility level raises the expenditure needed to reach the target.

Shephard’s lemma

Shephard’s Lemma → Hicksian demand.

$$h_i(p,\bar u)=\frac{\partial e(p,\bar u)}{\partial p_i}.$$

Take the function $e(p,\bar u)$ and differentiate it with respect to $p_i$.

$$e(p,\bar u)\longrightarrow\frac{\partial}{\partial p_i}\longrightarrow h_i(p,\bar u)\text{ = Hicksian demand.}$$

Duality of the Consumer’s Problem

The duality principle

The consumer’s choice can be viewed from two perspectives. The primal problem, the UMP, fixes income and maximizes utility. The dual problem, the EmP, fixes required utility and minimizes expenditure.

When the utility target and income are matched as below, the two problems lead to the same optimal consumption bundle. The identities specify how to make that match.

Suppose $u(\cdot)$ represents rational, continuous, and locally non-satiated preferences on $\mathbb R_+^n$. Prices satisfy $p\gg0$ and income is nonnegative.

From utility maximization to expenditure minimization

Start with an optimal UMP bundle $x^*$ at income $y>0$. Its utility is $u(x^*)=v(p,y)$. Now use that achieved utility as the target for the EmP, at the same prices.

The UMP optimum also minimizes the expenditure needed to reach this target:

$$h(p,v(p,y))=x(p,y).$$

Read the inside argument first: $v(p,y)$ gives the utility reached with income $y$. Hicksian demand then asks for the least-cost bundle delivering that level. It returns the same optimal bundle.

The minimum expenditure is exactly the original income:

$$e(p,v(p,y))=y.$$

From expenditure minimization to utility maximization

Start with an optimal EmP bundle $x^*$ for an attainable target $\bar u\geq u(0)$. Its expenditure is $p\cdot x^*=e(p,\bar u)$. Now give the consumer exactly this amount as income in the UMP, at the same prices.

The EmP optimum also solves the UMP at this income:

$$x(p,e(p,\bar u))=h(p,\bar u).$$

Here, $e(p,\bar u)$ becomes the income argument of Marshallian demand. The resulting optimal bundle is the Hicksian bundle with which we started.

The maximum attainable utility is exactly the original target:

$$v(p,e(p,\bar u))=\bar u.$$
