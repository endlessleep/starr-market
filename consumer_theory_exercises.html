Exercise 1

Exercise 1. Duality of the consumer problem

For each utility function below, draw the indifference curves and compute the Marshallian demand, Hicksian demand, indirect utility function, and expenditure function.

1

Utility function

$$u(x,y)=x^\alpha y^{1-\alpha},\qquad 0<\alpha<1.$$
2

Utility function

$u(x,y)=\max(\alpha x,\alpha y)+\min(x,y),\qquad 0<\alpha<1.$
$\begin{gathered}u(x,y)=\max(\alpha x,\alpha y)\\+\min(x,y),\qquad 0<\alpha<1.\end{gathered}$

Assume $\dfrac{p_x}{p_y}\neq\alpha$ and $\dfrac{p_x}{p_y}\neq\dfrac1\alpha$.

3

Utility function

$u(x,y)=\log x+\beta\log y,\qquad 0<\beta<1.$
$\begin{gathered}u(x,y)=\log x+\beta\log y\\0<\beta<1.\end{gathered}$

Hint. There is a trick.

Starting point. Prices are positive. We use $m>0$ for income so that it is not confused with the good $y$. For Questions 1 and 2 the utility target is nonnegative; the calculations below use $\bar u>0$ (at $\bar u=0$, minimum expenditure and Hicksian quantities are zero). For Question 3 the target may be any real number.

Goal. For each utility function, identify its indifference curves, its two demand functions, and the associated value functions.

1. Utility representation

$$u(x,y)=x^\alpha y^{1-\alpha}, \qquad 0<\alpha<1.$$

Step 1 — Indifference curves

Fix utility at $\bar u$:

$$x^\alpha y^{1-\alpha}=\bar u.$$

Solve for $y$:

$$\begin{gathered} y^{1-\alpha} = \frac{\bar u}{x^\alpha}.\\[0.65em] \boxed{ y= \bar u^{\frac1{1-\alpha}} x^{-\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}} } \end{gathered}$$

Step 2 — Marshallian demand

For $m>0$, a bundle with both goods positive gives positive utility, whereas either zero quantity gives zero utility. The optimum is therefore interior.

The UMP is:

$$\begin{gathered}\max_{x,y\ge0} x^\alpha y^{1-\alpha}\\[.4em]\text{subject to }p_xx+p_yy=m.\end{gathered}$$

Because utility is increasing, use the binding budget constraint.

Method 1 — Lagrangian
$$\mathcal L = x^\alpha y^{1-\alpha} + \lambda(m-p_xx-p_yy).$$

FOCs:

$$\begin{gathered} \alpha x^{\alpha-1}y^{1-\alpha} = \lambda p_x\\[0.65em] (1-\alpha)x^\alpha y^{-\alpha} = \lambda p_y\\[0.65em] p_xx+p_yy=m. \end{gathered}$$

Divide the first FOC by the second:

$$\begin{gathered} \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\frac{y}{x} = \frac{p_x}{p_y}.\\[0.65em] y = \frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha} \frac{p_x}{p_y}x. \end{gathered}$$

Substitute into the budget constraint:

$$\begin{gathered} p_xx + p_y \left( \frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha} \frac{p_x}{p_y}x \right) = m.\\[0.65em] p_xx \left( 1+\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha} \right) = m.\\[0.65em] \boxed{ x^M(p,m)=\frac{\alpha m}{p_x} } \end{gathered}$$

and

$$\boxed{ y^M(p,m)=\frac{(1-\alpha)m}{p_y}. }$$
Method 2 — Optimality condition

For an interior solution:

$$MRS_{xy} = \frac{MU_x}{MU_y} = \frac{p_x}{p_y}.$$

Here:

$$\begin{gathered} MU_x = \alpha x^{\alpha-1}y^{1-\alpha}\\[0.65em] MU_y = (1-\alpha)x^\alpha y^{-\alpha}.\\[0.65em] MRS_{xy} = \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\frac{y}{x}. \end{gathered}$$

So:

$$\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\frac{y}{x} = \frac{p_x}{p_y}.$$

Combine this equation with:

$$p_xx+p_yy=m.$$

This gives again:

$$\boxed{ x^M=\frac{\alpha m}{p_x}, \qquad y^M=\frac{(1-\alpha)m}{p_y}. }$$

This second method uses directly the optimality condition obtained by dividing the two Lagrangian FOCs.

Step 3 — Indirect utility function

By definition:

$$v(p,m) = u(x^M,y^M).$$

Substitute the Marshallian demands:

$$\begin{gathered} v(p,m) = \left( \frac{\alpha m}{p_x} \right)^\alpha \left( \frac{(1-\alpha)m}{p_y} \right)^{1-\alpha}.\\[0.65em] \boxed{ v(p,m) = \alpha^\alpha(1-\alpha)^{1-\alpha} \frac{m} {p_x^\alpha p_y^{1-\alpha}} } \end{gathered}$$

Step 4 — Hicksian demand

The EmP is:

$$\begin{gathered}\min_{x,y\ge0} p_xx+p_yy\\[.4em]\text{subject to }x^\alpha y^{1-\alpha}\ge\bar u.\end{gathered}$$

At the optimum:

$$x^\alpha y^{1-\alpha}=\bar u.$$
Method 1 — Lagrangian
$$\mathcal L = p_xx+p_yy + \lambda \left[ \bar u-x^\alpha y^{1-\alpha} \right].$$

The first-order conditions are:

$$\begin{gathered} p_x=\lambda\alpha x^{\alpha-1}y^{1-\alpha},\\[0.65em] p_y=\lambda(1-\alpha)x^\alpha y^{-\alpha}. \end{gathered}$$

Divide the first condition by the second to cancel $\lambda$:

$$\begin{gathered} \frac{MU_x}{MU_y} = \frac{p_x}{p_y}.\\[0.65em] \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\frac{y}{x} = \frac{p_x}{p_y}.\\[0.65em] y = \frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha} \frac{p_x}{p_y}x. \end{gathered}$$

Substitute this into:

$$x^\alpha y^{1-\alpha}=\bar u.$$

Substitution and $\alpha+(1-\alpha)=1$ give:

$$\begin{gathered} x^\alpha\left(\frac{(1-\alpha)p_x}{\alpha p_y}x\right)^{1-\alpha}=\bar u,\\[0.65em] x\left(\frac{(1-\alpha)p_x}{\alpha p_y}\right)^{1-\alpha}=\bar u. \end{gathered}$$

Divide by the positive price factor to find $x^H$, then substitute into the expression for $y$:

$$\begin{gathered} \boxed{ x^H(p,\bar u) = \bar u \left( \frac{\alpha p_y} {(1-\alpha)p_x} \right)^{1-\alpha} }\\[0.65em] \boxed{ y^H(p,\bar u) = \bar u \left( \frac{(1-\alpha)p_x} {\alpha p_y} \right)^\alpha. } \end{gathered}$$
Method 2 — Optimality condition

Use directly:

$$MRS_{xy} = \frac{p_x}{p_y}$$

together with:

$$x^\alpha y^{1-\alpha}=\bar u.$$

This gives the same Hicksian demands.

Step 5 — Expenditure function

By definition:

$$e(p,\bar u) = p_xx^H+p_yy^H.$$

The optimal ratio implies $p_yy^H=\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}p_xx^H$. Therefore:

$$e(p,\bar u)=p_xx^H\left(1+\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\right)=\frac{p_xx^H}{\alpha}.$$

Substitute $x^H$ and collect the powers of $\alpha$, $1-\alpha$, $p_x$, and $p_y$:

$$\boxed{ e(p,\bar u) = \frac{ \bar u p_x^\alpha p_y^{1-\alpha} }{ \alpha^\alpha(1-\alpha)^{1-\alpha} } }$$

2. A utility function with a kink

$$u(x,y)=\max(\alpha x,\alpha y)+\min(x,y),\qquad 0<\alpha<1.$$
$$\begin{gathered}u(x,y)=\max(\alpha x,\alpha y)\\+\min(x,y),\qquad 0<\alpha<1.\end{gathered}$$

We follow the lecture's order: first read the two parts of the indifference curves, then compare the MRS with the price ratio in each region. We keep $m$ for income, corresponding to $I$ in the handwritten notes. The notation $v(p,m)$ denotes the utility obtained at the optimal bundle; $\bar u$ denotes a fixed utility target in the expenditure problem.

Step 1 — Indifference curves: $x<y$, $x>y$, then $x=y$

When $x<y$

Since $\alpha>0$, we also have $\alpha x<\alpha y$. The maximum term is therefore $\alpha y$, while the minimum term is $x$. Fixing utility at $\bar u$ gives:

$$\begin{aligned} u(x,y)&=\alpha y+x,\\[0.5em] \alpha y+x&=\bar u,\\[0.5em] \alpha y&=\bar u-x,\\[0.5em] y&=\frac{\bar u}{\alpha}-\frac{1}{\alpha}x. \end{aligned}$$

This part of the indifference curve is a straight line with slope $-1/\alpha$. The MRS is written as a positive number in the notes, so here $MRS=1/\alpha$. The slope itself is negative.

When $x>y$

The maximum term is now $\alpha x$, and the minimum term is $y$. On the same fixed-utility curve:

$$\begin{aligned} u(x,y)&=\alpha x+y,\\[0.5em] \alpha x+y&=\bar u,\\[0.5em] y&=\bar u-\alpha x. \end{aligned}$$

This part is a straight line with slope $-\alpha$, so $MRS=\alpha$. It is less steep than the first part because $0<\alpha<1$.

When $x=y$

Write the common quantity as $t$, so that $x=y=t$. Substitution into the utility function gives:

$$u(t,t)=\alpha t+t=(1+\alpha)t.$$

On a given indifference curve, this identifies the point where the two straight pieces meet. Their slopes differ, so the curve has a kink there: there is no single slope at that point. In the figure, the steeper part lies in $x<y$ and the flatter part in $x>y$.

Step 2 — Start in $x<y$: compare the price ratio with $1/\alpha$

In this region, $MRS=1/\alpha$. The MRS expresses how much $y$ the consumer is willing to give up for one more unit of $x$, holding utility constant. The price ratio $p_x/p_y$ expresses how much $y$ that unit of $x$ costs on the market. The lecture compares these two valuations.

If $\dfrac{p_x}{p_y}>\dfrac1\alpha$

The market price of $x$, relative to $y$, is higher than the consumer's valuation. Although the consumer already has less $x$ than $y$, they still prefer to reduce $x$ and obtain more $y$. This leads to $x^M=0$. Substituting into the budget constraint gives:

$$\begin{aligned} p_xx^M+p_yy^M&=m,\\[0.5em] p_y y^M&=m,\\[0.5em] \boxed{x^M=0,\qquad y^M=\frac{m}{p_y}.} \end{aligned}$$

Substitute these quantities into $u=\alpha y+x$ to obtain indirect utility:

$$v(p,m)=\alpha\frac{m}{p_y}+0=\boxed{\frac{\alpha m}{p_y}}.$$

The notes then solve this relation for the income needed to reach a given utility level $\bar u$. This gives the expenditure function in this price region:

$$\bar u=\frac{\alpha m}{p_y} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m=\frac{\bar u p_y}{\alpha} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{e(p,\bar u)=\frac{\bar u p_y}{\alpha}.}$$

Using Shephard's lemma as in the notes, differentiate expenditure with respect to each price, keeping $\bar u$ fixed. These derivatives give Hicksian demand: the quantities that attain the utility target at minimum expenditure.

$$\begin{aligned} x^H&=\frac{\partial e(p,\bar u)}{\partial p_x}=\boxed{0},\\[0.7em] y^H&=\frac{\partial e(p,\bar u)}{\partial p_y}=\boxed{\frac{\bar u}{\alpha}}. \end{aligned}$$
If $\dfrac{p_x}{p_y}<\dfrac1\alpha$

The consumer values $x$ more highly than the market does. Starting from $x<y$, they prefer to increase $x$ and reduce $y$, moving towards $x=y$. Reaching this point does not yet settle the final choice: the MRS changes on the other side, which is why the lecture next examines $x>y$.

Step 3 — Start in $x>y$: compare the price ratio with $\alpha$

In this region, $MRS=\alpha$. We again compare the consumer's valuation of $x$ with its relative market price.

If $\dfrac{p_x}{p_y}>\alpha$

The consumer values $x$ less highly than the market does. Starting with more $x$ than $y$, they prefer to reduce $x$ and increase $y$, moving towards $x=y$.

If $\dfrac{p_x}{p_y}<\alpha$

The consumer values $x$ more highly than the market does. Even though they already have more $x$ than $y$, they prefer to increase $x$ further, until $y^M=0$. The budget constraint then gives:

$$\begin{aligned} p_xx^M+p_y\times0&=m,\\[0.5em] \boxed{x^M=\frac{m}{p_x},\qquad y^M=0.} \end{aligned}$$

Substituting into $u=\alpha x+y$ gives:

$$v(p,m)=\alpha\frac{m}{p_x}+0=\boxed{\frac{\alpha m}{p_x}}.$$

As before, solve for the income required to reach $\bar u$:

$$\bar u=\frac{\alpha m}{p_x} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m=\frac{\bar u p_x}{\alpha} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{e(p,\bar u)=\frac{\bar u p_x}{\alpha}.}$$

Shephard's lemma then gives:

$$\begin{aligned} y^H&=\frac{\partial e(p,\bar u)}{\partial p_y}=\boxed{0},\\[0.7em] x^H&=\frac{\partial e(p,\bar u)}{\partial p_x}=\boxed{\frac{\bar u}{\alpha}}. \end{aligned}$$

Step 4 — Bring the price regions together

The price line in the notes brings together the preceding comparisons. Below $\alpha$, the consumer chooses only $x$. Above $1/\alpha$, they choose only $y$. Between these thresholds, both directions lead towards equal quantities: from $x<y$ the consumer wants more $x$, and from $x>y$ they want less $x$. The optimum is therefore at $x=y$.

If $p_x/p_y<\alpha$:

$$x^M=\frac{m}{p_x},\qquad y^M=0.$$

If $\alpha<p_x/p_y<1/\alpha$:

$$x^M=y^M.$$

If $p_x/p_y>1/\alpha$:

$$x^M=0,\qquad y^M=\frac{m}{p_y}.$$

The interval $[\alpha,1/\alpha]$ marked in the notes is bounded by the MRS values on the two sides of the kink. It does not give a unique slope at the kink.

Step 5 — Calculate the equal-quantity choice

For $\alpha<p_x/p_y<1/\alpha$, write $x^M=y^M=t^M$. Since the consumer buys the same quantity of each good, the budget constraint becomes:

$$\begin{aligned} p_xt^M+p_yt^M&=m,\\[0.5em] (p_x+p_y)t^M&=m,\\[0.5em] \boxed{t^M=x^M=y^M=\frac{m}{p_x+p_y}.} \end{aligned}$$

At equal quantities, utility is $(1+\alpha)t$. Substituting the optimal common quantity gives:

$$v(p,m)=(1+\alpha)\frac{m}{p_x+p_y}=\boxed{\frac{(1+\alpha)m}{p_x+p_y}}.$$

Solving the same relation for the income needed to reach $\bar u$ gives:

$$\bar u=\frac{(1+\alpha)m}{p_x+p_y} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{e(p,\bar u)=\frac{\bar u(p_x+p_y)}{1+\alpha}.}$$

Apply Shephard's lemma with respect to each price:

$$\begin{aligned} x^H&=\frac{\partial e(p,\bar u)}{\partial p_x}=\frac{\bar u}{1+\alpha},\\[0.7em] y^H&=\frac{\partial e(p,\bar u)}{\partial p_y}=\frac{\bar u}{1+\alpha}. \end{aligned}$$

Thus the Hicksian quantities are also equal, with common quantity $t^H=\bar u/(1+\alpha)$, as in the notes.

Step 6 — The two equality cases discussed at the end of the lecture

The exercise initially excludes the two threshold price ratios. The handwritten correction ends by discussing them separately.

If $p_x/p_y=\alpha$, the price ratio equals the MRS on the $x>y$ side. Every bundle on the budget line with $x\ge y$ is optimal.

If $p_x/p_y=1/\alpha$, the price ratio equals the MRS on the $x<y$ side. Every bundle on the budget line with $x\le y$ is optimal.

Here, “all feasible bundles” in the notes refers to the relevant bundles satisfying $p_xx+p_yy=m$, rather than bundles that leave some income unspent.

3. Logarithmic utility

$$u(x,y)=\log x+\beta\log y, \qquad 0<\beta<1.$$
$$\begin{gathered}u(x,y)=\log x+\beta\log y\\0<\beta<1.\end{gathered}$$

Step 1 — Hint — the trick

Use:

$$\log x+\beta\log y = \log(xy^\beta).$$

Since the logarithm is strictly increasing, maximizing:

$$\log(xy^\beta)$$

gives the same choice as maximizing:

$$xy^\beta.$$

Step 2 — Indifference curves

Fix utility at $\bar u$:

$$\begin{gathered} \log x+\beta\log y=\bar u.\\[0.65em] \log(xy^\beta)=\bar u. \end{gathered}$$

Exponentiating:

$$\begin{gathered} xy^\beta=e^{\bar u}.\\[0.65em] \boxed{ y= e^{\bar u/\beta}x^{-1/\beta}. } \end{gathered}$$

Step 3 — Marshallian demand

The UMP is:

$$\begin{gathered}\max_{x,y>0} \log x+\beta\log y\\[.4em]\text{subject to }p_xx+p_yy=m.\end{gathered}$$
Method 1 — Lagrangian
$$\mathcal L = \log x+\beta\log y + \lambda(m-p_xx-p_yy).$$

FOCs:

$$\begin{gathered} \frac1x = \lambda p_x\\[0.65em] \frac{\beta}{y} = \lambda p_y\\[0.65em] p_xx+p_yy=m. \end{gathered}$$

Divide the first FOC by the second:

$$\begin{gathered} \frac{y}{\beta x} = \frac{p_x}{p_y}.\\[0.65em] y = \beta \frac{p_x}{p_y}x. \end{gathered}$$

Substitute into the budget constraint:

$$\begin{gathered} p_xx + p_y \left( \beta \frac{p_x}{p_y}x \right) = m.\\[0.65em] (1+\beta)p_xx=m.\\[0.65em] \boxed{ x^M(p,m) = \frac{m}{(1+\beta)p_x} } \end{gathered}$$

and:

$$\boxed{ y^M(p,m) = \frac{\beta m}{(1+\beta)p_y}. }$$
Method 2 — Optimality condition

Marginal utilities are:

$$\begin{gathered} MU_x=\frac1x\\[0.65em] MU_y=\frac{\beta}{y}.\\[0.65em] MRS_{xy} = \frac{MU_x}{MU_y} = \frac{y}{\beta x}. \end{gathered}$$

At an interior optimum:

$$\frac{y}{\beta x} = \frac{p_x}{p_y}.$$

Combine this with:

$$p_xx+p_yy=m.$$

This gives again:

$$\boxed{ x^M= \frac{m}{(1+\beta)p_x}, \qquad y^M= \frac{\beta m}{(1+\beta)p_y}. }$$

Step 4 — Indirect utility function

Substitute the Marshallian demand:

$$v(p,m) = \log \left( \frac{m}{(1+\beta)p_x} \right) + \beta \log \left( \frac{\beta m}{(1+\beta)p_y} \right).$$
$$\begin{aligned}v(p,m)&= \log \left( \frac{m}{(1+\beta)p_x} \right)\\&+\beta \log \left( \frac{\beta m}{(1+\beta)p_y} \right).\end{aligned}$$

Therefore:

$$\boxed{ v(p,m) = \log \left( \frac{m}{(1+\beta)p_x} \right) + \beta \log \left( \frac{\beta m}{(1+\beta)p_y} \right) }$$
$$\begin{aligned}v(p,m)&= \log \left( \frac{m}{(1+\beta)p_x} \right)\\&+\beta \log \left( \frac{\beta m}{(1+\beta)p_y} \right)\end{aligned}$$

Step 5 — Hicksian demand

The EmP is:

$$\begin{gathered}\min_{x,y>0}p_xx+p_yy\\ \text{subject to }\log x+\beta\log y\geq\bar u.\end{gathered}$$

At the optimum:

$$\log x+\beta\log y=\bar u.$$
Method 1 — Lagrangian
$$\mathcal L=p_xx+p_yy+\lambda[\bar u-\log x-\beta\log y].$$

Differentiating with respect to each quantity gives:

$$p_x=\frac{\lambda}{x},\qquad p_y=\frac{\lambda\beta}{y}.$$

Divide these conditions to cancel $\lambda$:

$$\begin{gathered} \frac{MU_x}{MU_y} = \frac{p_x}{p_y}.\\[0.65em] \frac{y}{\beta x} = \frac{p_x}{p_y}, \end{gathered}$$

so:

$$y = \beta \frac{p_x}{p_y}x.$$

Using:

$$xy^\beta=e^{\bar u},$$

substitute the expression for $y$:

$$x\left(\beta\frac{p_x}{p_y}x\right)^\beta=e^{\bar u}.$$

Collect the powers of $x$, then isolate $x^{1+\beta}$:

$$x^{1+\beta}=e^{\bar u}\left(\frac{p_y}{\beta p_x}\right)^\beta.$$

Raise both sides to the power $1/(1+\beta)$ to obtain $x^H$. Substitute it into $y=\beta(p_x/p_y)x$ to obtain $y^H$:

$$\begin{gathered} \boxed{ x^H(p,\bar u) = e^{\frac{\bar u}{1+\beta}} \left( \frac{p_y}{\beta p_x} \right)^{\frac{\beta}{1+\beta}} }\\[0.65em] \boxed{ y^H(p,\bar u) = e^{\frac{\bar u}{1+\beta}} \left( \frac{\beta p_x}{p_y} \right)^{\frac1{1+\beta}}. } \end{gathered}$$
Method 2 — Optimality condition

Use:

$$MRS_{xy} = \frac{p_x}{p_y}$$

together with:

$$\log x+\beta\log y=\bar u.$$

This gives the same Hicksian demand.

Step 6 — Expenditure function

Substitute the Hicksian demands into:

$$e(p,\bar u) = p_xx^H+p_yy^H.$$

Since $p_yy^H=\beta p_xx^H$, combine the expenditure terms:

$$e(p,\bar u)=(1+\beta)p_xx^H.$$

Substitute $x^H$. The power of $p_x$ becomes $1-\beta/(1+\beta)=1/(1+\beta)$:

$$\boxed{ e(p,\bar u) = (1+\beta) \beta^{-\frac{\beta}{1+\beta}} e^{\frac{\bar u}{1+\beta}} p_x^{\frac1{1+\beta}} p_y^{\frac{\beta}{1+\beta}} }$$
Exercise 2

Exercise 2. Uniqueness

A consumer has utility $u(x_1,x_2)=2x_1+x_2$, income $y>0$, and faces prices $p_1,p_2>0$.

  1. Find the Marshallian demand in each price case:
    $$\frac{p_1}{p_2}<2,\qquad \frac{p_1}{p_2}>2,\qquad \frac{p_1}{p_2}=2.$$
  2. Using a counter-example, show that these preferences are not strictly convex. Relate the result to the uniqueness, or non-uniqueness, of Marshallian demand.

Starting point. Utility is linear, income is positive, and both prices are positive. Goal. Find the optimal bundle in each price case, then test strict convexity using the requested counterexample.

Question 1 — Marshallian demand

Utility is:

$$u(x_1,x_2)=2x_1+x_2.$$

The marginal utilities are:

$$MU_1=2, \qquad MU_2=1.$$

Compare marginal utility per unit of expenditure:

$$\frac{MU_1}{p_1} = \frac2{p_1}, \qquad \frac{MU_2}{p_2} = \frac1{p_2}.$$
Case 1

If $p_1/p_2<2$, then:

$$\frac2{p_1} > \frac1{p_2}.$$

Good 1 gives more utility per unit of expenditure.

The consumer spends all income on good 1:

$$\boxed{ x_1^M=\frac{y}{p_1}, \qquad x_2^M=0. }$$
Case 2

If $\frac{p_1}{p_2}>2$, then:

$$\frac2{p_1} < \frac1{p_2}.$$

The consumer spends all income on good 2:

$$\boxed{ x_1^M=0, \qquad x_2^M=\frac{y}{p_2}. }$$
Case 3

If $\frac{p_1}{p_2}=2$, then:

$$\frac2{p_1} = \frac1{p_2}.$$

Every bundle on the budget line gives the same utility per unit of expenditure.

Therefore all bundles satisfying:

$$p_1x_1+p_2x_2=y$$

are optimal.

Thus:

Final result. Marshallian demand is the entire set of nonnegative bundles on the budget line:

$$\begin{gathered} p_1x_1+p_2x_2=y,\qquad x_1,x_2\geq0.\\[0.65em] \mathcal L = 2x_1+x_2 + \lambda(y-p_1x_1-p_2x_2). \end{gathered}$$

For an interior solution:

$$\begin{gathered} 2=\lambda p_1\\[0.65em] 1=\lambda p_2. \end{gathered}$$

Dividing gives:

$$\frac{p_1}{p_2}=2.$$

Therefore the equality case is precisely the case in which an interior combination can satisfy both FOCs.

For the other price ratios, the optimum is a corner.

Question 2 — Strict convexity and uniqueness

Take:

$$\begin{gathered} a=(1,0)\\[0.65em] b=(0,2). \end{gathered}$$

Their utilities are:

$$\begin{gathered} u(a)=2\\[0.65em] u(b)=2.\\[0.65em] a\sim b. \end{gathered}$$

Their equal-weight mixture is:

$$\frac12a+\frac12b = \left(\frac12,1\right).$$

Its utility is:

$$\begin{gathered} u\left(\frac12,1\right) = 2\left(\frac12\right)+1 = 2.\\[0.65em] u\left(\frac12a+\frac12b\right) = u(a) = u(b). \end{gathered}$$

But strict convexity would require the true mixture of two distinct indifferent bundles to be strictly preferred to them.

That does not happen here.

Hence:

$$\boxed{ \text{preferences are not strictly convex.} }$$

When:

$$\frac{p_1}{p_2}=2,$$

there are multiple optimal bundles on the budget line, so Marshallian demand is not single-valued.

Exercise 3. Example of a demand system

Consider the demand system $x(p,y)$ defined by:

$$\begin{aligned} x_1(p,y)&=\frac{p_2}{p_1+p_2+p_3}\frac{y}{p_1},\\[0.45em] x_2(p,y)&=\frac{p_3}{p_1+p_2+p_3}\frac{y}{p_2},\\[0.45em] x_3(p,y)&=\frac{\beta p_1}{p_1+p_2+p_3}\frac{y}{p_3}. \end{aligned}$$
  1. Does this demand function satisfy homogeneity of degree zero?
  2. Does this demand function satisfy Walras’ Law?

Starting point. Keep $\beta$ fixed and use positive prices, as in the chapter. Goal. Test the two stated identities. For Walras’ Law, check positive income; at zero income both expenditure and income are zero.

Question 1 — Homogeneity of degree zero

The course defines homogeneity of degree zero by:

$$x(\lambda p,\lambda y)=x(p,y)$$

for every:

$$\lambda>0.$$

Check the first demand:

$$x_1(\lambda p,\lambda y) = \frac{\lambda p_2} {\lambda p_1+\lambda p_2+\lambda p_3} \frac{\lambda y}{\lambda p_1}.$$

Factor and cancel $\lambda$:

$$\begin{gathered} x_1(\lambda p,\lambda y) = \frac{p_2} {p_1+p_2+p_3} \frac{y}{p_1}.\\[0.65em] x_1(\lambda p,\lambda y)=x_1(p,y). \end{gathered}$$

The same cancellation applies to $x_2$ and $x_3$.

Therefore:

$$\boxed{ x(\lambda p,\lambda y)=x(p,y) }$$

and the demand system satisfies:

$$\boxed{\text{homogeneity of degree zero}.}$$

Question 2 — Walras’ Law

The course defines Walras’ Law by:

$$p\cdot x=y.$$

Compute total expenditure:

$$p_1x_1+p_2x_2+p_3x_3.$$

First:

$$p_1x_1 = \frac{p_2}{p_1+p_2+p_3}y.$$

Second:

$$p_2x_2 = \frac{p_3}{p_1+p_2+p_3}y.$$

Third:

$$p_3x_3 = \frac{\beta p_1}{p_1+p_2+p_3}y.$$

Add them:

$$p\cdot x = \frac{ p_2+p_3+\beta p_1 }{ p_1+p_2+p_3 }y.$$

For Walras’ Law to hold, this must equal $y$.

Hence:

$$\begin{gathered} p_2+p_3+\beta p_1 = p_1+p_2+p_3.\\[0.65em] \beta p_1=p_1. \end{gathered}$$

Since $p_1>0$, division by $p_1$ gives:

$$\beta=1.$$

Thus:

Final result. The system satisfies Walras’ Law if and only if $\beta=1$: this value makes the numerator equal to the denominator, so total expenditure equals income.

$$\boxed{\beta=1.}$$
