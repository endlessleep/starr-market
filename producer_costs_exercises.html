Costs, input choices, and profit maximization

Exercise 1

Exercise 1. Economies of Scale

Consider a firm with total cost

$$TC=5+2y+y^3.$$

Find the average cost curve ($AC(y)$) and its minimum point. Interpret your results in terms of economies of scale.

Economies of scale — Correction

1. Average cost

For positive output $y>0$, average cost is total cost divided by output:

$$\begin{aligned} AC(y)&=\frac{TC(y)}{y}\\ &=\frac{5+2y+y^3}{y}. \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{AC(y)=\frac5y+2+y^2}$$

2. Find the minimizing output

Differentiate average cost with respect to output and set the derivative equal to zero:

$$\frac{dAC(y)}{dy}=-\frac5{y^2}+2y.$$
$$\begin{aligned} -\frac5{y^2}+2y&=0\\ 2y&=\frac5{y^2}\\ 2y^3&=5\\ y^3&=\frac52. \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{y^*=\left(\frac52\right)^{1/3}}$$

This is the output level that minimizes average cost. It is the unique minimum because:

$$AC''(y)=\frac{10}{y^3}+2>0\qquad(y>0).$$

3. Minimum average cost

Substitute the minimizing output into the average cost function:

$$\begin{aligned} AC_{\min}&=\frac5{y^*}+2+(y^*)^2\\ &=2+5\left(\frac25\right)^{1/3} +\left(\frac52\right)^{2/3}. \end{aligned}$$

Since $5=2(y^*)^3$, we also have $5/y^*=2(y^*)^2$. Therefore:

$$\boxed{AC_{\min}=2+3\left(\frac52\right)^{2/3}\approx7.53}$$

This is approximately $7.5$ to one decimal place. $AC_{\min}$ is the lowest average cost per unit of output; $y^*$ is the quantity produced at that cost. The minimum point on the average cost curve is $(y^*,AC_{\min})$.

4. Economic interpretation

The sign of the slope is particularly clear when written as:

$$AC'(y)=\frac{2y^3-5}{y^2}.$$
  • If $0<y<y^*$: economies of scale. Average cost falls as output increases.
  • If $y=y^*$: minimum average cost. The firm is at the bottom of its average cost curve, where $AC=MC$.
  • If $y>y^*$: diseconomies of scale. Average cost rises as output increases.

Indeed, $MC(y)=TC'(y)=2+3y^2$, so $MC(y^*)=2+3(y^*)^2=AC_{\min}$.

Zero-profit price threshold. Under competitive pricing, the break-even price is $p=\min AC$. At $y^*$, profit is $\pi=[p-AC_{\min}]y^*$, so the firm earns zero profit if the market price equals minimum average cost. Minimizing average cost alone does not imply zero profit.
Exercise 2

Exercise 2. Profit Maximization and Cost Minimization

Consider a firm with the Cobb-Douglas production function:

$$f(K,L)=4K^{1/2}L^{1/3}$$

where $K$ denotes units of capital and $L$ represents units of labor. The input prices are:

$$w_K=10,\qquad w_L=7.$$

The firm sells every unit of output at a price $p>0$.

  1. Solve the firm's cost-minimization problem to find the input demand functions.
  2. Use your results from Question 1 to find the firm's cost function. This is its long-run total cost, as all inputs can be altered.
  3. Find the firm's marginal cost function and its average cost function. Interpret.
  4. Assume that capital is fixed at $\bar K=3$. Find the level of labor $L$ needed to produce an output level $\bar y$.
  5. Using profit maximization, find the level of labor $L$ that maximizes profit.

Question 1 — Conditional input demands

1. Cost minimization and the Lagrangian

For a positive output target $\bar y$, both inputs must be positive. Since inputs are costly, the firm produces exactly the target:

$$\begin{aligned} &\min_{K,L\geq0}\;10K+7L\\ &\text{s.t.}\quad\bar y=4K^{1/2}L^{1/3}. \end{aligned}$$
$$\mathcal L=10K+7L+\lambda(\bar y-4K^{1/2}L^{1/3}).$$

The first-order conditions are:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial K} &=10-2\lambda K^{-1/2}L^{1/3}=0,\\ \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial L} &=7-\frac43\lambda K^{1/2}L^{-2/3}=0,\\ \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\lambda} &=\bar y-4K^{1/2}L^{1/3}=0. \end{aligned}$$

2. Find the cost-minimizing input ratio

Divide the first two conditions to eliminate $\lambda$:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{10}{7} &=\frac{2K^{-1/2}L^{1/3}}{\frac43K^{1/2}L^{-2/3}}\\ &=\frac32\frac{L}{K}. \end{aligned}$$
$$20K=21L \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{K=\frac{21}{20}L}.$$

At this ratio, the marginal product per unit of input expenditure is the same for capital and labor.

3. Substitute into the production constraint

$$\begin{aligned} \bar y&=4\left(\frac{21}{20}L\right)^{1/2}L^{1/3}\\ &=4\left(\frac{21}{20}\right)^{1/2}L^{5/6},\\ L^{5/6}&=\frac{\bar y}{4}\left(\frac{20}{21}\right)^{1/2}. \end{aligned}$$

Raise both sides to the power $6/5$. For capital, use $K=(21/20)L$ and simplify its price factor:

$$\frac{21}{20}\left(\frac{20}{21}\right)^{3/5} =\left(\frac{21}{20}\right)^{1-3/5} =\left(\frac{21}{20}\right)^{2/5}.$$

Conditional input demands

With input prices fixed at $w=(10,7)$, the final demands depend only on the output target $\bar y$:

$$\boxed{ L^d(w,\bar y)= \left(\frac{\bar y}{4}\right)^{6/5} \left(\frac{20}{21}\right)^{3/5} }$$
$$\boxed{ K^d(w,\bar y)= \left(\frac{\bar y}{4}\right)^{6/5} \left(\frac{21}{20}\right)^{2/5} }$$

Question 2 — Long-run total cost

Multiply each conditional input demand by its price and add:

$$C(\bar y)=10K^d(\bar y)+7L^d(\bar y).$$

Both demands contain $(\bar y/4)^{6/5}$. Factoring it out gives:

$$ C(\bar y)= \left[ 10\left(\frac{21}{20}\right)^{2/5} +7\left(\frac{20}{21}\right)^{3/5} \right] \left(\frac{\bar y}{4}\right)^{6/5} .$$

Evaluate the numerical coefficient on a calculator:

$$\frac{ 10(21/20)^{2/5}+7(20/21)^{3/5} }{4^{6/5}}=3.21997485\ldots$$
$$\boxed{C(\bar y)\approx3.220\,\bar y^{6/5}}$$

This is the long-run total cost function: both capital and labor can be adjusted to minimize the cost of producing each output target.

Numerical coefficients in Questions 2–5 are rounded to four significant figures; calculations use unrounded values.

Question 3 — Marginal and average cost

1. Write cost as a power of output

Using the numerical coefficient from Question 2 and $y$ for output:

$$C(y)\approx3.220\,y^{6/5}.$$

2. Differentiate for marginal cost

$$\begin{aligned} MC(y)&=\frac{dC(y)}{dy}\\ &\approx3.220\times\frac65\,y^{6/5-1}. \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{MC(y)\approx3.864\,y^{1/5}}$$

3. Divide by output for average cost

$$\begin{aligned} AC(y)&=\frac{C(y)}{y}\\ &\approx3.220\,\frac{y^{6/5}}y. \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{AC(y)\approx3.220\,y^{1/5}}\qquad(y>0).$$

Interpretation

Both costs increase with output because they are positive multiples of $y^{1/5}$. In particular:

$$\boxed{MC(y)=\frac65AC(y)>AC(y)}\qquad(y>0).$$

The firm faces diseconomies of scale. This is consistent with decreasing returns to scale in production, since the Cobb-Douglas exponents sum to $1/2+1/3=5/6<1$.

Question 4 — Short-run labor demand

With capital fixed at $\bar K=3$, the production constraint is:

$$\bar y=4\sqrt3\,L^{1/3}.$$

Divide by $4\sqrt3$ and cube both sides:

$$\begin{aligned} L^{1/3}&=\frac{\bar y}{4\sqrt3},\\ L^d(\bar y)&=\frac{1}{(4\sqrt3)^3}\,\bar y^3. \end{aligned}$$

The coefficient is $1/(4\sqrt3)^3=0.00300703265\ldots$, so:

$$\boxed{L^d(\bar y)\approx0.003007\,\bar y^3}$$

Labor is the variable input needed to reach the output target; capital remains fixed.

Question 5 — Short-run profit maximization

1. Write profit and differentiate

Capital is still fixed at $\bar K=3$, so its cost is $10\times3=30$:

$$\max_{L\geq0}\;\pi(L)=4p\sqrt3\,L^{1/3}-7L-30.$$

The first-order condition equates the value of labor's marginal product to its price:

$$\pi'(L)=\frac{4p\sqrt3}{3}L^{-2/3}-7=0.$$

2. Solve for optimal labor

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{4p\sqrt3}{3}L^{-2/3}&=7\\ L^{2/3}&=\frac{4\sqrt3}{21}\,p \approx0.3299\,p. \end{aligned}$$

Raise both sides to the power $3/2$ and evaluate the coefficient:

$$L^*=\left(\frac{4\sqrt3}{21}\right)^{3/2}p^{3/2}.$$
$$\boxed{L^*\approx0.1895\,p^{3/2}}$$

For $p>0$, profit is strictly concave for positive labor:

$$\pi''(L)=-\frac{8p\sqrt3}{9}L^{-5/3}<0.$$

Thus this positive solution is the unique maximum. The fixed capital cost does not affect the optimal labor choice: the constant $-30$ disappears when profit is differentiated.

Exercise 3

Exercise 3. Perfect Substitutes and Complements

Derive the profit function $\pi(p)$ and the optimal quantity of output $q$ and inputs $(z_1,z_2)$ for single-output firms with the following production functions.

  1. Perfect Substitutes
    $$f(z_1,z_2)=2\sqrt{z_1+z_2}.$$

    Hint: The inputs are perfect substitutes.

  2. Perfect Complements
    $$f(z_1,z_2)=2\sqrt{\min(z_1,z_2)}.$$

    Hint: The inputs are perfect complements.

  3. CES Production Function
    $$f(z_1,z_2)=(z_1^\rho+z_2^\rho)^{1/\rho}.$$

    Use $0<\rho<1$ for the differentiable interior case. Discuss $\rho=1$ separately. The displayed expression is not defined at $\rho=0$.

Throughout this exercise, take $p>0$ and $w_1,w_2>0$. Input prices are held fixed when writing the profit function $\pi(p)$.

Question 1 — Perfect Substitutes

1. Optimization program

Production depends only on the sum of the inputs. Replacing one unit of either input with one unit of the other leaves output unchanged:

$$\max_{z_1,z_2\geq0}\; \pi=2p\sqrt{z_1+z_2}-w_1z_1-w_2z_2.$$

For a given total input quantity, the firm therefore chooses the cheaper input.

2. Marginal products and interior FOCs

$$MP_1=MP_2=\frac1{\sqrt{z_1+z_2}}.$$

If both inputs are positive, the first-order conditions require:

$$pMP_1=w_1,\qquad pMP_2=w_2.$$

Since the marginal products are equal, both conditions can hold simultaneously only when $w_1=w_2$.

3A. Input 1 is cheaper: $w_1<w_2$

Set $z_2^*=0$. The reduced problem and its first-order condition are:

$$\begin{aligned} &\max_{z_1\geq0}\;2p\sqrt{z_1}-w_1z_1,\\ &\frac p{\sqrt{z_1}}-w_1=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sqrt{z_1}=\frac p{w_1}. \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{z_1^*=\left(\frac p{w_1}\right)^2,\qquad z_2^*=0}$$

Substitute into production and profit:

$$q^*=2\sqrt{z_1^*}=\boxed{\frac{2p}{w_1}}.$$
$$\begin{aligned} \pi(p)&=p\frac{2p}{w_1}-w_1\left(\frac p{w_1}\right)^2\\ &=\frac{2p^2}{w_1}-\frac{p^2}{w_1} =\boxed{\frac{p^2}{w_1}}. \end{aligned}$$

3B. Input 2 is cheaper: $w_2<w_1$

The same calculation applies with the inputs reversed. Set $z_1^*=0$ and solve $p/\sqrt{z_2}=w_2$:

$$\boxed{z_1^*=0,\qquad z_2^*=\left(\frac p{w_2}\right)^2}$$
$$\boxed{q^*=\frac{2p}{w_2}},\qquad \pi(p)=\frac{2p^2}{w_2}-\frac{p^2}{w_2} =\boxed{\frac{p^2}{w_2}}.$$

3C. Equal input prices: $w_1=w_2=w$

Let $s=z_1+z_2$. Profit is $2p\sqrt s-ws$, independently of how the total input is split. The first-order condition gives:

$$\frac p{\sqrt s}=w \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{z_1^*+z_2^*=\left(\frac pw\right)^2}.$$

Any nonnegative combination satisfying this condition is optimal.

$$\boxed{q^*=\frac{2p}{w},\qquad\pi(p)=\frac{p^2}{w}}$$
Comparison. Unequal prices give a unique choice of the cheaper input; equal prices allow any split of the optimal total. In all three cases, output and profit depend on the lowest input price. The reduced objective is strictly concave in total input, since its second derivative is $-p/(2s^{3/2})<0$, so the optimal total is unique.

Question 2 — Perfect Complements

1. Fixed input proportions

In $f(z_1,z_2)=2\sqrt{\min(z_1,z_2)}$, the smaller input determines output. If $z_1>z_2$, the excess of input 1 adds cost without increasing production; the same argument applies to excess input 2. With positive input prices:

$$\boxed{z_1=z_2=z}.$$

2. Reduced optimization problem

$$\max_{z\geq0}\;\pi(z)=2p\sqrt z-(w_1+w_2)z.$$

3. Solve the first-order condition

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{d\pi}{dz}&=\frac p{\sqrt z}-(w_1+w_2)=0,\\ \sqrt z&=\frac p{w_1+w_2}. \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{z_1^*=z_2^*=\left(\frac p{w_1+w_2}\right)^2}$$

This is the unique maximum because $\pi''(z)=-p/(2z^{3/2})<0$ for $z>0$.

4. Optimal output

$$q^*=2\sqrt{z^*}=\boxed{\frac{2p}{w_1+w_2}}.$$

5. Optimal profit

Subtract the cost of both inputs from revenue:

$$\begin{aligned} \pi(p)&=pq^*-w_1z_1^*-w_2z_2^*\\ &=\frac{2p^2}{w_1+w_2} -(w_1+w_2)\left(\frac p{w_1+w_2}\right)^2\\ &=\frac{2p^2}{w_1+w_2}-\frac{p^2}{w_1+w_2}. \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\pi(p)=\frac{p^2}{w_1+w_2}}$$

Perfect complements must be used together in fixed proportions. Unlike perfect substitutes, extra units of one input cannot replace missing units of the other.

Question 3 — CES Production

1. Optimization program

First consider $0<\rho<1$:

$$\max_{z_1,z_2\geq0}\; \pi=p(z_1^\rho+z_2^\rho)^{1/\rho}-w_1z_1-w_2z_2.$$

2. First-order conditions

At positive input quantities, apply the chain rule. For input 1:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial f}{\partial z_1} &=\frac1\rho(z_1^\rho+z_2^\rho)^{1/\rho-1} \cdot\rho z_1^{\rho-1}\\ &=z_1^{\rho-1}(z_1^\rho+z_2^\rho)^{(1-\rho)/\rho}. \end{aligned}$$

The factors $1/\rho$ and $\rho$ cancel. Applying the same rule to input 2 gives the interior profit FOCs:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\pi}{\partial z_1} &=p z_1^{\rho-1}(z_1^\rho+z_2^\rho)^{(1-\rho)/\rho}-w_1=0,\\ \frac{\partial\pi}{\partial z_2} &=p z_2^{\rho-1}(z_1^\rho+z_2^\rho)^{(1-\rho)/\rho}-w_2=0. \end{aligned}$$

3. Optimal input proportions

Divide the two conditions to cancel the common factors:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{w_1}{w_2}&=\left(\frac{z_1}{z_2}\right)^{\rho-1},\\ \frac{z_1}{z_2} &=\left(\frac{w_1}{w_2}\right)^{1/(\rho-1)} =\left(\frac{w_2}{w_1}\right)^{1/(1-\rho)}. \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{x=\left(\frac{w_2}{w_1}\right)^{1/(1-\rho)}}, \qquad\boxed{z_1=xz_2}.$$

This determines the optimal proportions, not the production scale. The same ratio follows from cost minimization, where $MP_1/MP_2=w_1/w_2$. For positive output and $0<\rho<1$, both inputs are used: the marginal product of an absent input becomes arbitrarily large as its quantity approaches zero.

4. Conditional input demands

For a given output target $\bar q>0$, substitute $z_1=xz_2$ into the production constraint:

$$\begin{aligned} \bar q&=(z_1^\rho+z_2^\rho)^{1/\rho}\\ &=\bigl[(xz_2)^\rho+z_2^\rho\bigr]^{1/\rho}\\ &=\bigl[(1+x^\rho)z_2^\rho\bigr]^{1/\rho}\\ &=(1+x^\rho)^{1/\rho}z_2. \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{z_2^c=\frac{\bar q}{(1+x^\rho)^{1/\rho}}}$$
$$\boxed{z_1^c=\frac{x\bar q}{(1+x^\rho)^{1/\rho}}}$$
These are conditional cost-minimizing input demands for a specified output target. They also give zero inputs when $\bar q=0$. Profit maximization still requires choosing the output level.

5. Cost function and unit cost

Substitute the conditional demands into total input expenditure:

$$\begin{aligned} C(w,\bar q)&=w_1z_1^c+w_2z_2^c\\ &=\frac{w_1x\bar q+w_2\bar q}{(1+x^\rho)^{1/\rho}}. \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{C(w,\bar q)=\frac{w_1x+w_2}{(1+x^\rho)^{1/\rho}}\bar q}$$

Define the cost of one unit of output:

$$\boxed{c(w)=\frac{w_1x+w_2}{(1+x^\rho)^{1/\rho}}}, \qquad\boxed{C(w,\bar q)=c(w)\bar q}.$$

6. Profit maximization under constant returns to scale

Multiplying both inputs by $t>0$ multiplies output by $t$:

$$\begin{aligned} f(tz_1,tz_2) &=\bigl[t^\rho(z_1^\rho+z_2^\rho)\bigr]^{1/\rho}\\ &=t f(z_1,z_2). \end{aligned}$$

The function is homogeneous of degree 1. Unit cost is therefore independent of scale, and profit maximization reduces to:

$$\max_{q\geq0}\,[pq-c(w)q] =\max_{q\geq0}\,[p-c(w)]q.$$

Case A: $p<c(w)$

Every positive unit of output generates a loss. The firm chooses zero production:

$$\boxed{q^*=0,\quad z_1^*=z_2^*=0,\quad\pi(p)=0}.$$

Case B: $p=c(w)$

Revenue exactly covers cost at every scale, provided the firm uses cost-minimizing input proportions:

$$\boxed{\pi(p)=0}.$$

Any $q\geq0$ is optimal, with $z_1^*$ and $z_2^*$ given by the conditional demands evaluated at that chosen $q$.

Case C: $p>c(w)$

Each additional unit yields the same positive margin. Because the technology has constant returns to scale, expansion does not raise unit cost:

$$\boxed{\sup\pi=+\infty}.$$

There is no finite profit-maximizing output or input bundle. Along the cost-minimizing input ratio, output, both inputs, and profit grow without bound.

Why the FOCs do not determine a unique scale. A finite positive profit-maximizing bundle can satisfy the interior FOCs only at $p=c(w)$. Below unit cost, the optimum is zero; above it, profit is unbounded.

7. Boundary case: $\rho=1$

$$f(z_1,z_2)=z_1+z_2.$$

The inputs are perfect substitutes. Cost minimization uses only the cheaper input, or any split when prices are equal, so unit cost is $c(w)=\min(w_1,w_2)$.

The same three price cases apply: below this unit cost the firm produces zero; at equality any scale is optimal using the cheapest input(s); above it profit is unbounded. Unlike Question 1's square-root technology, this linear technology has constant returns to scale.

Do not use the interior CES ratio formula at $\rho=1$, because $1/(1-\rho)$ is undefined. The displayed CES production function is also not defined at $\rho=0$; direct substitution is invalid.
