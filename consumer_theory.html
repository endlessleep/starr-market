Consumer Theory

Consumer’s preferences, utility maximization, and expenditure minimization

Utility Function

Under the preceding assumptions on preferences, the consumer’s ranking of bundles can be represented by a utility function $u(x)$. The function assigns a number to each bundle so that a preferred bundle receives a higher number. We now use this representation to describe preferences and the trade-offs between goods.

How to read the three properties of $u$

Strictly increasing
A bundle with at least as much of every good and strictly more of at least one good has higher utility. This expresses strict monotonicity in terms of the utility function.
Strictly quasi-concave
For two distinct bundles, a genuine mixture has strictly higher utility than the lower of their two utility levels. This is how the utility function expresses strictly convex preferences.
Unique up to a strictly increasing transformation
The utility numbers themselves are not unique. A strictly increasing transformation changes those numbers while preserving their order, so it represents the same preferences.

A transformation $f$ is strictly increasing if:

$$u_1>u_2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad f(u_1)>f(u_2).$$

Thus, replacing $u(x)$ by $f(u(x))$ preserves which bundle is ranked above which. It changes the numerical representation, not the consumer’s ranking.

Marginal Rate of Substitution

The consumer’s willingness to exchange goods

Assuming that $u$ is differentiable, the marginal rate of substitution of good $x_2$ for good $x_1$ is:

$$MRS_{12}=\frac{\partial u(x_1,x_2)/\partial x_1}{\partial u(x_1,x_2)/\partial x_2}.$$

The numerator is the marginal utility of good 1; the denominator is the marginal utility of good 2. Each measures how utility changes when the quantity of that good increases slightly, holding the other quantity fixed.

In one sentence: $MRS_{12}$ measures how much of $x_2$ the consumer is willing to give up for one more unit of $x_1$, locally, while keeping utility constant.

The same intuition using differentials

We want a small change $(dx_1,dx_2)$ that keeps utility constant. The total change in utility must therefore be zero:

$$du=\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_1}dx_1+\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_2}dx_2=0.$$

Both partial derivatives are evaluated at $(x_1,x_2)$. The utility gained from increasing good 1 must offset the utility lost from reducing good 2. Move the first term to the right:

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_2}dx_2=-\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_1}dx_1.$$

Dividing by $dx_1$ and by the nonzero marginal utility of good 2 gives the slope along the indifference curve:

$$\frac{dx_2}{dx_1}=-\frac{\partial u/\partial x_1}{\partial u/\partial x_2}.$$

The MRS is the amount given up, so we take the negative of that slope:

$$MRS_{12}=-\frac{dx_2}{dx_1}=\frac{\partial u/\partial x_1}{\partial u/\partial x_2}.$$
