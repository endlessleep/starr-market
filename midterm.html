01

Rationality & Utility Representability

A. Utility Representability $\implies$ Rationality

Part 1: Completeness

Since $u(x), u(y) \in \mathbb{R}$, either $u(x) \ge u(y)$ or $u(y) \ge u(x)$.

Therefore, $x \succeq y$ or $y \succeq x$. Hence $\succeq$ is complete.

Part 2: Transitivity

Suppose $x \succeq y$ and $y \succeq z$.

Then $u(x) \ge u(y)$ and $u(y) \ge u(z)$, which implies $u(x) \ge u(z)$ by transitivity of $\ge$ on $\mathbb{R}$.

Therefore, $x \succeq z$. Hence $\succeq$ is transitive. $\blacksquare$

B. Rationality $\implies$ Utility Representability

Does rationality guarantee utility representability?

Suppose that $\succeq$ is rational, meaning that it is complete and transitive.

CASE 1 — FINITE CHOICE SET

If $X$ is finite, all alternatives can be ranked consistently because preferences are complete and transitive.

We can assign real numbers to alternatives according to their ranking, assigning the same utility to indifferent alternatives.

Thus, there exists a utility function $u:X\to\mathbb{R}$ such that:

$$x \succeq y \iff u(x)\geq u(y)$$

Therefore:

$$\boxed{\text{Rationality}\implies\text{Utility Representability}}$$

CASE 2 — INFINITE CHOICE SET

Suppose $X$ is infinite and $\succeq$ is rational (complete and transitive).

1. Rationality alone

Completeness and transitivity do not exclude discontinuous preferences (sudden jumps): even a very small change in an alternative may lead to a sudden change in its ranking.

Therefore:

$$\boxed{\text{Rationality}\not\implies\text{Representability in general}}$$

2. Adding Continuity

Continuity prevents sudden jumps in preferences: small changes in alternatives cannot reverse a strict preference.

On $X = \mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}$, we obtain:

$$\underbrace{\text{Complete + Transitive}}_{\text{Rationality}}+\text{Continuous}$$
$$\Downarrow$$
$$\boxed{\text{Continuous Utility Representability}}$$

Remark.

Continuity is sufficient, but not necessary: some discontinuous preferences are still representable.

02

Property α ⇔ Rationalizability

Choice Functions

$$\boxed{\text{A choice function on } X \text{ is rationalizable } \iff \text{it satisfies Property } \alpha}$$
Direction 1: Rationalizable $\implies$ Property α
Starting point: Suppose $C$ is rationalizable.
Then there exists a rational preference relation $\succeq$ such that, for every choice set $A$, $C(A)$ is the unique best alternative in $A$.

Let $C$ be a rationalizable choice function on $X$.

Then there exists a rational preference relation $\succeq$ such that, for every choice set $A$, $C(A)$ is the unique best alternative in $A$:

$$C(A) \succ y \qquad \forall y \in A, \ y \neq C(A)$$

Assume $B \subset A$ and $C(A) \in B$.

Since $B \subset A$, it is also true that:

$$C(A) \succ y \qquad \forall y \in B, \ y \neq C(A)$$

Hence $C(A)$ is also the unique best alternative in $B$, so:

$$\boxed{C(B) = C(A)}$$

Thus, $C$ satisfies Property $\alpha$. $\blacksquare$

Direction 2: Property α $\implies$ Rationalizable

Starting point: Let $X$ be a choice set and $c$ a choice function on $X$. Suppose $c$ satisfies Property α.
We construct a preference relation from pairwise choices, then show that it is rational and generates exactly the choices made by $c$.

Define a binary relation $\succeq$ by:

$$x \succeq y \iff c(\{x, y\}) = x$$

Show that this relation is a rational preference relation.

(Note: We are defining a particular preference relation from $c$ to show that $c$ is rationalizable.)

Show that for every choice set $A$, $c(A) \succeq a$ for every $a \in A$. In other words, that the chosen alternative is the unique best alternative according to $\succeq$.

Part (a) — Completeness

Pick any $x, y \in X$. Since $c$ is a single-valued choice function on the two-element choice set $\{x, y\}$:

$$c(\{x, y\}) \in \{x, y\}$$

Therefore, either:

$$c(\{x, y\}) = x \implies x \succeq y \qquad\text{or}\qquad c(\{x, y\}) = y \implies y \succeq x$$

Hence:

$$\boxed{\succeq \text{ is complete}}$$
Part (b) — Transitivity

Assume $x \succeq y$ and $y \succeq z$. By definition of $\succeq$:

$$c(\{x, y\}) = x \qquad\text{and}\qquad c(\{y, z\}) = y$$

Consider the choice set $\{x, y, z\}$. Since $c$ selects an element from $\{x, y, z\}$:

$$c(\{x, y, z\}) \in \{x, y, z\}$$

We check the three possibilities:

• If $c(\{x, y, z\}) = y$: since $\{x, y\} \subset \{x, y, z\}$ and $y \in \{x, y\}$, Property α implies $c(\{x, y\}) = y$, which contradicts $c(\{x, y\}) = x$.

• If $c(\{x, y, z\}) = z$: since $\{y, z\} \subset \{x, y, z\}$ and $z \in \{y, z\}$, Property α implies $c(\{y, z\}) = z$, which contradicts $c(\{y, z\}) = y$.

• Therefore, we must have: $c(\{x, y, z\}) = x$.

Now consider the pair $\{x, z\} \subset \{x, y, z\}$. Since $x \in \{x, z\}$, Property α implies:

$$c(\{x, z\}) = c(\{x, y, z\}) = x$$

By definition of $\succeq$, $c(\{x, z\}) = x \implies \mathbf{x \succeq z}$.

Hence:

$$\boxed{\succeq \text{ is transitive}}$$
Conclusion

Since $\succeq$ is both complete and transitive:

$$\boxed{\succeq \text{ is a rational preference relation}}$$
Goal. For every choice set $A$,
$$\boxed{c(A) \succeq a \qquad \forall a \in A.}$$
In other words, the alternative chosen from $A$ must be the unique best alternative according to $\succeq$.
Proof by Contradiction

Take any choice set $A$, with:

$$c(A) \in A.$$

Suppose by contradiction that $c(A)$ is not the best alternative in $A$.

Then there exists some:

$$a \in A, \qquad a \neq c(A),$$

such that:

$$a \succeq c(A).$$

By the definition of the preference relation:

$$a \succeq c(A) \iff c(\{a, c(A)\}) = a.$$

Hence:

$$\boxed{c(\{a, c(A)\}) = a.}$$
Contradiction via Property α

However,

$$\{a, c(A)\} \subseteq A \qquad\text{and}\qquad c(A) \in \{a, c(A)\}.$$

Since $c(A)$ is chosen from the larger set $A$, Property α implies that it must remain chosen in every smaller set that still contains it:

$$\boxed{c(\{a, c(A)\}) = c(A).}$$

This contradicts: $c(\{a, c(A)\}) = a$.

Therefore, the assumption was false: there cannot exist any $a \in A$ that is preferred to $c(A)$.

Hence:

$$\boxed{c(A) \succeq a \qquad \forall a \in A.}$$
Uniqueness & Strict Preference

Moreover, for every $a \neq c(A)$, the choice function selects only one element from the pair:

$$c(\{a, c(A)\}) = c(A) \implies a \not\succeq c(A).$$

Therefore:

$$c(A) \succ a \qquad \forall a \in A, \ a \neq c(A).$$

Thus:

$$\boxed{c(A) \text{ is the unique best alternative in } A.}$$
Conclusion

Therefore, the rational preference relation $\succeq$ generates exactly the choices made by $c$, and:

$$\boxed{c \text{ is rationalizable.}}$$
03

HARP ⇔ Rationalizability

Choice Rules

$$\boxed{\text{Given a finite set } X, \text{ a choice rule } C \text{ is rationalizable } \iff C \text{ satisfies HARP.}}$$
Direction 1: Rationalizable $\implies$ HARP
Starting point: Suppose $C$ is rationalizable.
Then, by definition, there exists a rational preference relation $\succeq$ such that $C(A)$ contains the maximal alternatives of every choice set $A$.

Suppose $C$ is rationalizable (by a rational preference relation $\succeq$).

Consider $a, b \in A \cap B$ such that $a \in C(A)$ and $b \in C(B)$:

• Since $a \in C(A)$ and $b \in A$, we have: $a \succeq b$.

• Similarly, since $b \in C(B)$ and $a \in B$, we have: $b \succeq a$.

Hence: $a \sim b$.

Since $b$ is maximal in $B$ and $a \sim b$, $a$ is also maximal in $B$, so: $a \in C(B)$.

Similarly, since $a$ is maximal in $A$ and $b \sim a$, $b$ is also maximal in $A$, so: $b \in C(A)$.

Thus, $C$ satisfies HARP. $\blacksquare$

Direction 2: HARP $\implies$ Rationalizable
Starting point: Suppose $C$ satisfies HARP.
Now we do not yet have a preference relation: we construct one from observed choices, and then prove it is rational.

Define the revealed preference relation $\succeq$ by:

$$\boxed{x \succeq y \iff \exists A \ni x, y \text{ such that } x \in C(A)}$$

i.e. $x \succeq y$ iff we have observed $x$ being chosen when $y$ was available.

Let's show that $\succeq$ is complete and transitive, and that $C(\cdot) = C(\cdot, \succeq)$:

Pick any $x, y \in X$.

Since $C(\{x, y\}) \neq \varnothing$, either:

$$x \in C(\{x, y\}) \quad \text{or} \quad y \in C(\{x, y\}) \quad \text{(or both).}$$

Hence:

$$x \succeq y \quad \text{or} \quad y \succeq x$$
$$\boxed{\succeq \text{ is complete}}$$

Suppose $x \succeq y$ and $y \succeq z$, and let $A = \{x, y, z\}$. Let's show that $x \succeq z$.

Since $C(A) \neq \varnothing$, at least one of $x, y, z$ belongs to $C(A)$:

(a) If $x \in C(A)$:

Then, by definition, $x \succeq z$.

(b) If $y \in C(A)$:

Recall that $x \succeq y$, hence $\exists B \ni x, y$ such that $x \in C(B)$.
Since $y \in C(A)$, $x \in C(B)$, and $x, y \in A \cap B$, HARP implies: $x \in C(A)$.
And we are back to (a).

(c) If $z \in C(A)$:

Recall that $y \succeq z$, hence $\exists D \ni y, z$ such that $y \in C(D)$.
Since $z \in C(A)$, $y \in C(D)$, and $y, z \in A \cap D$, HARP implies: $y \in C(A)$.
And we are back to (b) (which in turn leads to (a)).

Therefore: $x \succeq z$.

$$\boxed{\succeq \text{ is transitive}}$$
$$z \in C(A) \implies y \in C(A) \implies x \in C(A) \implies x \succeq z$$

Chosen alternatives are exactly the maximal alternatives:

First inclusion $C(A) \subseteq C(A, \succeq)$:

Suppose $y \in C(A)$.
Then for every $z \in A$, by definition of revealed preference: $y \succeq z$.
Hence $y$ is maximal in $A$, so $y \in C(A, \succeq)$.
Therefore:

$$\boxed{C(A) \subseteq C(A, \succeq)}$$
Second inclusion $C(A, \succeq) \subseteq C(A)$:

Now suppose $y \in C(A, \succeq)$.
Choose any $w \in C(A)$.
Since $y$ is maximal: $y \succeq w$.
By definition of revealed preference, there exists a choice set $B$ containing $y, w$ such that $y \in C(B)$.
Since $w \in C(A)$, $y \in C(B)$, and $y, w \in A \cap B$, HARP implies: $y \in C(A)$.
Therefore:

$$\boxed{C(A, \succeq) \subseteq C(A)}$$
$$\boxed{C(A) = C(A, \succeq)}$$

Therefore, $C$ is rationalizable. $\blacksquare$

Consumer Preferences

01

Monotonicity

Consumption set: $X=\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}$

Strict Monotonicity

$$x\geq y,\quad x\neq y\implies x\succ y$$

If bundle $x$ contains at least as much of every good as bundle $y$, and strictly more of at least one good, then $x$ is strictly preferred to $y$.

Weak Monotonicity

$$x\geq y\implies x\succeq y$$

If bundle $x$ contains at least as much of every good as bundle $y$, then $x$ is at least as good as $y$. The consumer may strictly prefer $x$ or be indifferent between $x$ and $y$.

Local Non-Satiation (LNS)

$$\forall x\in X,\ \forall\varepsilon>0,\quad \exists y\in X:\quad \|x-y\|<\varepsilon,\quad y\succ x$$

For every bundle $x$, there exists another bundle $y$, arbitrarily close to $x$, that is strictly preferred to $x$.

Relationship Between Monotonicity and LNS

$$\boxed{\text{Strict Monotonicity}\implies\text{LNS}}$$

Strict monotonicity guarantees LNS because even the smallest positive increase in one good, without reducing the others, makes the new bundle strictly better.

$$\boxed{\text{Weak Monotonicity}\not\implies\text{LNS}}$$

Weak monotonicity does not guarantee LNS because having more goods may leave the consumer indifferent, whereas LNS requires a strictly preferred nearby bundle.

02

Convexity

Convex Preferences

$$x\succeq z,\quad y\succeq z \implies tx+(1-t)y\succeq z$$

For $t\in[0,1]$. If bundles $x$ and $y$ are both at least as good as bundle $z$, then any weighted average of $x$ and $y$ is also at least as good as $z$.

Strict Convexity

$$x\succeq z,\quad y\succeq z \implies tx+(1-t)y\succ z$$

For $x\neq y$ and $t\in(0,1)$. If two distinct bundles $x$ and $y$ are both at least as good as bundle $z$, then any weighted average that gives positive weight to both bundles is strictly preferred to $z$.

Implication of Convexity

$$x\succ y \implies tx+(1-t)y\succeq y$$

For $t\in[0,1]$. If bundle $x$ is strictly preferred to bundle $y$, then any weighted average of $x$ and $y$ is at least as good as $y$.

03

Homotheticity

Homothetic Preferences

$$x\succeq y \iff \lambda x\succeq\lambda y \qquad \lambda>0$$

Multiplying every quantity in both bundles by the same positive factor $\lambda$ preserves their preference ranking.

04

Separability

Separable Preferences

$$(x,y)\succeq(x',y) \iff (x,y')\succeq(x',y')$$

The ranking between $x$ and $x'$ does not depend on the common value of the other component: replacing $y$ with $y'$ in both bundles preserves the ranking.

05

Numéraire Properties

Here, $t$ represents money, while $y$ represents other goods.

1. The Numéraire Good Is Valuable

$$(t,y)\succeq(t',y) \iff t\geq t'$$

When the other goods $y$ remain unchanged, the consumer prefers the alternative with more money. If the money amounts are equal, the consumer is indifferent.

2. Compensation Is Possible

$$\forall y,y'\in Y,\quad \exists t\in\mathbb R: (0,y)\sim(t,y')$$

For any two bundles of other goods $y$ and $y'$, there exists an amount of money $t$ that makes the consumer indifferent between $(0,y)$ and $(t,y')$.

3. No Wealth Effect

$$(t,y)\succeq(t',y') \iff (t+d,y)\succeq(t'+d,y')$$

Adding the same amount of money $d$ to both alternatives does not change their preference ranking, even if their initial money amounts $t$ and $t'$ are different.

06

Quasi-Linear Utility

Numéraire Properties ⇔ Quasi-Linear Utility

For rational preferences on $\mathbb R\times Y$, the three numéraire properties hold if and only if preferences can be represented by:

$$\boxed{u(t,y)=t+v(y)}$$

Quasi-linear in money: money $t$ plus utility $v(y)$ from other goods.

Starting Point / Hypotheses:
We assume rational preferences on $\mathbb{R}\times Y$ satisfying the three numéraire properties: (1) $t$ is valuable, (2) Compensation is possible, and (3) No wealth effect.
Goal: Construct $v:Y\to\mathbb{R}$ so that $u(t,y)=t+v(y)$ represents the preference relation.
1
Because compensation is possible (Property 2).
Fix an arbitrary reference bundle $\bar{y} \in Y$. Because compensation is possible, for every $y \in Y$, there exists $v(y) \in \mathbb{R}$, the money amount that makes the reference alternative $\bar{y}$ equivalent to $y$:
$$(0, y) \sim (v(y), \bar{y})$$
Similarly, for $y'\in Y$:
$$(0, y') \sim (v(y'), \bar{y})$$
2
Because there is no wealth effect (Property 3).
Indifference means that both weak comparisons hold. Apply property 3 to each comparison, adding $t$ to both alternatives:
$$(t, y) \sim (t + v(y), \bar{y})$$
Similarly, adding $t'$ preserves indifference:
$$(t', y') \sim (t' + v(y'), \bar{y})$$
3
Hence, compare the monetary equivalents.
By transitivity of $\succeq$, comparing $(t, y)$ and $(t', y')$ is equivalent to comparing their monetary equivalents at $\bar{y}$:
$$\begin{gathered} (t,y)\succeq(t',y')\\ \iff (t+v(y),\bar y)\succeq(t'+v(y'),\bar y). \end{gathered}$$
In each direction, replace the two bundles by their indifferent monetary equivalents and use transitivity.
4
Because the numéraire good is valuable (Property 1).
Because the numéraire good is valuable (Property 1), the last comparison can be rewritten purely in terms of the numéraire amounts:
$$\begin{gathered} (t,y)\succeq(t',y')\\ \iff t+v(y)\geq t'+v(y'). \end{gathered}$$
Hence $\succeq$ can be represented by the quasi-linear utility function $u(t, y) = t + v(y)$. $\blacksquare$

Revealed Preference Theory

01

Revealed Preferences

Revealed Preference (RP)

$X=(x_1,x_2)$ is chosen at prices $p=(p_1,p_2)$.

$$\boxed{p_1x_1+p_2x_2\geq p_1y_1+p_2y_2}$$

$X$ is revealed preferred to $Y$ because the consumer chooses $X$ even though $Y$ is affordable.

Strict Revealed Preference (SRP)

$X=(x_1,x_2)$ is chosen at prices $p=(p_1,p_2)$.

$$\boxed{p_1x_1+p_2x_2>p_1y_1+p_2y_2}$$

$X$ is strictly revealed preferred to $Y$ because the consumer chooses $X$ even though $Y$ is strictly cheaper.

02

Weak Axiom of Revealed Preference (WARP)

Definition — WARP

We observe two different bundles chosen at two different price vectors:

$X=(x_1,x_2)$ is chosen at prices $p=(p_1,p_2)$.

$Y=(y_1,y_2)$ is chosen at prices $q=(q_1,q_2)$.

If:

$$\boxed{p_1x_1+p_2x_2\geq p_1y_1+p_2y_2}$$

$X$ is revealed preferred to $Y$ because $Y$ was affordable when $X$ was chosen.

It must NOT be the case that

When $Y$ is chosen at prices $q$, $X$ cannot also be affordable.

$$\boxed{q_1y_1+q_2y_2\geq q_1x_1+q_2x_2}$$

It MUST be the case that

When $Y$ is chosen at prices $q$, $X$ must be too expensive to afford.

$$\boxed{q_1y_1+q_2y_2<q_1x_1+q_2x_2}$$

These are two equivalent ways of expressing the same WARP condition, not two separate definitions.

WARP — Key takeaway

If $X$ is directly revealed preferred to $Y$, then $Y$ cannot be directly revealed preferred to $X$.

$$\boxed{X\;\mathrm{DRP}\;Y\quad\Rightarrow\quad Y\;\text{not DRP}\;X}$$

WARP prevents two opposite direct revealed preferences.

When $Y$ is chosen at prices $q$, $X$ must be strictly more expensive: $q\cdot X>q\cdot Y$. Equal cost would still make $X$ affordable, creating a reverse DRP.

03

Direct & Indirect Revealed Preferences

Directly Revealed Preference (DRP)

$X=(x_1,x_2)$ is chosen at prices $p=(p_1,p_2)$.

$$\boxed{p_1x_1+p_2x_2\geq p_1y_1+p_2y_2}$$

$X$ is directly revealed preferred to $Y$ because the consumer chooses $X$ while $Y$ is affordable.

A single observed choice establishes the comparison.

Indirectly Revealed Preference (IRP)

Consider three different bundles $X$, $Y$, and $Z$.

  • $X$ is chosen when $Y$ is affordable.
  • $Y$ is chosen when $Z$ is affordable.

Therefore:

$$\boxed{X\;\mathrm{DRP}\;Y\;\mathrm{DRP}\;Z}$$

And consequently:

$$\boxed{X\;\mathrm{IRP}\;Z}$$

$X$ is indirectly revealed preferred to $Z$ through the intermediate bundle $Y$.

Key difference

DRP: The preference is revealed through one observed choice.

IRP: The preference is revealed through a chain of observed choices, connected by intermediate bundles.

04

Generalized Axiom of Revealed Preference (GARP)

Definition — GARP

Consider a sequence of bundles: $x^1,x^2,\ldots,x^K$.

Each bundle $x^k$ is chosen at prices $p^k$.

If:

$$\boxed{\begin{gathered} x^1\;\mathrm{DRP}\;x^2\\ x^2\;\mathrm{DRP}\;x^3\\ \vdots\\ x^{K-1}\;\mathrm{DRP}\;x^K \end{gathered}}$$

Then:

$$\boxed{x^1\;\mathrm{IRP}\;x^K}$$

It must NOT be the case that

When $x^K$ is chosen at prices $p^K$, $x^1$ cannot be strictly cheaper than $x^K$.

$$\boxed{p^K\cdot x^K>p^K\cdot x^1}$$

In other words: $x^K\text{ is not SRP to }x^1$.

It MUST be the case that

When $x^K$ is chosen at prices $p^K$, $x^1$ must cost at least as much as $x^K$.

$$\boxed{p^K\cdot x^1\geq p^K\cdot x^K}$$

GARP — Key takeaway

If $X$ is directly or indirectly revealed preferred to $Y$, then $Y$ cannot be strictly revealed preferred to $X$.

$$\boxed{X\;\mathrm{RP}\;Y\quad\Rightarrow\quad Y\;\text{not SRP}\;X}$$

Here, RP includes DRP (direct) and IRP (through a chain).

GARP prevents a strict reversal, even after a chain of revealed preferences.

When $Y$ is chosen at prices $q$, $X$ must cost at least as much: $q\cdot X\geq q\cdot Y$. Equal cost is allowed: it does not create a reverse SRP.

WARP vs. GARP

WARP · Direct

$$\boxed{\mathrm{DRP}\Rightarrow\text{not DRP}}$$

Rules out two opposite direct comparisons.

When $Y$ is chosen at $q$, $X$ must be unaffordable: $q\cdot X>q\cdot Y.$

GARP · Direct or Indirect

$$\boxed{\mathrm{RP}\Rightarrow\text{not SRP}}$$

Rules out a strict reversal, including after a chain.

When $Y$ is chosen at $q$, $X$ cannot be strictly cheaper: $q\cdot X\geq q\cdot Y.$

Equal cost: forbidden by WARP; allowed by GARP.

05

Afriat's Theorem

For a finite dataset $D$ of nonnegative bundles and strictly positive prices, the following statements are equivalent:

1

The dataset satisfies GARP.

$\Updownarrow$
2

The dataset can be rationalized by a locally non-satiated utility function.

There exists a utility function under which every observed bundle maximizes utility within its budget.

$\Updownarrow$
3

The dataset can be rationalized by a continuous, concave, and strictly monotone utility function.

Key takeaway

$$\boxed{\text{GARP}\iff\text{Utility Maximization}}$$

GARP guarantees a utility function that makes every observed choice optimal within its budget.

Conversely, choices generated by utility maximization with LNS satisfy GARP.

You do not need the consumer’s actual utility function: a suitable one exists.

Consumer Demand & Duality

I

Marshallian Demand

Utility Maximization Problem (UMP) · Fix income, maximize utility.

01The Utility Maximization Problem

Choose the affordable bundle that maximizes utility.

$$\boxed{\begin{aligned}\max_{x_1,x_2\geq0}\quad &u(x_1,x_2)\\\text{s.t.}\quad &p_1x_1+p_2x_2\leq y\end{aligned}}$$

Under local non-satiation, the budget binds:

$$p_1x_1+p_2x_2=y$$

Solution: $x_1^M(p,y)$ and $x_2^M(p,y)$, the optimal quantities as functions of prices and income.

02How to Solve the UMP

Interior solution: differentiable utility and positive marginal utilities, $MU_i=\partial u/\partial x_i$.

Method 1 — Lagrangian
  1. Write the Lagrangian.
    $$\begin{aligned}\mathcal L&=u(x_1,x_2)\\&\quad+\lambda(y-p_1x_1-p_2x_2)\end{aligned}$$
  2. Compute the FOCs.
    $$\begin{cases}\dfrac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial x_1}=MU_1-\lambda p_1=0\\[5pt]\dfrac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial x_2}=MU_2-\lambda p_2=0\\[5pt]\dfrac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\lambda}=y-p_1x_1-p_2x_2=0\end{cases}$$
  3. Eliminate $\lambda$.

    $MU_1=\lambda p_1$ and $MU_2=\lambda p_2$. Divide:

    $$\frac{MU_1}{MU_2}=\frac{p_1}{p_2}$$
  4. Solve with the budget. Express one quantity in terms of the other and substitute into $p_1x_1+p_2x_2=y$.
  5. Obtain $x_1^M(p,y)$ and $x_2^M(p,y)$. Check that both quantities are nonnegative.
Method 2 — MRS Condition

Use the same interior condition directly:

$$\boxed{\begin{cases}MRS_{12}=\dfrac{MU_1}{MU_2}=\dfrac{p_1}{p_2}\\[8pt]p_1x_1+p_2x_2=y\end{cases}}$$
  1. Compute $MU_1$ and $MU_2$, then form the MRS.
  2. Set the MRS equal to the price ratio and express one quantity in terms of the other.
  3. Substitute into the budget constraint, solve for both demands, and check the solution.

03Indirect Utility Function

The maximum utility attainable at given prices and income.

$$v(p,y)=\max_{\substack{x\geq0\\p\cdot x\leq y}}u(x)$$

Compute: find Marshallian demand, substitute into the original utility function, then simplify.

$$\boxed{v(p,y)=u\big(x_1^M(p,y),x_2^M(p,y)\big)}$$

04Roy’s Identity

Recover Marshallian demand from differentiable indirect utility, with $v_y>0$.

$$\boxed{x_i^M(p,y)=-\frac{\partial v(p,y)/\partial p_i}{\partial v(p,y)/\partial y}}$$

Apply: differentiate $v$ with respect to $p_i$ and $y$, divide, and keep the minus sign.

$\displaystyle x_1^M=-\frac{v_{p_1}}{v_y}$$\displaystyle x_2^M=-\frac{v_{p_2}}{v_y}$

05Lagrange Multiplier — Envelope Theorem

For $\mathcal L=u(x)+\lambda(y-p\cdot x)$:

$$\boxed{\frac{\partial v(p,y)}{\partial y}=\lambda^*}$$

The multiplier is the marginal utility of income: the increase in maximum utility per extra unit of income.

II

Hicksian Demand

Expenditure Minimization Problem (EmP) · Fix utility, minimize expenditure.

01The Expenditure Minimization Problem

Choose the least expensive bundle that attains the required utility $\bar u$.

$$\boxed{\begin{aligned}\min_{x_1,x_2\geq0}\quad &p_1x_1+p_2x_2\\\text{s.t.}\quad &u(x_1,x_2)\geq\bar u\end{aligned}}$$

At the optimum, the utility target is reached:

$$u(x_1,x_2)=\bar u$$

Solution: $h_1(p,\bar u)$ and $h_2(p,\bar u)$, the optimal quantities as functions of prices and target utility, not income.

02How to Solve the EmP

Interior solution: differentiable utility and positive marginal utilities.

Method 1 — Lagrangian
  1. Write the Lagrangian.
    $$\begin{aligned}\mathcal L&=p_1x_1+p_2x_2\\&\quad+\lambda[\bar u-u(x_1,x_2)]\end{aligned}$$
  2. Compute the FOCs.
    $$\begin{cases}\dfrac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial x_1}=p_1-\lambda MU_1=0\\[5pt]\dfrac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial x_2}=p_2-\lambda MU_2=0\\[5pt]\dfrac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\lambda}=\bar u-u(x_1,x_2)=0\end{cases}$$
  3. Eliminate $\lambda$.

    $p_1=\lambda MU_1$ and $p_2=\lambda MU_2$. Divide:

    $$\frac{MU_1}{MU_2}=\frac{p_1}{p_2}$$
  4. Solve with the utility constraint. Express one quantity in terms of the other and substitute into $u(x_1,x_2)=\bar u$.
  5. Obtain $h_1(p,\bar u)$ and $h_2(p,\bar u)$. Check nonnegativity and attainment of the target utility.
Method 2 — MRS Condition

Use the same interior condition directly:

$$\boxed{\begin{cases}MRS_{12}=\dfrac{MU_1}{MU_2}=\dfrac{p_1}{p_2}\\[8pt]u(x_1,x_2)=\bar u\end{cases}}$$
  1. Compute $MU_1$ and $MU_2$, then form the MRS.
  2. Set the MRS equal to the price ratio and express one quantity in terms of the other.
  3. Substitute into the utility constraint, solve for both demands, and check the solution.

03Expenditure Function

The minimum expenditure needed to attain $\bar u$.

$$e(p,\bar u)=\min_{\substack{x\geq0\\u(x)\geq\bar u}}p\cdot x$$

Compute: find Hicksian demand, substitute into total expenditure, then simplify.

$$\boxed{\begin{aligned}e(p,\bar u)&=p_1h_1(p,\bar u)\\&\quad+p_2h_2(p,\bar u)\end{aligned}}$$

04Shephard’s Lemma

Recover Hicksian demand by differentiating expenditure.

$$\boxed{h_i(p,\bar u)=\frac{\partial e(p,\bar u)}{\partial p_i}}$$

Apply: differentiate $e(p,\bar u)$ with respect to $p_i$, holding target utility fixed.

$\displaystyle h_1=\frac{\partial e}{\partial p_1}$$\displaystyle h_2=\frac{\partial e}{\partial p_2}$

UMP & EmPCorner Solutions

Corner = one good is consumed in quantity zero.

UMP: under local non-satiation, all income goes to the other good. EmP: buy enough of the other good to reach the utility target at minimum cost.

Method 1 — Look at the Utility

Set good $x_1$ to zero. Does utility collapse, or is it still well-defined?

  • Collapses: logarithmic utility is undefined at zero, or a standard product/minimum utility gives zero. If a feasible positive bundle gives higher utility (UMP), or zero is below the target (EmP), $x_1=0$ cannot be optimal.
  • Still well-defined: the good is simply added, such as $+x_1$. Setting $x_1=0$ is possible: a corner may occur.

Repeat the same test for $x_2$.

Method 2 — Look at the Demand

For smooth preferences, solve $MRS_{12}=p_1/p_2$ together with:

$$\begin{aligned}\text{UMP:}\quad &p_1x_1+p_2x_2=y\\\text{EmP:}\quad &u(x_1,x_2)=\bar u\end{aligned}$$

Are the resulting quantities positive?

  • Both positive: the candidate is interior, so neither good is at a corner.
  • One is negative: a negative quantity is impossible. Set that good to zero and solve for the other using the relevant constraint. This gives a corner candidate.

For minimum utility, use the kink condition instead of the MRS equation.

Choose the best feasible candidate: highest utility for the UMP; lowest expenditure while reaching $\bar u$ for the EmP.

Key Properties & Theorems

Results and checks for solving exercises.

01Uniqueness of Marshallian Demand

1. Definition and Main Result

Marshallian demand is unique when there is exactly one optimal consumption bundle for given prices and income.

Strictly convex preferences + existence of an optimum
$\Rightarrow$ Unique Marshallian demand.

Strict convexity guarantees that two different affordable bundles cannot both maximize utility.

2. How to Prove Uniqueness

If two different optimal bundles existed, strict convexity would allow us to construct another affordable bundle that is strictly better. This is impossible because the original bundles were assumed optimal.

Step 1 — Assume Two Optimal Bundles Exist

Suppose two distinct bundles $A$ and $B$ are both optimal:

$$A\neq B,\qquad A\sim B$$

They must be indifferent because both provide the maximum utility.

This is a temporary assumption, not an established result. We will show that it leads to a contradiction.

Step 2 — Apply Strict Convexity

Recall the definition from Consumer Preferences:

$$\begin{gathered}x\succeq z,\quad y\succeq z\\\Rightarrow tx+(1-t)y\succ z\end{gathered}$$

For $x\neq y$ and $t\in(0,1)$.

Apply it with $x=A$, $y=B$, and $z=A$. Since $A\succeq A$ and $B\succeq A$, strict convexity gives:

$$tA+(1-t)B\succ A$$

The mixture of $A$ and $B$ is strictly preferred to $A$.

Step 3 — Check Affordability

Since $A$ and $B$ are both affordable and the budget set is convex:

$$tA+(1-t)B\in B(p,y)$$

Therefore, the mixture is also affordable.

Step 4 — Obtain a Contradiction

We assumed that $A$ was optimal. However, the mixture is:

  • Affordable.
  • Strictly preferred to $A$.

This contradicts the assumption that $A$ maximizes utility. Two distinct optimal bundles therefore cannot exist.

Conclusion: if an optimum exists, it must be unique.

3. How to Prove Non-Uniqueness

Goal: Show that multiple optimal bundles exist.

Step 1 — Linear Utility Function
$$u(x_1,x_2)=ax_1+bx_2$$

Constants $a,b>0$; quantities $x_1,x_2\geq0$; prices $p_1,p_2>0$; income $m>0$.

Step 2 — Calculate the MRS
$$MU_1=a,\qquad MU_2=b$$
$$\boxed{MRS=\frac{a}{b}}$$

The MRS is constant and does not depend on $x_1$ or $x_2$.

Step 3 — Compare with the Price Ratio

If:

$$\boxed{\frac{a}{b}=\frac{p_1}{p_2}}$$

the consumer is indifferent between spending money on either good.

Step 4 — Show Non-Uniqueness

Every nonnegative bundle satisfying the budget constraint at equality:

$$p_1x_1+p_2x_2=m$$

provides the same maximum utility:

$$\boxed{u_{\max}=\frac{bm}{p_2}}$$

Since infinitely many bundles satisfy this budget equality (with $m>0$ and divisible goods), the optimal bundle is not unique.

Key Takeaway
$$\boxed{\begin{gathered}\frac{a}{b}=\frac{p_1}{p_2}\quad\Rightarrow\\\text{Infinitely many optimal bundles}\end{gathered}}$$

Important: If the ratios differ, the consumer chooses a unique corner solution.

4. Final Exam Reminder
Uniqueness — Proof by Contradiction

If two different optimal bundles existed, strict convexity would make an affordable mixture strictly better. This contradicts optimality, so the optimum is unique.

Non-Uniqueness — Direct Proof

For linear utility, equal marginal utility per euro makes every bundle on the budget line optimal. With positive income, multiple optimal bundles exist.

02Walras’ Law

Local non-satiation ⇒ the entire budget is spent.

$$\boxed{p\cdot x^M(p,y)=y}$$

With two goods: $p_1x_1^M+p_2x_2^M=y$.

Verify: multiply each demand by its price, add, and simplify.

$$\sum_{i=1}^n p_ix_i^M(p,y)\overset{?}{=}y$$

If equality fails, the proposed demand system does not satisfy Walras’ Law.

03Homogeneity of Degree Zero

Scaling all prices and income by $\lambda>0$ leaves demand unchanged.

$$\boxed{x^M(\lambda p,\lambda y)=x^M(p,y)}$$

Verify: replace every $p_i$ by $\lambda p_i$ and $y$ by $\lambda y$ in each demand; simplify.

$$x_i^M(\lambda p,\lambda y)\overset{?}{=}x_i^M(p,y)$$

Scale income too: changing prices alone is not this test.

04Duality of Consumer Problems

UMP: fix income and maximize utility. EmP: fix utility and minimize expenditure.

Marshallian → Hicksian

Start with income $y$. Compute $v(p,y)$ and use it as the utility target in the EmP.

$$\boxed{h(p,v(p,y))=x^M(p,y)}$$

The required expenditure equals the original income:

$$\boxed{e(p,v(p,y))=y}$$
Hicksian → Marshallian

Start with target $\bar u$. Compute $e(p,\bar u)$ and use it as income in the UMP.

$$\boxed{x^M(p,e(p,\bar u))=h(p,\bar u)}$$

The maximum utility equals the original target:

$$\boxed{v(p,e(p,\bar u))=\bar u}$$

Matching income and utility ⇒ the same optimal bundle.

Income and Price Effects

01Slutsky Equation

$$\boxed{\begin{aligned}\frac{\partial x_i(p,y)}{\partial p_j}&=\frac{\partial h_i(p,\bar u)}{\partial p_j}\\&-\left[\frac{\partial x_i(p,y)}{\partial y}\right]x_j(p,y)\end{aligned}}$$

Total effect

$$\frac{\partial x_i(p,y)}{\partial p_j}$$

Substitution effect

$$\frac{\partial h_i(p,\bar u)}{\partial p_j}$$

Income effect

$$-\left[\frac{\partial x_i(p,y)}{\partial y}\right]x_j(p,y)$$
Read the derivation step by step
$$h_i(p,\bar u)=x_i(p,e(p,\bar u)).$$

1. Match the two problems. Hicksian demand equals Marshallian demand evaluated at the expenditure needed to attain $\bar u$: $y=e(p,\bar u)$.

2. Identify both channels. When $p_j$ changes, the right-hand side changes directly through $p_j$ and indirectly through expenditure $e(p,\bar u)$.

3. Differentiate. Expenditure enters the income argument of Marshallian demand, so the chain rule gives:

$$\begin{aligned}\frac{\partial h_i(p,\bar u)}{\partial p_j}&=\frac{\partial x_i(p,y)}{\partial p_j}\\&+\left[\frac{\partial x_i(p,y)}{\partial y}\right]\left[\frac{\partial e(p,\bar u)}{\partial p_j}\right].\end{aligned}$$

4. Apply Shephard’s Lemma.

$$\frac{\partial e(p,\bar u)}{\partial p_j}=h_j(p,\bar u).$$

5. Use the same-problem identity.

$$h_j(p,\bar u)=x_j(p,y).$$

Therefore:

$$\begin{aligned}\frac{\partial h_i(p,\bar u)}{\partial p_j}&=\frac{\partial x_i(p,y)}{\partial p_j}\\&+\left[\frac{\partial x_i(p,y)}{\partial y}\right]x_j(p,y).\end{aligned}$$

Rearrange:

$$\boxed{\begin{aligned}\frac{\partial x_i(p,y)}{\partial p_j}&=\frac{\partial h_i(p,\bar u)}{\partial p_j}\\&-\left[\frac{\partial x_i(p,y)}{\partial y}\right]x_j(p,y)\end{aligned}}$$

02Substitution and Income Effects

Substitution effect

Change in demand caused by a price change holding utility constant.

$$\boxed{SE=\frac{\partial h_i(p,\bar u)}{\partial p_j}}$$
$$\boxed{\frac{\partial h_i(p,\bar u)}{\partial p_i}\leq0}$$

For an own-price change, the substitution effect is always non-positive.

Income effect

A price change changes the consumer’s purchasing power. The resulting income effect depends on whether the good is normal or inferior.

$$\boxed{IE=-\left[\frac{\partial x_i(p,y)}{\partial y}\right]x_j(p,y)}$$

03Income and Price Elasticities

A. Income elasticity

$$\boxed{\eta_i=\left[\frac{\partial x_i(p,y)}{\partial y}\right]\frac{y}{x_i(p,y)}}$$

Demand response to income.

  • $\eta_i>0$: Normal good → income $\uparrow$ ⇒ demand for good $i$ $\uparrow$.
  • $\eta_i<0$: Inferior good → income $\uparrow$ ⇒ demand for good $i$ $\downarrow$.
  • $\eta_i>1$: Luxury good → demand increases more than proportionally with income.

B. Own-price elasticity

$$\boxed{\varepsilon_{ii}=\left[\frac{\partial x_i(p,y)}{\partial p_i}\right]\frac{p_i}{x_i(p,y)}}$$

Demand response to its own price.

  • $\varepsilon_{ii}<0$: Ordinary good → price of $i$ $\uparrow$ ⇒ demand for $i$ $\downarrow$.
  • $\varepsilon_{ii}>0$: Giffen good → price of $i$ $\uparrow$ ⇒ demand for $i$ $\uparrow$.

C. Cross-price elasticity

$$\boxed{\varepsilon_{ij}=\left[\frac{\partial x_i(p,y)}{\partial p_j}\right]\frac{p_j}{x_i(p,y)}}$$

Demand for $i$ when the price of $j$ changes.

  • $\varepsilon_{ij}>0$: Gross substitutes → price of $j$ $\uparrow$ ⇒ demand for $i$ $\uparrow$.
  • $\varepsilon_{ij}<0$: Gross complements → price of $j$ $\uparrow$ ⇒ demand for $i$ $\downarrow$.

Slutsky equation in elasticity form

$$\boxed{\varepsilon_{ij}=\varepsilon_{ij}^{h}-\eta_i\left(\frac{p_jx_j}{y}\right)}$$

$\varepsilon_{ij}$: Marshallian price elasticity

$\varepsilon_{ij}^{h}$: Hicksian price elasticity

$\eta_i$: income elasticity

$p_jx_j/y$: expenditure share of good $j$

04Research Focus

Giffen Good — Core Idea

A Giffen good is a good for which an increase in its price causes an increase in its quantity demanded.

$$\boxed{\varepsilon_{ii}>0}$$

The positive own-price elasticity arises when a negative income effect dominates the substitution effect.

$$\boxed{\varepsilon_{ii}=\varepsilon_{ii}^{h}-\eta_i\left(\frac{p_ix_i}{y}\right)>0}$$
Sign logic
  • Own-price effect: $\varepsilon_{ii}>0$
  • Substitution effect: $\varepsilon_{ii}^{h}<0$
  • Income elasticity: $\eta_i<0$

Hence:

$$-\eta_i\frac{p_ix_i}{y}>0$$

The income-effect component is strong enough to dominate the negative substitution effect.

Conditions for Giffen behavior
  • The staple is an inferior good: $\eta_i<0$.
  • Its budget share is large: $p_ix_i/y$ is large.
  • The staple has no ready substitute.
  • The household is near subsistence: not too rich, but not so poor that it already consumes almost only the staple.
$$\begin{gathered}\boxed{\text{Giffen}\Rightarrow\text{Inferior}}\\[6pt]\boxed{\text{Inferior}\not\Rightarrow\text{Giffen}}\end{gathered}$$
$$\begin{gathered}p_i\uparrow\ \Rightarrow\ \text{purchasing power}\downarrow\\\Rightarrow\ \text{demand for the inferior staple}\uparrow\end{gathered}$$

If this response dominates substitution:

$$\boxed{x_i\uparrow}$$

Core idea

Conspicuous consumption means purchasing visible goods partly to display wealth, status, or social prestige.

Do more visible goods have higher income elasticities?

Benchmark — No status concerns

$v$: visible consumption, with prices normalized to 1.

$$v(y)=\left[\frac{\beta}{1+\beta}\right]y$$

Therefore:

$$\frac{v}{y}=\frac{\beta}{1+\beta}$$

The expenditure share is constant, hence:

$$\boxed{\eta_v=1}$$
With status concerns

Visible consumption can signal income/status. As income rises, the share spent on the visible good rises:

$$\frac{v}{y}\uparrow$$

Visible consumption increases more than proportionally with income:

$$\boxed{\eta_v>1}$$

Richer consumers shift a larger share of their budget toward visible consumption.

Empirical takeaway — Heffetz (2011): More visible goods tend to have higher income elasticities.

05Welfare Effects of a Price Change

$$\begin{gathered}p\longrightarrow p',\qquad u'<u,\\u=v(p,w),\qquad u'=v(p',w).\end{gathered}$$

Money measure: use the expenditure function. Only $p_1$ rises to $p_1'>p_1$; income $w$ and other prices stay fixed.

A. Compensating Variation — CV

After the price increase, how much money restores the consumer’s initial utility?

CV = NEW prices + INITIAL utility.

$$\boxed{CV=e(p',u)-w}$$

Since $w=e(p,u)$:

$$\boxed{CV=e(p',u)-e(p,u)}$$
$$\boxed{CV=\int_{p_1}^{p_1'}h_1(p,u)\,dp_1}$$

CV is the area under Hicksian demand evaluated at the initial utility level $u$.

For a harmful price change: $CV>0$.

B. Equivalent Variation — EV

Before the price increase, how much income could we take away to reach the consumer’s final utility?

EV = OLD prices + FINAL utility.

$$\boxed{EV=w-e(p,u')}$$

Since $w=e(p',u')$:

$$\boxed{EV=e(p',u')-e(p,u')}$$
$$\boxed{EV=\int_{p_1}^{p_1'}h_1(p,u')\,dp_1}$$

EV is the area under Hicksian demand evaluated at the final utility level $u'$.

For a harmful price change: $EV>0$.

06Comparing CV and EV · Price Increase

Normal good

$$\boxed{CV>EV}$$

CV gives additional income after the price increase. For a normal good, this raises demand for the now-more-expensive good, so more compensation is required.

Inferior good

$$\boxed{EV>CV}$$

The income response goes in the opposite direction.

No income effect

$$\boxed{CV=EV}$$

Changing income does not change demand for the good, so both measures coincide.

07CV and EV Using the Indirect Utility Function

CV

Be as well off after the change as before the change.

$$\boxed{v(p',w+CV)=u}$$

where $u=v(p,w)$.

CV → give money AFTER.

EV

Be as well off before the change as after the change.

$$\boxed{v(p,w-EV)=u'}$$

where $u'=v(p',w)$.

EV → take money BEFORE.

08Consumer Surplus

For a price increase from $p_1$ to $p_1'$:

Change in consumer surplus

$$\boxed{\Delta CS=-\int_{p_1}^{p_1'}x_1(p,y)\,dp_1<0}$$

Consumer surplus loss

$$\boxed{-\Delta CS=\int_{p_1}^{p_1'}x_1(p,y)\,dp_1>0}$$

CV and EV: Hicksian demand. Consumer surplus: Marshallian demand.

Normal good · Price increase

$$\boxed{EV<-\Delta CS<CV}$$

No income effect

$$\boxed{EV=-\Delta CS=CV}$$

With quasilinear utility at an interior optimum, consumer-surplus loss is an exact monetary welfare measure.

3.1 — Technology and Production

01Returns to Scale and Homogeneity

Returns to scale describe the output response when all inputs change in the same proportion. For $s>1$, compare $f(sz)$ with $s f(z)$, holding input proportions fixed.

Increasing returns to scale (IRS)

$$\boxed{f(sz)>s f(z)}$$

Output increases more than proportionally to inputs.

Constant returns to scale (CRS)

$$\boxed{f(sz)=s f(z)}$$

Output increases in exactly the same proportion as inputs.

Decreasing returns to scale (DRS)

$$\boxed{f(sz)<s f(z)}$$

Output increases less than proportionally to inputs.

Homogeneous production functions

A production function is homogeneous of degree $\nu$ if:

$$\boxed{f(sz_1,sz_2)=s^\nu f(z_1,z_2)\qquad\text{for every }s>0}$$

Determine returns to scale: substitute $sz_1,sz_2$ into $f$, factor out the power of $s$, and compare its exponent $\nu$ with one (for positive output).

$$\boxed{\begin{aligned}\nu>1&\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\text{Increasing returns to scale},\\\nu=1&\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\text{Constant returns to scale},\\\nu<1&\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\text{Decreasing returns to scale}.\end{aligned}}$$

Elasticity of scale

The percentage change in output associated with a 1% increase in every input:

$$\boxed{E=\frac{\partial f(sz)}{\partial s}\frac{s}{f(sz)}}$$

For a homogeneous production function, the elasticity is constant and equals its degree:

$$\boxed{E=\nu}$$

Marginal products under scaling

$$\boxed{f_i'(sz)=s^{\nu-1}f_i'(z)}$$

If $f$ is differentiable and homogeneous of degree $\nu$, its marginal products are homogeneous of degree $\nu-1$.

MRTS under scaling

$$\boxed{MRTS_{12}(sz)=MRTS_{12}(z)}$$

For homogeneous $f$, scaling all inputs leaves MRTS unchanged.

02Diminishing Marginal Product

Test: differentiate twice with respect to the same input, holding the others fixed.

$$\boxed{\frac{\partial MP_1}{\partial z_1}=\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial z_1^2}<0}$$

Find the condition: simplify the second derivative, identify its positive factors, and determine when the remaining factor is negative.

Do not use the returns-to-scale test here: one input changes, not all inputs. Diminishing marginal product does not imply decreasing returns to scale.

03Elasticity of Substitution

The percentage change in the input ratio $z_2/z_1$ associated with a 1% change in MRTS, along a given isoquant. For positive inputs and positive MRTS:

$$\boxed{\sigma=\frac{d\ln(z_2/z_1)}{d\ln MRTS_{12}}}$$

High $\sigma$: easier substitution. Low $\sigma$: harder substitution.

Calculate: set $r=z_2/z_1$, express MRTS in terms of $r$, and take logarithms. Differentiate with respect to $\ln r$, then take the reciprocal:

$$\boxed{\sigma=\left(\frac{d\ln MRTS_{12}}{d\ln r}\right)^{-1}}$$

$E$: output response when all inputs are scaled. $\sigma$: substitution between inputs at fixed output.

3.2 — Profit Maximization

Optimal inputs, output supply and maximum profit.

01The Profit Maximization Problem (PMP)

Choose the inputs $z=(z_1,z_2)$ that maximize profit at given output and input prices.

$$\boxed{\max_{z\geq0}\;\big[pf(z)-w\cdot z\big]}$$

Revenue: $pf(z_1,z_2)$.

Input expenditure: $w\cdot z=w_1z_1+w_2z_2$.

Solution: $z^\pi(p,w)$, the optimal inputs as functions of output and input prices. Output is chosen through $y^*=f(z^\pi)$.

Interior first-order conditions

With differentiable production and positive optimal inputs:

$$\frac{\partial\pi}{\partial z_i}=pMP_i-w_i=0$$
$$\boxed{\begin{cases} pMP_1=w_1,\\ pMP_2=w_2. \end{cases}}$$

The value of each input's marginal product equals its price.

If $pMP_i>w_i$, increasing input $i$ raises profit. If $pMP_i<w_i$, reducing it raises profit at an interior bundle.

Together, these conditions determine the interior input demands $z_1^\pi(p,w)$ and $z_2^\pi(p,w)$.

Interior FOCs are necessary, but do not by themselves establish a maximum. A corner solution or unbounded profit is also possible.

At fixed prices and holding the other input constant, for $i=1,2$:

$$\frac{\partial\pi}{\partial z_i} =p\frac{\partial f}{\partial z_i}-w_i =pMP_i-w_i.$$

At an interior maximum, small increases and decreases in each input are feasible, so each derivative must be zero:

$$\begin{gathered}pMP_i-w_i=0\\\Longrightarrow\quad pMP_i=w_i.\end{gathered}$$

Output is $f(z)$ and is not fixed in advance, so this problem has no output constraint or associated Lagrange multiplier. These equalities apply to positive inputs.

Input demand, output supply and profit

ResultMeaning / calculation
$z^\pi(p,w)$Unconditional input demand: the chosen inputs, with no fixed output target.
$y^*(p,w)=f(z^\pi(p,w))$Output supply: production obtained from those inputs.
$\pi(p,w)$Maximized profit: the value of the objective at an optimal bundle.
$$\boxed{\begin{aligned} \pi(p,w)&=pf(z^\pi(p,w))\\ &\quad-w\cdot z^\pi(p,w) \end{aligned}}$$

These expressions use a finite optimum when one exists; optimal inputs and output need not be unique.

02When Interior FOCs Are Not Enough

Perfect substitutes

$$f(z_1,z_2)=2\sqrt{z_1+z_2}$$

Both inputs contribute equally to the sum. The firm uses only the cheaper input; equal prices allow several optimal splits.

Perfect complements

$$f(z_1,z_2)=2\sqrt{\min(z_1,z_2)}$$

At strictly positive input prices, optimal inputs satisfy $z_1=z_2=z$. Excess units of either input add cost without increasing output.

Constant returns and production scale

If long-run cost is $C(w,y)=c(w)y$, where $c(w)$ is unit cost:

$$\boxed{\pi(y)=[p-c(w)]y}$$
  • $p<c(w)$: $y^*=0$, with zero profit.
  • $p=c(w)$: any scale is optimal, with zero profit, using cost-minimizing inputs.
  • $p>c(w)$: profit is unbounded; there is no finite optimum in the unconstrained model.

CES production: $(z_1^\rho+z_2^\rho)^{1/\rho}$ has constant returns for $0<\rho<1$. The interior input ratio determines proportions; scale depends on the three price cases above. At $\rho=1$, the inputs are perfect substitutes. The displayed formula is undefined at $\rho=0$.

3.3 — Cost Minimization and Cost Functions

Conditional input demands, cost functions and output decisions.

01Cost Minimization: From Input Choice to Cost

Choose the least expensive input bundle that produces the target output $\bar y$.

$$\boxed{\begin{aligned} \min_{z_1,z_2\geq0}\quad&w_1z_1+w_2z_2\\ \text{s.t.}\quad&f(z_1,z_2)\geq\bar y \end{aligned}}$$

With positive input prices, a continuous increasing production function, and a feasible positive target, the output constraint binds:

$$f(z_1,z_2)=\bar y.$$

Solution: $z^C(w,\bar y)$, the optimal inputs as functions of input prices and target output.

Interior solution: differentiable production, positive inputs and positive marginal products.

Lagrangian

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal L&=w_1z_1+w_2z_2\\ &\quad+\lambda[\bar y-f(z_1,z_2)] \end{aligned}$$

The first-order conditions are:

$$\begin{cases} w_1-\lambda MP_1=0,\\ w_2-\lambda MP_2=0,\\ f(z_1,z_2)=\bar y. \end{cases}$$

The ratio of the first two conditions eliminates $\lambda$ and gives the MRTS condition.

Interior optimum

Tangency and the output constraint give:

$$\boxed{\begin{cases} MRTS_{12}=\dfrac{MP_1}{MP_2}=\dfrac{w_1}{w_2}\\[8pt] f(z_1,z_2)=\bar y \end{cases}}$$

The technical rate of substitution equals the input-price ratio.

The input ratio and output target jointly determine the conditional input demands.

Tangency applies to an interior optimum. Perfect substitutes may give a corner solution; perfect complements require fixed input proportions.

For $f(K,L)=AK^\alpha L^\beta$, with positive $A$, $\alpha$, $\beta$ and input prices, marginal products are:

$$\begin{aligned} MP_K&=A\alpha K^{\alpha-1}L^\beta,\\ MP_L&=A\beta K^\alpha L^{\beta-1}. \end{aligned}$$

The tangency condition gives the optimal input ratio:

$$\begin{gathered} \frac{MP_K}{MP_L}=\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\frac{L}{K}=\frac{w_K}{w_L}\\ \Longrightarrow\quad\boxed{K=\frac{\alpha w_L}{\beta w_K}L}. \end{gathered}$$

Writing $r=\alpha w_L/(\beta w_K)$, the output constraint becomes:

$$\bar y=A(rL)^\alpha L^\beta=Ar^\alpha L^{\alpha+\beta}.$$

Hence the conditional input demands and minimum cost are:

$$\begin{gathered} \boxed{L^C=\left(\frac{\bar y}{Ar^\alpha}\right)^{1/(\alpha+\beta)}}\\[4pt] \boxed{K^C=rL^C}. \end{gathered}$$
$$\begin{aligned} C(w,\bar y)&=w_KK^C+w_LL^C\\ &=(w_Kr+w_L)L^C. \end{aligned}$$

Conditional input demands and the cost function

$z^C(w,\bar y)$ gives the cheapest quantities of inputs for the target. $C(w,\bar y)$ gives the resulting minimum expenditure:

$$\boxed{\begin{aligned} C(w,\bar y)&=w_1z_1^C(w,\bar y)\\ &\quad+w_2z_2^C(w,\bar y) \end{aligned}}$$

With input prices held fixed, write $TC(y)=C(w,y)$. The demand functions and the cost function are different answers: quantities versus money.

The multiplier measures marginal cost

$$\boxed{\lambda^*=\frac{\partial C(w,\bar y)}{\partial\bar y}=MC(\bar y)}$$

Where the value function is differentiable, the Envelope Theorem gives the extra minimum cost required by a small increase in the output target.

At fixed input prices and a differentiable interior optimum:

$$\frac{\partial C}{\partial\bar y} =w_1\frac{\partial z_1^C}{\partial\bar y} +w_2\frac{\partial z_2^C}{\partial\bar y}.$$

The FOCs give $w_i=\lambda^*MP_i$, so:

$$\frac{\partial C}{\partial\bar y} =\lambda^*\sum_{i=1}^{2}MP_i\frac{\partial z_i^C}{\partial\bar y}.$$

Differentiating the binding constraint $f(z^C(w,\bar y))=\bar y$ makes the sum equal to $1$. Thus $\partial C/\partial\bar y=\lambda^*$, the envelope result.

02From Minimum Cost to Maximum Profit

PMP — output is chosen

$$\max_{z\geq0}\;pf(z)-w\cdot z$$

$z^\pi(p,w)$ depends on the output price as well as input prices.

CmP — output is given

$$\min_{z\geq0}\;w\cdot z \quad\text{s.t. }f(z)\geq\bar y$$

$z^C(w,\bar y)$ is conditional on the output target.

Using the cost function, profit maximization becomes:

$$\begin{gathered} \boxed{\max_{y\geq0}\;py-C(w,y)}\\[4pt] \boxed{p=MC(y^*)} \end{gathered}$$

At an interior optimum, $\pi'(y)=p-MC(y)=0$. Rising marginal cost gives $\pi''(y)=-MC'(y)<0$, a local maximum. The global optimum yields at least as much profit as any other output, including zero.

$p=MC$ is a condition, not a guarantee of a maximum. Every profit-maximizing input bundle is cost-minimizing for its own output; otherwise a cheaper way to produce that output would raise profit. A cost-minimizing bundle at an arbitrary target need not maximize profit.

With $z_1$ variable and $\bar z_2$ fixed, hold input prices constant:

$$\boxed{TC(y)=VC(y)+FC}.$$

Here $VC(y)=w_1z_1(y;\bar z_2)$ and $FC=w_2\bar z_2$.

$$\boxed{MC=\frac{w_1}{MP_1}}$$

This applies with one variable input, fixed input price $w_1$, and $MP_1>0$. Rising marginal product lowers marginal cost; falling marginal product raises it.

Marginal productivity may rise initially as production becomes more effective, then fall as fixed inputs constrain expansion. This can produce a U-shaped $MC$ curve; fixed costs alone do not imply that shape.

With fixed input prices, $TC(y)=w_1z_1(y;\bar z_2)+w_2\bar z_2$ gives $MC=w_1\,dz_1/dy$.

Along $y=f(z_1,\bar z_2)$, $dy/dz_1=MP_1>0$, so $dz_1/dy=1/MP_1$. Hence:

$$MC=w_1\frac{dz_1}{dy}=\frac{w_1}{MP_1}.$$

A more productive input requires fewer extra units to produce one more unit of output.

Fixed capital: $f(\bar K,L)=\bar y$ determines the labor required for a target output. Short-run profit is $pf(\bar K,L)-w_LL-w_K\bar K$; the fixed capital cost does not affect the labor FOC.

05Shutdown versus Zero Profit

In the short run, fixed costs remain payable at zero output and $VC(0)=0$.

Shutdown threshold — cover variable costs

$$\boxed{p^s=\min_{y>0}AVC(y)}$$

Shutting down gives $\pi(0)=-FC$. At a positive output:

$$\pi(y)-\pi(0)=py-VC(y).$$

Production is better than shutdown when $TR>VC$, or $p>AVC(y)$. At equality, the firm is indifferent.

Zero-profit threshold — cover total costs

$$\boxed{p^{zp}=\min_{y>0}AC(y)}$$

At any positive output:

$$\begin{gathered} \pi(y)=y[p-AC(y)],\\ \pi(y)=0\iff p=AC(y). \end{gathered}$$

At a minimum-AC output, zero profit therefore requires $p=\min AC$.

06Long-Run Costs and Economies of Scale

All inputs can adjust

Long-run cost selects the cheapest fixed-input level for each output. Average cost is the lower envelope of the short-run curves:

$$\boxed{AC_{LR}(y)=\min_{\bar z_2}AC_{SR}(y;\bar z_2)}.$$

A tangency identifies the plant best suited to that output; it need not be the minimum of that plant's own short-run curve.

Minimum efficient scale

The smallest output attaining minimum long-run average cost. If the minimum is a flat interval, minimum efficient scale is its left endpoint.

This identifies an efficient scale in cost terms, not the quantity that maximizes profit at every price.

Cost elasticity

At fixed input prices, cost elasticity measures the percentage change in minimum total cost for a 1% increase in output:

$$\boxed{ E^c=\frac{d\ln C}{d\ln y} =\frac{dC}{dy}\frac yC =\frac{MC}{AC} }.$$
ConditionCost interpretation
$E^c<1$ / $MC<AC$Economies of scale: average cost decreases.
$E^c=1$ / $MC=AC$Locally stationary average cost.
$E^c>1$ / $MC>AC$Diseconomies of scale: average cost increases.

For a technology homogeneous of degree $\nu>0$, fixed input prices, and the standard long-run cost-minimization setup:

$$\boxed{E^c=\frac1\nu}.$$

IRS therefore give economies of scale; CRS give constant average cost; DRS give diseconomies of scale. This connects the technology results in 3.1 to cost behavior.

Equality $MC=AC$ at one output only makes $AC$ stationary there. It does not establish globally constant returns to scale.

Homogeneity means that scaling every input by $t^{1/\nu}$ multiplies output by $t>0$. At fixed input prices, expenditure scales by $t^{1/\nu}$.

Scaling a cheapest bundle gives a feasible bundle for the new target. A cheaper alternative could be scaled back to contradict the original minimum. Therefore:

$$C(w,ty)=t^{1/\nu}C(w,y).$$

With $k=C(w,1)>0$, cost is $C(w,y)=k y^{1/\nu}$, so:

$$\begin{aligned} MC&=\frac{k}{\nu}y^{1/\nu-1},\\ AC&=k y^{1/\nu-1}. \end{aligned}$$

The ratio gives $E^c=MC/AC=1/\nu$ for positive output.

3.4 — Efficiency

Output efficiency, technical efficiency and economic efficiency.

01Output, Technical and Economic Efficiency

Output efficiency · Given inputs

The firm cannot produce more output with the same inputs.

$$\boxed{y=f(z)}$$

The output produced reaches the maximum allowed by the technology.

Technical efficiency · Given output

The firm cannot produce the same output with less of at least one input and no more of any other input.

With several inputs, this means reducing inputs component by component, rather than adding their quantities together.

Economic efficiency · Given output and input prices

For a given output and given input prices, a firm is economically efficient when no feasible input combination costs less.

$$\boxed{w\cdot z^*=C(w,y)}$$

The cost of the chosen bundle must equal the minimum cost of producing $y$.

For strictly positive input prices:

$$\boxed{\begin{gathered} \text{Economic efficiency}\\ \Longrightarrow\;\text{Technical efficiency} \end{gathered}}.$$

If an input could be reduced without increasing another or lowering output, cost would fall. The original bundle could not be cost-minimizing.

The converse does not hold. A technically efficient firm may still use an expensive input mix. It can sometimes reduce cost by using more of a cheaper input and less of an expensive input, while keeping output unchanged.

02The Production Side of the Welfare Theorems

Here $a\in Y$ is a net-output vector: outputs are positive components and inputs are negative components. At price vector $\mathbf p$, profit is $\mathbf p\cdot a$. A plan is efficient if no feasible $a'\geq a$, $a'\neq a$ improves one component without worsening another.

First result — profit maximization implies efficiency

If $a^*$ maximizes profit at strictly positive prices, it is efficient:

$$\boxed{\begin{gathered} \mathbf p\gg0,\quad a^*\in\arg\max_{a\in Y}\mathbf p\cdot a\\ \Longrightarrow\;a^*\text{ efficient} \end{gathered}}.$$

Any feasible improvement would raise profit. Convexity is not required for this direction.

Profit maximization gives $\mathbf p\cdot a^*\geq\mathbf p\cdot a'$ for every $a'\in Y$.

If $a^*$ were inefficient, a feasible $a'\geq a^*$ would exist with at least one strictly larger component. Strictly positive prices imply:

$$\begin{gathered} \mathbf p\cdot(a'-a^*)\\ =\sum_i p_i(a_i'-a_i^*)>0. \end{gathered}$$

This contradicts profit maximization, so $a^*$ is efficient.

If a price were zero, improving only that component might leave profit unchanged. This is why strict positivity matters.

Second result — efficiency admits supporting prices

If the production set $Y$ is convex, an efficient plan $a^*$ maximizes profit at some nonzero, nonnegative price vector:

$$\boxed{\begin{gathered} \exists\,\mathbf p\geq0,\quad\mathbf p\neq0:\\ \mathbf p\cdot a^*\geq\mathbf p\cdot a \quad\text{for all }a\in Y. \end{gathered}}$$

These are some supporting prices, not necessarily the prevailing market prices. Some components can be zero.

Convexity permits a supporting price hyperplane at the efficient plan. Every feasible production plan lies on or below its profit level:

$$\begin{gathered} \mathbf p\cdot(a-a^*)\leq0\\ \text{for every }a\in Y. \end{gathered}$$

Rearranging gives $\mathbf p\cdot a\leq\mathbf p\cdot a^*$, exactly the profit-maximization condition. Without convexity, an efficient plan need not have such supporting prices.

Different directions, different hypotheses: strictly positive given prices make profit maximization imply efficiency; convexity makes supporting prices exist for an efficient plan. These are production-side results, not a full statement of general-equilibrium welfare theorems.
