Choice Functions & Choice Rules

01

Rationality & Utility Representability

A. Utility Representability $\implies$ Rationality

Part 1: Completeness

Since $u(x), u(y) \in \mathbb{R}$, either $u(x) \ge u(y)$ or $u(y) \ge u(x)$.

Therefore, $x \succeq y$ or $y \succeq x$. Hence $\succeq$ is complete.

Part 2: Transitivity

Suppose $x \succeq y$ and $y \succeq z$.

Then $u(x) \ge u(y)$ and $u(y) \ge u(z)$, which implies $u(x) \ge u(z)$ by transitivity of $\ge$ on $\mathbb{R}$.

Therefore, $x \succeq z$. Hence $\succeq$ is transitive. $\blacksquare$

B. Rationality $\implies$ Utility Representability

Does rationality guarantee utility representability?

Suppose that $\succeq$ is rational, meaning that it is complete and transitive.

CASE 1 — FINITE CHOICE SET

If $X$ is finite, all alternatives can be ranked consistently because preferences are complete and transitive.

We can assign real numbers to alternatives according to their ranking, assigning the same utility to indifferent alternatives.

Thus, there exists a utility function $u:X\to\mathbb{R}$ such that:

$$x \succeq y \iff u(x)\geq u(y)$$

Therefore:

$$\boxed{\text{Rationality}\implies\text{Utility Representability}}$$

CASE 2 — INFINITE CHOICE SET

Suppose $X$ is infinite and $\succeq$ is rational (complete and transitive).

1. Rationality alone

Completeness and transitivity do not exclude discontinuous preferences (sudden jumps): even a very small change in an alternative may lead to a sudden change in its ranking.

Therefore:

$$\boxed{\text{Rationality}\not\implies\text{Representability in general}}$$

2. Adding Continuity

Continuity prevents sudden jumps in preferences: small changes in alternatives cannot reverse a strict preference.

On $X = \mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}$, we obtain:

$$\underbrace{\text{Complete + Transitive}}_{\text{Rationality}}+\text{Continuous}$$
$$\Downarrow$$
$$\boxed{\text{Continuous Utility Representability}}$$

Remark.

Continuity is sufficient, but not necessary: some discontinuous preferences are still representable.

02

Property α ⇔ Rationalizability

Choice Functions

$$\boxed{\text{A choice function on } X \text{ is rationalizable } \iff \text{it satisfies Property } \alpha}$$
Direction 1: Rationalizable $\implies$ Property α
Starting point: Suppose $C$ is rationalizable.
Then there exists a rational preference relation $\succeq$ such that, for every choice set $A$, $C(A)$ is the unique best alternative in $A$.

Let $C$ be a rationalizable choice function on $X$.

Then there exists a rational preference relation $\succeq$ such that, for every choice set $A$, $C(A)$ is the unique best alternative in $A$:

$$C(A) \succ y \qquad \forall y \in A, \ y \neq C(A)$$

Assume $B \subset A$ and $C(A) \in B$.

Since $B \subset A$, it is also true that:

$$C(A) \succ y \qquad \forall y \in B, \ y \neq C(A)$$

Hence $C(A)$ is also the unique best alternative in $B$, so:

$$\boxed{C(B) = C(A)}$$

Thus, $C$ satisfies Property $\alpha$. $\blacksquare$

Direction 2: Property α $\implies$ Rationalizable

Starting point: Let $X$ be a choice set and $c$ a choice function on $X$. Suppose $c$ satisfies Property α.
We construct a preference relation from pairwise choices, then show that it is rational and generates exactly the choices made by $c$.

Define a binary relation $\succeq$ by:

$$x \succeq y \iff c(\{x, y\}) = x$$

Show that this relation is a rational preference relation.

(Note: We are defining a particular preference relation from $c$ to show that $c$ is rationalizable.)

Show that for every choice set $A$, $c(A) \succeq a$ for every $a \in A$. In other words, that the chosen alternative is the unique best alternative according to $\succeq$.

Part (a) — Completeness

Pick any $x, y \in X$. Since $c$ is a single-valued choice function on the two-element choice set $\{x, y\}$:

$$c(\{x, y\}) \in \{x, y\}$$

Therefore, either:

$$c(\{x, y\}) = x \implies x \succeq y \qquad\text{or}\qquad c(\{x, y\}) = y \implies y \succeq x$$

Hence:

$$\boxed{\succeq \text{ is complete}}$$
Part (b) — Transitivity

Assume $x \succeq y$ and $y \succeq z$. By definition of $\succeq$:

$$c(\{x, y\}) = x \qquad\text{and}\qquad c(\{y, z\}) = y$$

Consider the choice set $\{x, y, z\}$. Since $c$ selects an element from $\{x, y, z\}$:

$$c(\{x, y, z\}) \in \{x, y, z\}$$

We check the three possibilities:

• If $c(\{x, y, z\}) = y$: since $\{x, y\} \subset \{x, y, z\}$ and $y \in \{x, y\}$, Property α implies $c(\{x, y\}) = y$, which contradicts $c(\{x, y\}) = x$.

• If $c(\{x, y, z\}) = z$: since $\{y, z\} \subset \{x, y, z\}$ and $z \in \{y, z\}$, Property α implies $c(\{y, z\}) = z$, which contradicts $c(\{y, z\}) = y$.

• Therefore, we must have: $c(\{x, y, z\}) = x$.

Now consider the pair $\{x, z\} \subset \{x, y, z\}$. Since $x \in \{x, z\}$, Property α implies:

$$c(\{x, z\}) = c(\{x, y, z\}) = x$$

By definition of $\succeq$, $c(\{x, z\}) = x \implies \mathbf{x \succeq z}$.

Hence:

$$\boxed{\succeq \text{ is transitive}}$$
Conclusion

Since $\succeq$ is both complete and transitive:

$$\boxed{\succeq \text{ is a rational preference relation}}$$
Goal. For every choice set $A$,
$$\boxed{c(A) \succeq a \qquad \forall a \in A.}$$
In other words, the alternative chosen from $A$ must be the unique best alternative according to $\succeq$.
Proof by Contradiction

Take any choice set $A$, with:

$$c(A) \in A.$$

Suppose by contradiction that $c(A)$ is not the best alternative in $A$.

Then there exists some:

$$a \in A, \qquad a \neq c(A),$$

such that:

$$a \succeq c(A).$$

By the definition of the preference relation:

$$a \succeq c(A) \iff c(\{a, c(A)\}) = a.$$

Hence:

$$\boxed{c(\{a, c(A)\}) = a.}$$
Contradiction via Property α

However,

$$\{a, c(A)\} \subseteq A \qquad\text{and}\qquad c(A) \in \{a, c(A)\}.$$

Since $c(A)$ is chosen from the larger set $A$, Property α implies that it must remain chosen in every smaller set that still contains it:

$$\boxed{c(\{a, c(A)\}) = c(A).}$$

This contradicts: $c(\{a, c(A)\}) = a$.

Therefore, the assumption was false: there cannot exist any $a \in A$ that is preferred to $c(A)$.

Hence:

$$\boxed{c(A) \succeq a \qquad \forall a \in A.}$$
Uniqueness & Strict Preference

Moreover, for every $a \neq c(A)$, the choice function selects only one element from the pair:

$$c(\{a, c(A)\}) = c(A) \implies a \not\succeq c(A).$$

Therefore:

$$c(A) \succ a \qquad \forall a \in A, \ a \neq c(A).$$

Thus:

$$\boxed{c(A) \text{ is the unique best alternative in } A.}$$
Conclusion

Therefore, the rational preference relation $\succeq$ generates exactly the choices made by $c$, and:

$$\boxed{c \text{ is rationalizable.}}$$
03

HARP ⇔ Rationalizability

Choice Rules

$$\boxed{\text{Given a finite set } X, \text{ a choice rule } C \text{ is rationalizable } \iff C \text{ satisfies HARP.}}$$
Direction 1: Rationalizable $\implies$ HARP
Starting point: Suppose $C$ is rationalizable.
Then, by definition, there exists a rational preference relation $\succeq$ such that $C(A)$ contains the maximal alternatives of every choice set $A$.

Suppose $C$ is rationalizable (by a rational preference relation $\succeq$).

Consider $a, b \in A \cap B$ such that $a \in C(A)$ and $b \in C(B)$:

• Since $a \in C(A)$ and $b \in A$, we have: $a \succeq b$.

• Similarly, since $b \in C(B)$ and $a \in B$, we have: $b \succeq a$.

Hence: $a \sim b$.

Since $b$ is maximal in $B$ and $a \sim b$, $a$ is also maximal in $B$, so: $a \in C(B)$.

Similarly, since $a$ is maximal in $A$ and $b \sim a$, $b$ is also maximal in $A$, so: $b \in C(A)$.

Thus, $C$ satisfies HARP. $\blacksquare$

Direction 2: HARP $\implies$ Rationalizable
Starting point: Suppose $C$ satisfies HARP.
Now we do not yet have a preference relation: we construct one from observed choices, and then prove it is rational.

Define the revealed preference relation $\succeq$ by:

$$\boxed{x \succeq y \iff \exists A \ni x, y \text{ such that } x \in C(A)}$$

i.e. $x \succeq y$ iff we have observed $x$ being chosen when $y$ was available.

Let's show that $\succeq$ is complete and transitive, and that $C(\cdot) = C(\cdot, \succeq)$:

Pick any $x, y \in X$.

Since $C(\{x, y\}) \neq \varnothing$, either:

$$x \in C(\{x, y\}) \quad \text{or} \quad y \in C(\{x, y\}) \quad \text{(or both).}$$

Hence:

$$x \succeq y \quad \text{or} \quad y \succeq x$$
$$\boxed{\succeq \text{ is complete}}$$

Suppose $x \succeq y$ and $y \succeq z$, and let $A = \{x, y, z\}$. Let's show that $x \succeq z$.

Since $C(A) \neq \varnothing$, at least one of $x, y, z$ belongs to $C(A)$:

(a) If $x \in C(A)$:

Then, by definition, $x \succeq z$.

(b) If $y \in C(A)$:

Recall that $x \succeq y$, hence $\exists B \ni x, y$ such that $x \in C(B)$.
Since $y \in C(A)$, $x \in C(B)$, and $x, y \in A \cap B$, HARP implies: $x \in C(A)$.
And we are back to (a).

(c) If $z \in C(A)$:

Recall that $y \succeq z$, hence $\exists D \ni y, z$ such that $y \in C(D)$.
Since $z \in C(A)$, $y \in C(D)$, and $y, z \in A \cap D$, HARP implies: $y \in C(A)$.
And we are back to (b) (which in turn leads to (a)).

Therefore: $x \succeq z$.

$$\boxed{\succeq \text{ is transitive}}$$
$$z \in C(A) \implies y \in C(A) \implies x \in C(A) \implies x \succeq z$$

Chosen alternatives are exactly the maximal alternatives:

First inclusion $C(A) \subseteq C(A, \succeq)$:

Suppose $y \in C(A)$.
Then for every $z \in A$, by definition of revealed preference: $y \succeq z$.
Hence $y$ is maximal in $A$, so $y \in C(A, \succeq)$.
Therefore:

$$\boxed{C(A) \subseteq C(A, \succeq)}$$
Second inclusion $C(A, \succeq) \subseteq C(A)$:

Now suppose $y \in C(A, \succeq)$.
Choose any $w \in C(A)$.
Since $y$ is maximal: $y \succeq w$.
By definition of revealed preference, there exists a choice set $B$ containing $y, w$ such that $y \in C(B)$.
Since $w \in C(A)$, $y \in C(B)$, and $y, w \in A \cap B$, HARP implies: $y \in C(A)$.
Therefore:

$$\boxed{C(A, \succeq) \subseteq C(A)}$$
$$\boxed{C(A) = C(A, \succeq)}$$

Therefore, $C$ is rationalizable. $\blacksquare$

Midterm Sheet · Definitions & Properties

Consumer Preferences

01

Monotonicity

$X=\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}$

Strict Monotonicity

$$x\geq y,\quad x\neq y\implies x\succ y$$

If bundle $x$ contains at least as much of every good as bundle $y$, and strictly more of at least one good, then $x$ is strictly preferred to $y$.

Weak Monotonicity

$$x\geq y\implies x\succeq y$$

If bundle $x$ contains at least as much of every good as bundle $y$, then $x$ is at least as good as $y$. The consumer may strictly prefer $x$ or be indifferent between $x$ and $y$.

Local Non-Satiation (LNS)

$$\forall x\in X,\ \forall\varepsilon>0,\quad \exists y\in X:\quad \|x-y\|<\varepsilon,\quad y\succ x$$

For every bundle $x$, there exists another bundle $y$, arbitrarily close to $x$, that is strictly preferred to $x$.

Relationship Between Monotonicity and LNS

$$\boxed{\text{Strict Monotonicity}\implies\text{LNS}}$$

Strict monotonicity guarantees LNS because even the smallest positive increase in one good, without reducing the others, makes the new bundle strictly better.

$$\boxed{\text{Weak Monotonicity}\not\implies\text{LNS}}$$

Weak monotonicity does not guarantee LNS because having more goods may leave the consumer indifferent, whereas LNS requires a strictly preferred nearby bundle.

02

Convexity

Convex Preferences

$$x\succeq z,\quad y\succeq z \implies tx+(1-t)y\succeq z$$

For $t\in[0,1]$.

If bundles $x$ and $y$ are both at least as good as bundle $z$, then any weighted average of $x$ and $y$ is also at least as good as $z$.

Strict Convexity

$$x\succeq z,\quad y\succeq z \implies tx+(1-t)y\succ z$$

For $x\neq y$ and $t\in(0,1)$.

If two distinct bundles $x$ and $y$ are both at least as good as bundle $z$, then any weighted average that gives positive weight to both bundles is strictly preferred to $z$.

Implication of Convexity

$$x\succ y \implies tx+(1-t)y\succeq y$$

For $t\in[0,1]$.

If bundle $x$ is strictly preferred to bundle $y$, then any weighted average of $x$ and $y$ is at least as good as $y$.

03

Homotheticity

Homothetic Preferences

$$x\succeq y \iff \lambda x\succeq\lambda y \qquad \lambda>0$$

If bundle $x$ is at least as good as bundle $y$, multiplying every quantity in both bundles by the same positive factor $\lambda$ does not change their preference ranking, and vice versa.

04

Separability

Separable Preferences

$$(x,y)\succeq(x',y) \iff (x,y')\succeq(x',y')$$

If $(x,y)$ is at least as good as $(x',y)$, replacing the common component $y$ with $y'$ in both bundles does not change the preference ranking, and vice versa.

The preference ranking between $x$ and $x'$ does not depend on the common value of the other component.

05

Numéraire Properties

Here, $t$ represents money, while $y$ represents other goods.

1. The Numéraire Good Is Valuable

$$(t,y)\succeq(t',y) \iff t\geq t'$$

When the other goods $y$ remain unchanged, the consumer prefers the alternative with more money. If the money amounts are equal, the consumer is indifferent.

2. Compensation Is Possible

$$\forall y,y'\in Y,\quad \exists t\in\mathbb R: (0,y)\sim(t,y')$$

For any two bundles of other goods $y$ and $y'$, there exists an amount of money $t$ that makes the consumer indifferent between $(0,y)$ and $(t,y')$.

3. No Wealth Effect

$$(t,y)\succeq(t',y') \iff (t+d,y)\succeq(t'+d,y')$$

Adding the same amount of money $d$ to both alternatives does not change their preference ranking, even if their initial money amounts $t$ and $t'$ are different.

06

Quasi-Linear Utility

Numéraire Properties ⇔ Quasi-Linear Utility

For rational preferences on $\mathbb R\times Y$, the three numéraire properties hold if and only if preferences can be represented by:

$$\boxed{u(t,y)=t+v(y)}$$

Utility is quasi-linear in money: total utility equals the amount of money $t$ plus the utility $v(y)$ derived from the other goods.

Starting Point / Hypotheses:
We assume rational preferences on $\mathbb{R}\times Y$ satisfying the three numéraire properties: (1) $t$ is valuable, (2) Compensation is possible, and (3) No wealth effect.
Goal: Construct $v:Y\to\mathbb{R}$ so that $u(t,y)=t+v(y)$ represents the preference relation.
1
Because compensation is possible (Property 2).
Fix an arbitrary reference bundle $\bar{y} \in Y$. Because compensation is possible, for every $y \in Y$, there exists $v(y) \in \mathbb{R}$, the money amount that makes the reference alternative $\bar{y}$ equivalent to $y$:
$$(0, y) \sim (v(y), \bar{y})$$
Similarly, for $y'\in Y$:
$$(0, y') \sim (v(y'), \bar{y})$$
2
Because there is no wealth effect (Property 3).
Indifference means that both weak comparisons hold. Apply property 3 to each comparison, adding $t$ to both alternatives:
$$(t, y) \sim (t + v(y), \bar{y})$$
Similarly, adding $t'$ preserves indifference:
$$(t', y') \sim (t' + v(y'), \bar{y})$$
3
Hence, compare the monetary equivalents.
By transitivity of $\succeq$, comparing $(t, y)$ and $(t', y')$ is equivalent to comparing their monetary equivalents at $\bar{y}$:
$$\begin{gathered} (t,y)\succeq(t',y')\\ \iff (t+v(y),\bar y)\succeq(t'+v(y'),\bar y). \end{gathered}$$
In each direction, replace the two bundles by their indifferent monetary equivalents and use transitivity.
4
Because the numéraire good is valuable (Property 1).
Because the numéraire good is valuable (Property 1), the last comparison can be rewritten purely in terms of the numéraire amounts:
$$\begin{gathered} (t,y)\succeq(t',y')\\ \iff t+v(y)\geq t'+v(y'). \end{gathered}$$
Hence $\succeq$ can be represented by the quasi-linear utility function $u(t, y) = t + v(y)$. $\blacksquare$
Midterm Sheet · Observed Choices

Revealed Preference Theory

01

Revealed Preferences

Revealed Preference (RP)

$X=(x_1,x_2)$ is chosen at prices $p=(p_1,p_2)$.

$$\boxed{p_1x_1+p_2x_2\geq p_1y_1+p_2y_2}$$

$X$ is revealed preferred to $Y$ because the consumer chooses $X$ even though $Y$ is affordable.

Strict Revealed Preference (SRP)

$X=(x_1,x_2)$ is chosen at prices $p=(p_1,p_2)$.

$$\boxed{p_1x_1+p_2x_2>p_1y_1+p_2y_2}$$

$X$ is strictly revealed preferred to $Y$ because the consumer chooses $X$ even though $Y$ is strictly cheaper.

02

Weak Axiom of Revealed Preference (WARP)

Definition — WARP

We observe two different bundles chosen at two different price vectors:

$X=(x_1,x_2)$ is chosen at prices $p=(p_1,p_2)$.

$Y=(y_1,y_2)$ is chosen at prices $q=(q_1,q_2)$.

If:

$$\boxed{p_1x_1+p_2x_2\geq p_1y_1+p_2y_2}$$

$X$ is revealed preferred to $Y$ because $Y$ was affordable when $X$ was chosen.

It must NOT be the case that

When $Y$ is chosen at prices $q$, $X$ cannot also be affordable.

$$\boxed{q_1y_1+q_2y_2\geq q_1x_1+q_2x_2}$$

It MUST be the case that

When $Y$ is chosen at prices $q$, $X$ must be too expensive to afford.

$$\boxed{q_1y_1+q_2y_2<q_1x_1+q_2x_2}$$

These are two equivalent ways of expressing the same WARP condition, not two separate definitions.

WARP — Key takeaway

If $X$ is revealed preferred to $Y$, then $Y$ cannot be revealed preferred to $X$.

The consumer cannot choose $X$ over an affordable $Y$, and then choose $Y$ over an affordable $X$.

Demonstration

Who was chosen in this proof?

Observation 1: $X^0$ was chosen at prices $p^0$ (the $X$ and $p$ above).

Observation 2: $X^1$ was chosen at prices $p^1$ (the $Y$ and $q$ above).

Starting point: Choices maximize the same strictly quasi-concave utility function on convex budget sets.
Goal: Show that these choices satisfy WARP. The proof follows the course notation $X^0,X^1$ and $p^0,p^1$: the superscripts identify the two observations.

1
Strictly quasi-concave utility + convex budget set ⇒ at most one utility-maximizing bundle. This is the uniqueness property used in the proof. Whenever a bundle is chosen as the maximizer, no different affordable bundle can give the same maximum utility.
2
Let $X^0$ maximize utility facing $p^0$, and $X^1$ maximize utility facing $p^1$, with $X^0\neq X^1$. These are two different bundles chosen under two sets of prices. We compare their costs using the first prices, then the second prices.
3
Suppose $X^0\;\mathrm{RP}\;X^1$. This means:
$$p^0\cdot X^0\geq p^0\cdot X^1.$$
At the first prices, $X^1$ costs no more than $X^0$, so $X^1$ was affordable when $X^0$ was chosen.
4
Hence, $u(X^0)>u(X^1)$. Choosing $X^0$ as a maximizer gives at least as much utility as the affordable $X^1$. The inequality is strict because the bundles differ and the maximum is unique: equal utility would make both bundles maximizers.
5
When $X^1$ is chosen, it must therefore be that
$$p^1\cdot X^0>p^1\cdot X^1.$$
If $X^0$ were affordable at the second observation, the consumer could buy a bundle with strictly higher utility than $X^1$, contradicting the choice of $X^1$ as a maximizer. Therefore $X^0$ must cost more than the amount spent on $X^1$. This is the WARP conclusion. $\blacksquare$
03

Direct & Indirect Revealed Preferences

Directly Revealed Preference (DRP)

$X=(x_1,x_2)$ is chosen at prices $p=(p_1,p_2)$.

$$\boxed{p_1x_1+p_2x_2\geq p_1y_1+p_2y_2}$$

$X$ is directly revealed preferred to $Y$ because the consumer chooses $X$ while $Y$ is affordable.

A single observed choice establishes the comparison.

Indirectly Revealed Preference (IRP)

Consider three different bundles $X$, $Y$, and $Z$.

  • $X$ is chosen when $Y$ is affordable.
  • $Y$ is chosen when $Z$ is affordable.

Therefore:

$$\boxed{X\;\mathrm{DRP}\;Y\;\mathrm{DRP}\;Z}$$

And consequently:

$$\boxed{X\;\mathrm{IRP}\;Z}$$

$X$ is indirectly revealed preferred to $Z$ through the intermediate bundle $Y$.

Key difference

DRP: The preference is revealed through one observed choice.

IRP: The preference is revealed through a chain of observed choices, connected by intermediate bundles.

04

Generalized Axiom of Revealed Preference (GARP)

Definition — GARP

Consider a sequence of bundles: $x^1,x^2,\ldots,x^K$.

Each bundle $x^k$ is chosen at prices $p^k$.

If:

$$\boxed{\begin{gathered} x^1\;\mathrm{DRP}\;x^2\\ x^2\;\mathrm{DRP}\;x^3\\ \vdots\\ x^{K-1}\;\mathrm{DRP}\;x^K \end{gathered}}$$

Then:

$$\boxed{x^1\;\mathrm{IRP}\;x^K}$$

It must NOT be the case that

When $x^K$ is chosen at prices $p^K$, $x^1$ cannot be strictly cheaper than $x^K$.

$$\boxed{p^K\cdot x^K>p^K\cdot x^1}$$

In other words: $x^K\text{ is not SRP to }x^1$.

It MUST be the case that

When $x^K$ is chosen at prices $p^K$, $x^1$ must cost at least as much as $x^K$.

$$\boxed{p^K\cdot x^1\geq p^K\cdot x^K}$$

GARP — Key takeaway

If $x^1$ is indirectly revealed preferred to $x^K$, then $x^K$ cannot be strictly revealed preferred to $x^1$.

GARP prevents a chain of revealed preferences from ending with a strict reversal.

WARP vs. GARP

WARP — Direct Contradictions

WARP examines two different bundles chosen at two different price vectors.

  • $X$ is chosen when $Y$ is affordable.
  • $Y$ is chosen at another price vector.

WARP requires that $X$ must not also be affordable when $Y$ is chosen.

WARP checks whether two observed choices directly contradict each other.

GARP — Direct & Indirect Contradictions

GARP examines a sequence of observed choices, which may involve several intermediate bundles.

  • $x^1$ is chosen when $x^2$ is affordable.
  • $x^2$ is chosen when $x^3$ is affordable.
  • This continues until $x^K$.

GARP requires that $x^K$ cannot be strictly revealed preferred to $x^1$.

GARP checks whether a chain of revealed preferences leads to a strict contradiction.

Key difference

$$\boxed{\text{WARP: Direct comparisons}}$$
$$\boxed{\text{GARP: Direct + Indirect comparisons}}$$

WARP focuses on two choices, whereas GARP can detect inconsistencies involving a longer chain of choice.

05

Afriat's Theorem

For a finite dataset $D$ of nonnegative bundles and strictly positive prices, the following statements are equivalent:

1

The dataset satisfies GARP.

The observed choices do not violate GARP.

$\Updownarrow$
2

The dataset can be rationalized by a locally non-satiated utility function.

There exists a utility function under which every observed bundle maximizes utility within its budget.

$\Updownarrow$
3

The dataset can be rationalized by a continuous, concave, and strictly monotone utility function.

There exists a utility function with these properties that also explains every observed choice.

Key takeaway

$$\boxed{\text{GARP}\iff\text{Utility Maximization}}$$

If the observed choices satisfy GARP, there exists a utility function that makes every chosen bundle optimal within its budget.

Conversely, choices generated by utility maximization with LNS satisfy GARP.

Important: We do not need to know the consumer's actual utility function. Afriat's Theorem guarantees that a suitable utility function exists.

Consumer Demand & Duality

I

Marshallian Demand

Utility Maximization Problem (UMP) · Fix income, maximize utility.

01The Utility Maximization Problem

Choose the affordable bundle that maximizes utility.

$$\boxed{\begin{aligned}\max_{x_1,x_2\geq0}\quad &u(x_1,x_2)\\\text{s.t.}\quad &p_1x_1+p_2x_2\leq y\end{aligned}}$$

Under local non-satiation, the budget binds:

$$p_1x_1+p_2x_2=y$$

Solution: $x_1^M(p,y)$ and $x_2^M(p,y)$, the optimal quantities as functions of prices and income.

02How to Solve the UMP

For a differentiable interior optimum with positive marginal utilities. Write $MU_i=\partial u/\partial x_i$.

Method 1 — Lagrangian
  1. Write the Lagrangian.
    $$\begin{aligned}\mathcal L&=u(x_1,x_2)\\&\quad+\lambda(y-p_1x_1-p_2x_2)\end{aligned}$$
  2. Compute the FOCs.
    $$\begin{cases}\dfrac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial x_1}=MU_1-\lambda p_1=0\\[5pt]\dfrac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial x_2}=MU_2-\lambda p_2=0\\[5pt]\dfrac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\lambda}=y-p_1x_1-p_2x_2=0\end{cases}$$
  3. Eliminate $\lambda$.

    $MU_1=\lambda p_1$ and $MU_2=\lambda p_2$. Divide:

    $$\frac{MU_1}{MU_2}=\frac{p_1}{p_2}$$
  4. Solve with the budget. Express one quantity in terms of the other and substitute into $p_1x_1+p_2x_2=y$.
  5. Obtain $x_1^M(p,y)$ and $x_2^M(p,y)$. Check that both quantities are nonnegative.
Method 2 — MRS Condition

Use the same interior condition directly:

$$\boxed{\begin{cases}MRS_{12}=\dfrac{MU_1}{MU_2}=\dfrac{p_1}{p_2}\\[8pt]p_1x_1+p_2x_2=y\end{cases}}$$
  1. Compute $MU_1$ and $MU_2$, then form the MRS.
  2. Set the MRS equal to the price ratio and express one quantity in terms of the other.
  3. Substitute into the budget constraint, solve for both demands, and check the solution.

Same condition, shorter route: this method skips the Lagrangian.

Exam reminder: an interior tangency may not exist for linear or kinked preferences. Check the relevant boundaries or kink separately.

03Corner Solutions

A corner has $x_1^*=0$ or $x_2^*=0$. Under local non-satiation, all income is spent on the other good.

Method 1 — Inspect the Utility Function

Set each good to zero in turn and examine utility.

$$\begin{gathered}u(x_1,x_2)=x_1^\alpha x_2^{1-\alpha}\\u(x_1,x_2)=\ln x_1+\beta\ln x_2\end{gathered}$$

For $0<\alpha<1$, $\beta>0$, positive income and prices.

No corner in these standard cases: a zero quantity gives zero Cobb–Douglas utility, while a positive bundle gives positive utility; logarithmic utility is undefined at zero.

If utility remains well-defined, as with $u=2x_1+x_2$, a corner may occur. Compare the options.

Method 2 — Check the Interior Candidate

First solve $MRS_{12}=p_1/p_2$ together with the budget constraint.

  • Both quantities positive: the interior candidate is feasible.
  • A quantity is negative: the candidate is infeasible. Set that quantity to zero and solve the corresponding boundary problem.
  • No tangency: compare the relevant corner candidates.

Final check: compare utility across feasible candidates and select the maximum.

Linear Utility — Compare Utility per Euro

For $u(x_1,x_2)=ax_1+bx_2$, with $a,b>0$:

$$\frac{a}{p_1}>\frac{b}{p_2}$$

Spend everything on good 1.

$$\frac{a}{p_1}<\frac{b}{p_2}$$

Spend everything on good 2.

$$\frac{a}{p_1}=\frac{b}{p_2}$$

Multiple optima: every bundle on the budget line.

04Indirect Utility Function

The maximum utility attainable at given prices and income.

$$v(p,y)=\max_{\substack{x\geq0\\p\cdot x\leq y}}u(x)$$

Compute: find Marshallian demand, substitute into the original utility function, then simplify.

$$\boxed{v(p,y)=u\big(x_1^M(p,y),x_2^M(p,y)\big)}$$

The final expression depends on prices and income, not consumption quantities.

05Roy’s Identity

Recover Marshallian demand from indirect utility.

$$\boxed{x_i^M(p,y)=-\frac{\partial v(p,y)/\partial p_i}{\partial v(p,y)/\partial y}}$$

Apply: differentiate $v$ with respect to $p_i$ and $y$, divide, and keep the minus sign.

$\displaystyle x_1^M=-\frac{v_{p_1}}{v_y}$$\displaystyle x_2^M=-\frac{v_{p_2}}{v_y}$

Where the relevant derivatives exist and $v_y>0$.

06Lagrange Multiplier — Envelope Theorem

For $\mathcal L=u(x)+\lambda(y-p\cdot x)$:

$$\boxed{\frac{\partial v(p,y)}{\partial y}=\lambda^*}$$

The multiplier is the marginal utility of income: it measures how much maximum attainable utility changes when income increases slightly.

II

Hicksian Demand

Expenditure Minimization Problem (EmP) · Fix utility, minimize expenditure.

01The Expenditure Minimization Problem

Choose the least expensive bundle that attains the required utility $\bar u$.

$$\boxed{\begin{aligned}\min_{x_1,x_2\geq0}\quad &p_1x_1+p_2x_2\\\text{s.t.}\quad &u(x_1,x_2)\geq\bar u\end{aligned}}$$

Under the standard course assumptions, the utility constraint binds:

$$u(x_1,x_2)=\bar u$$

Solution: $h_1(p,\bar u)$ and $h_2(p,\bar u)$, the optimal quantities as functions of prices and target utility, not income.

02How to Solve the EmP

For a differentiable interior optimum with positive marginal utilities.

Method 1 — Lagrangian
  1. Write the Lagrangian.
    $$\begin{aligned}\mathcal L&=p_1x_1+p_2x_2\\&\quad+\lambda[\bar u-u(x_1,x_2)]\end{aligned}$$
  2. Compute the FOCs.
    $$\begin{cases}\dfrac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial x_1}=p_1-\lambda MU_1=0\\[5pt]\dfrac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial x_2}=p_2-\lambda MU_2=0\\[5pt]\dfrac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\lambda}=\bar u-u(x_1,x_2)=0\end{cases}$$
  3. Eliminate $\lambda$.

    $p_1=\lambda MU_1$ and $p_2=\lambda MU_2$. Divide:

    $$\frac{MU_1}{MU_2}=\frac{p_1}{p_2}$$
  4. Solve with the utility constraint. Express one quantity in terms of the other and substitute into $u(x_1,x_2)=\bar u$.
  5. Obtain $h_1(p,\bar u)$ and $h_2(p,\bar u)$. Check nonnegativity and attainment of the target utility.
Method 2 — MRS Condition

Use the same interior condition directly:

$$\boxed{\begin{cases}MRS_{12}=\dfrac{MU_1}{MU_2}=\dfrac{p_1}{p_2}\\[8pt]u(x_1,x_2)=\bar u\end{cases}}$$
  1. Compute $MU_1$ and $MU_2$, then form the MRS.
  2. Set the MRS equal to the price ratio and express one quantity in terms of the other.
  3. Substitute into the utility constraint, solve for both demands, and check the solution.

Same tangency, different constraint: here the target utility is fixed.

Exam reminder: for linear or kinked preferences, compare feasible corner or kink candidates if an interior tangency does not exist.

03Expenditure Function

The minimum expenditure needed to attain $\bar u$.

$$e(p,\bar u)=\min_{\substack{x\geq0\\u(x)\geq\bar u}}p\cdot x$$

Compute: find Hicksian demand, substitute into total expenditure, then simplify.

$$\boxed{\begin{aligned}e(p,\bar u)&=p_1h_1(p,\bar u)\\&\quad+p_2h_2(p,\bar u)\end{aligned}}$$

The final expression depends on prices and target utility, not income.

04Shephard’s Lemma

Recover Hicksian demand from expenditure.

$$\boxed{h_i(p,\bar u)=\frac{\partial e(p,\bar u)}{\partial p_i}}$$

Apply: differentiate $e(p,\bar u)$ with respect to $p_i$, holding target utility fixed.

$\displaystyle h_1=\frac{\partial e}{\partial p_1}$$\displaystyle h_2=\frac{\partial e}{\partial p_2}$

Where expenditure is differentiable with respect to the relevant price.

III

Key Properties & Theorems

Results and checks for solving exercises.

01Uniqueness of Marshallian Demand

Strictly convex preferences + a convex budget set: the optimal bundle is unique, provided an optimum exists.

Counterexample — Linear Utility
$$\begin{gathered}u(x_1,x_2)=2x_1+x_2\\MU_1=2,\qquad MU_2=1\end{gathered}$$
$$\frac{2}{p_1}=\frac{1}{p_2}\iff\boxed{p_1=2p_2}$$

At these prices, both goods give the same utility per euro. Every nonnegative bundle on the budget line is optimal; with positive income, demand is not unique.

Demand function: one optimal bundle.
Demand correspondence: potentially several optimal bundles.

02Walras’ Law

Condition: locally non-satiated preferences.

$$\boxed{p\cdot x^M(p,y)=y}$$

At the optimum, the consumer spends the entire budget. With two goods, $p_1x_1^M+p_2x_2^M=y$.

Verify: multiply each demand by its price, add, and simplify.

$$\sum_{i=1}^n p_ix_i^M(p,y)\overset{?}{=}y$$

If equality fails, the proposed demand system does not satisfy Walras’ Law.

03Homogeneity of Degree Zero

Scaling all prices and income by the same positive factor leaves demand unchanged.

$$\boxed{x^M(\lambda p,\lambda y)=x^M(p,y)}$$

For $\lambda>0$.

Verify: replace every $p_i$ by $\lambda p_i$ and $y$ by $\lambda y$ in each demand; simplify.

$$x_i^M(\lambda p,\lambda y)\overset{?}{=}x_i^M(p,y)$$

Scale income too: changing prices alone is not this test.

04Duality of Consumer Problems

UMP: fix income and maximize utility. EmP: fix utility and minimize expenditure.

Under the course assumptions: rational, continuous, locally non-satiated preferences on $\mathbb R_+^n$, positive prices, and attainable targets $\bar u\geq u(0)$. The demand equalities below use unique optima.

Marshallian → Hicksian

Start with income $y$. Compute $v(p,y)$ and use it as the utility target in the EmP.

$$\boxed{h(p,v(p,y))=x^M(p,y)}$$

The required expenditure equals the original income:

$$\boxed{e(p,v(p,y))=y}$$
Hicksian → Marshallian

Start with target $\bar u$. Compute $e(p,\bar u)$ and use it as income in the UMP.

$$\boxed{x^M(p,e(p,\bar u))=h(p,\bar u)}$$

The maximum utility equals the original target:

$$\boxed{v(p,e(p,\bar u))=\bar u}$$

What to remember: matching income and target utility makes both problems recover the same optimal consumption choice.
