Part IV — Equilibrium & Welfare

3. General Equilibrium in the Productive Economy

Production plans, profit income, market clearing, and efficiency.

01 · Adding production

Consumers, Firms, and Goods

The economy now contains \(N\) goods, \(I\) consumers, and \(K\) firms. Firms transform inputs into outputs, so total consumption can differ from the initial endowment.

Consumers

Consumer \(i\) has utility \(u_i\), endowment \(\omega_i\), and consumption bundle \(x_i\geq0\).

Firms

Firm \(k\) chooses a net-output vector \(y_k\) from its production set \(Y_k\).

Read the signs. A positive component \(y_{kn}\) is an output of good \(n\); a negative component is an input. For example, \(y_k=(-2,3)\) uses 2 units of good 1 to produce 3 units of good 2.

At prices \(p\), profit is revenue minus input expenditure, both included in the dot product:

\[ \pi_k(p)=\max_{y_k\in Y_k}p\cdot y_k, \qquad y_k(p)\in\arg\max_{y_k\in Y_k}p\cdot y_k. \]
02 · Ownership & income

How Are Profits Distributed?

Consumers own the firms. Let \(\theta_{ki}\) be consumer \(i\)'s ownership share in firm \(k\), and therefore their share of its profit:

\[ \theta_{ki}\geq0, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{I}\theta_{ki}=1 \quad\text{for every firm }k. \]

Full ownership

If \(\theta_{ki}=1\), consumer \(i\) owns all of firm \(k\); all other consumers have zero shares in that firm.

Equal ownership

If \(\theta_{ki}=1/I\) for every \(i\), each consumer receives the same share of firm \(k\)'s profit.

Wealth includes both the value of the initial endowment and distributed profits:

\[ m_i(p)=p\cdot\omega_i+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta_{ki}\pi_k(p), \qquad p\cdot x_i\leq m_i(p).\tag{4} \]
Ownership concerns profit, not gross output. The firm must pay for its inputs before distributing profit. The budget therefore uses \(\theta_{ki}\pi_k(p)\), not a share of sales revenue.
03 · Walrasian equilibrium

Optimization and Market Clearing

An allocation \(x^*\), production plans \(y^*\), and a nonzero price vector \(p^*\geq0\) form a Walrasian equilibrium when all three conditions hold.

Firm optimization

Each firm chooses a feasible plan that maximizes profit:

\[ y_k^*\in\arg\max_{y_k\in Y_k}p^*\cdot y_k \quad\text{for every }k. \]

Consumer optimization

Each consumer chooses their best bundle at the wealth generated by endowments and equilibrium profits:

\[ x_i^*\in\arg\max_{x_i\geq0}u_i(x_i) \quad\text{s.t.}\quad p^*\cdot x_i\leq p^*\cdot\omega_i+ \sum_k\theta_{ki}\,p^*\cdot y_k^*. \]

Market clearing

For each good, consumption equals the initial endowment plus net production:

\[ \sum_i x_{in}^*=\sum_i\omega_{in}+\sum_k y_{kn}^* \quad(n=1,\ldots,N). \]

Equivalently, aggregate net purchases equal aggregate net output: \(\sum_i(x_i^*-\omega_i)=\sum_k y_k^*\).

Aggregate excess demand

Using demand at the full wealth \(m_i(p)\), including profit income:

\[ z_n(p)=\sum_i x_{in}(p,m_i(p)) -\sum_i\omega_{in}-\sum_k y_{kn}(p).\tag{5} \]

If preferences are locally nonsatiated and all profits are distributed, Walras' law remains valid:

\[ p\cdot z(p) =\sum_i m_i(p)-p\cdot\sum_i\omega_i-\sum_k\pi_k(p)=0. \]
04 · Existence

Conditions for Equilibrium

Existence requires assumptions on both preferences and technology. A convenient, restrictive sufficient benchmark for a finite economy is:

  • Consumption sets are \(\mathbb R_+^N\); every \(u_i\) is continuous, strictly increasing, and strictly concave.
  • Every consumer has a strictly positive endowment, \(\omega_i\gg0\).
  • Each production set \(Y_k\) is nonempty, closed, bounded, and convex, and contains the no-production plan \(0\).
  • Ownership shares are nonnegative and sum to one for each firm.

Closed and bounded production sets

Compactness ensures that the continuous profit objective attains a finite maximum. Convexity supports the equilibrium existence argument.

Positive endowments and \(0\in Y_k\)

Consumers can finance some consumption, and firms can choose not to produce. Maximum profit is therefore nonnegative.

A sufficient benchmark. Strict convexity of technology is not needed. More general existence theorems allow unbounded production sets under suitable restrictions on feasible production and resources; free disposal, for example, makes production sets unbounded in negative directions.
05 · Feasibility & efficiency

Feasible Consumption and Production

An allocation and production plan \((x,y)\) is feasible when \(x_i\geq0\), \(y_k\in Y_k\), and, for every good:

\[ \sum_i x_{in}\leq\sum_i\omega_{in}+\sum_k y_{kn} \qquad(n=1,\ldots,N). \]

The inequality permits disposal of available goods. Inputs already enter \(y_k\) negatively, so they must not be subtracted a second time.

Pareto efficiency

A feasible \((x,y)\) is Pareto efficient if there is no other feasible pair \((x',y')\) for which:

\[ u_i(x_i')\geq u_i(x_i)\ \text{for every }i, \qquad u_j(x_j')>u_j(x_j)\ \text{for some }j. \]
Production can change too. To assess efficiency, consider both reallocating existing goods and choosing different feasible production plans. Firms' profits affect consumer wealth, but consumer utilities determine the Pareto comparison.
06 · Welfare theorems

Efficiency with Production

First welfare theorem

In the competitive private-goods economy, with locally nonsatiated preferences, every Walrasian equilibrium \((x^*,y^*,p^*)\) is Pareto efficient.

A Pareto improvement would require consumption whose value exceeds total equilibrium wealth. But feasibility and profit maximization imply that no alternative production plans can provide a greater total value at the equilibrium prices.

\[ \sum_i p^*\cdot x_i' \leq p^*\cdot\sum_i\omega_i+\sum_k p^*\cdot y_k' \leq p^*\cdot\sum_i\omega_i+\sum_k\pi_k(p^*). \]

This contradicts the value required for a Pareto improvement. Convexity of production is not needed for this direction.

Second welfare theorem

With continuous, strongly monotone, convex preferences and closed, convex production sets, an efficient allocation with interior consumption can be supported by prices and suitable lump-sum wealth transfers.

At the supporting prices, firms maximize profit. Transfers give consumers the wealth required to choose the target bundles:

\[ p^*\cdot x_i\leq p^*\cdot\omega_i+ \sum_k\theta_{ki}\pi_k(p^*)+T_i, \qquad \sum_i T_i=0. \]

For a target \((x^*,y^*)\), use \(T_i=p^*\cdot x_i^*-p^*\cdot\omega_i-\sum_k\theta_{ki}\pi_k(p^*)\). Feasibility at the efficient allocation and full profit distribution make these transfers sum to zero.

Changing ownership alone need not be enough. Redistributing shares changes profit income, but cannot generate every required wealth distribution when profits are zero or too small. The general second welfare theorem allows wealth transfers or a redistribution of endowments, rather than relying only on ownership shares.

The role of convexity and wealth transfers is discussed in MIT's review of the welfare theorems and Gallardo, section 5.
