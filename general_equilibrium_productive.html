Part IV — Equilibrium & Welfare

3. General Equilibrium in the Productive Economy

Production · Ownership · Consumer budgets · Market clearing · Efficiency

01 · Adding production

Consumers, Firms, and Goods

The economy contains \(N\) goods, \(I\) consumers, and \(K\) firms. Production changes the quantities available for consumption.

Consumers

Consumer \(i\) has utility \(u_i\), endowment \(\omega_i\), and consumption bundle \(x_i\geq0\).

Firms

Firm \(k\) chooses a net-output vector \(y_k\) in its feasible production set \(Y_k\).

Net output: \(y_{kn}>0\) is an output; \(y_{kn}<0\) is an input. A negative component records goods used up; a positive component records goods made available.

Profit maximization

\[\begin{gathered} \pi_k(p)=\max_{y_k\in Y_k}p\cdot y_k,\\ y_k(p)\in\arg\max_{y_k\in Y_k}p\cdot y_k. \end{gathered}\]

The dot product includes output revenues and input expenditures. Inputs already carry a negative sign in \(y_k\).

02 · Ownership & income

Profits Belong to Consumers

\(\theta_{ki}\) is consumer \(i\)'s ownership share in firm \(k\), hence their share of its profit.

\[\theta_{ki}\geq0,\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{I}\theta_{ki}=1\quad\text{for every }k.\]

\(\theta_{ki}=1\)

Consumer \(i\) owns the entire firm and receives all its profit.

\(\theta_{ki}=1/I\) for every \(i\)

All consumers own equal shares and receive equal portions of the firm's profit.

The consumer's budget

Wealth = value of the initial endowment + distributed profit:

\[p\cdot x_i\leq p\cdot\omega_i+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta_{ki}\pi_k(p).\tag{4}\]
Shares of profit, not sales revenue. Firms pay for their inputs before distributing profit to their owners.

How production changes the graphs

The graphs in Course 2 help us understand budgets here too. We must now include firms and their profits.

  1. Prices still give the slope. The consumer’s budget line has slope \(-p_1/p_2\). At a smooth interior choice, the line just touches an indifference curve. This gives the best bundle the consumer can afford.
  2. Profit gives the consumer more income. Keep prices fixed. When a consumer receives a positive share of profit, they can spend more. The budget line moves outward with the same slope. It no longer has to pass through the initial endowment.
  3. Production changes the available goods. Firms use some goods as inputs and produce others as outputs. So the original box from Course 2 cannot keep the same size. To find what consumers can share, start with the initial goods, subtract inputs, and add outputs.
  4. We must also check firms’ choices. Consumers choose their best affordable bundles. Firms choose production plans that give the highest profit. At equilibrium, total consumption equals the goods available after production.

The outward budget shift above keeps prices fixed. If we compare two full equilibria, prices can also change. Then both the position and the slope of the budget line may change.

03 · Walrasian equilibrium

Three Conditions for Equilibrium

Allocation \(x^*\), production plans \(y^*\), and a nonzero price vector \(p^*\geq0\) form a Walrasian equilibrium when:

1. Every firm maximizes profit

\[y_k^*\in\arg\max_{y_k\in Y_k}p^*\cdot y_k\quad\text{for every }k.\]

2. Every consumer maximizes utility

\[\begin{gathered} x_i^*\in\arg\max_{x_i\geq0}u_i(x_i)\\ \text{s.t.}\quad p^*\cdot x_i\leq p^*\cdot\omega_i+\sum_k\theta_{ki}\pi_k(p^*). \end{gathered}\]

3. Every market clears

\[\sum_i x_{in}^*=\sum_i\omega_{in}+\sum_k y_{kn}^*\quad(n=1,\ldots,N).\]

Equivalently, aggregate net purchases equal aggregate net production:

\[\sum_i(x_i^*-\omega_i)=\sum_k y_k^*.\]

Aggregate excess demand with production

Subtract both initial resources and firms' net output from total desired consumption:

\[\begin{aligned} z_n(p)&=\sum_i x_{in}\!\left(p,\,p\cdot\omega_i+\sum_k\theta_{ki}\pi_k(p)\right)\\ &\quad-\sum_i\omega_{in}-\sum_k y_{kn}(p). \end{aligned}\tag{5}\]

The second argument of demand is the consumer's full wealth, including profit income. Market clearing means \(z_n(p^*)=0\) for every good.

Why does Walras' law still hold?

With locally nonsatiated preferences, each budget binds. Since ownership shares sum to one for every firm, all profit is distributed:

\[\sum_i p\cdot x_i=p\cdot\sum_i\omega_i+\sum_k\pi_k(p).\]

Profit-maximizing production gives \(\pi_k(p)=p\cdot y_k(p)\). Therefore:

\[\begin{aligned} p\cdot z(p)&=\sum_i p\cdot x_i-p\cdot\sum_i\omega_i\\ &\quad-\sum_k p\cdot y_k(p)=0. \end{aligned}\]
04 · Existence

Assumptions on Preferences and Technology

The lecture's sufficient benchmark combines:

  • Preferences: every \(u_i\) is continuous, strictly increasing in every good, and strictly concave.
  • Endowments: each consumer has positive amounts of every good, \(\omega_i\gg0\).
  • Technology: production sets are closed, bounded, and strictly convex.

Use nonempty production sets containing \(0\), consumption sets \(\mathbb R_+^N\), and nonnegative ownership shares summing to one for each firm.

Closed + bounded

Production sets are compact. The continuous profit objective attains a finite maximum.

Convex technology

Mixtures of feasible production plans remain feasible. This supports the equilibrium existence argument.

The no-production option: \(0\in Y_k\) lets a firm earn zero by not producing, so its maximum profit cannot be negative.
How restrictive are these assumptions?

These are sufficient conditions. Strict convexity is stronger than required in the compact-production benchmark; convexity suffices for the existence argument.

More general theorems allow unbounded production sets with appropriate resource restrictions. Free disposal, for example, makes sets unbounded in negative directions.

05 · Feasibility & efficiency

Include Production in Feasibility

A pair \((x,y)\) is feasible if \(x_i\geq0\), \(y_k\in Y_k\), and, for every good:

\[\sum_i x_{in}\leq\sum_i\omega_{in}+\sum_k y_{kn}.\]

In vector form:

\[\sum_i(x_i-\omega_i)\leq\sum_k y_k.\]

The inequality allows disposal. Since inputs enter net production negatively, do not subtract them again.

Pareto efficiency

A feasible \((x,y)\) is Pareto efficient if no other feasible \((x',y')\) satisfies:

\[\begin{gathered} u_i(x_i')\geq u_i(x_i)\quad\text{for every }i,\\ u_j(x_j')>u_j(x_j)\quad\text{for at least one }j. \end{gathered}\]
Both choices can change: how goods are produced and how they are allocated. Consumer utilities determine the Pareto comparison; firms' profits enter through consumer wealth.
06 · Welfare theorems

The Welfare Theorems with Production

First theorem: equilibrium → efficiency

With continuous, strictly increasing utility in the competitive private-goods economy, every Walrasian equilibrium \((x^*,y^*,p^*)\) is Pareto efficient.

Intuition: consumers already choose their best affordable bundles, and firms maximize the value of production at the same prices.

Proof idea: compare the value of resources

A Pareto improvement would require consumption worth strictly more than total equilibrium wealth. Yet feasibility and profit maximization imply:

\[\begin{aligned} \sum_i p^*\cdot x_i'&\leq p^*\cdot\sum_i\omega_i+\sum_k p^*\cdot y_k'\\ &\leq p^*\cdot\sum_i\omega_i+\sum_k\pi_k(p^*). \end{aligned}\]

Contradiction. Local nonsatiation suffices for the preference argument; convexity of production is not needed for this direction.

Second theorem: efficiency + redistribution → equilibrium

With continuous, strictly increasing, concave utility and closed, convex production sets, an efficient allocation with interior consumption can be supported by prices after suitable wealth redistribution.

At the supporting prices, firms maximize profit. Lump-sum transfers give each consumer the wealth needed for the target bundle:

\[\begin{gathered} p^*\cdot x_i\leq p^*\cdot\omega_i+\sum_k\theta_{ki}\pi_k(p^*)+T_i,\\ \sum_i T_i=0. \end{gathered}\]

The transfer required for target \((x^*,y^*)\) is:

\[T_i=p^*\cdot x_i^*-p^*\cdot\omega_i-\sum_k\theta_{ki}\pi_k(p^*).\]

Feasibility at the efficient allocation and full profit distribution ensure that the transfers sum to zero.

Where does redistribution of ownership fit?

Changing ownership shares redistributes profit income. It supports a target allocation only if feasible shares \(\theta'_{ki}\) can finance every target bundle:

\[\begin{gathered} p^*\cdot x_i^*=p^*\cdot\omega_i+\sum_k\theta'_{ki}\pi_k(p^*),\\ \theta'_{ki}\geq0,\qquad\sum_i\theta'_{ki}=1. \end{gathered}\]
Ownership alone is not always enough. If all profits are zero, changing shares changes no one's wealth. The general second theorem therefore allows transfers or redistribution of endowments; ownership changes implement it only when they generate the required wealth distribution.
