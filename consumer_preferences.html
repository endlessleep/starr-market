4. Consumer’s Preferences: Reasonable Assumptions

Monotonicity, convexity, homotheticity, separability, and numéraire properties: definitions, economic intuition, and their meaning for utility.

Preamble
From abstract alternatives to bundles of goods

In Chapters 1–3, we studied how an individual ranks alternatives and chooses from a choice set. Consumer theory gives these alternatives a concrete meaning: each $x\in X$ is a consumption bundle, specifying a quantity of each good.

This makes the choice problem more specific. Preferences describe which bundles the consumer values more; prices and the budget determine which bundles are affordable. The consumer’s choice depends on both.

Two ways to study consumer choice

From preferences to choices: Specify properties of preferences, then determine which affordable bundles the consumer would choose. Chapter 4 develops the preference assumptions used in this approach.

From choices to preferences: Observe the chosen bundles and the alternatives that were affordable. Then ask which preference rankings could explain those choices. This is the revealed-preference approach developed in Chapter 5.

Why introduce additional assumptions?

Rationality requires a complete and transitive ranking. With continuity, preferences on the consumption set considered here admit a continuous utility representation $u$. In the two-good case, bundles with the same utility lie on the same indifference curve: the consumer is indifferent between them.

These properties alone do not tell us how the consumer responds to more goods, mixtures of bundles, or changes in scale. We therefore introduce additional assumptions that restrict preferences. Each assumption describes a particular way in which bundles are ranked:

  • Monotonicity: More of some goods, with no reduction in the others, does not make the consumer worse off.
  • Convexity: If two bundles are each at least as good as a benchmark, any weighted average of them is also at least as good.
  • Homotheticity: Scaling both bundles by the same positive factor preserves their ranking.
  • Separability: The ranking of alternatives within one group of goods remains unchanged when the common quantities of other goods change.
  • Numéraire properties: One good, interpreted as money, provides a way to express compensation for changes in other goods.
1. Monotonicity

Let $X=\mathbb{R}_+^n$ be the consumption set. A bundle $x=(x_1,\ldots,x_n)$ lists the nonnegative quantity of each good. To compare quantities, we check each good separately. The notation $x\geq y$ means $x_i\geq y_i$ for every good $i$: $x$ contains the same amount or a larger amount of each good, and less of none. Some quantities may be unchanged. Adding $x\neq y$ means that at least one quantity is strictly larger.

Strict Monotonicity
$\succeq$ is strictly monotone if:
$$x \geq y \quad\text{and}\quad x \neq y \implies x \succ y$$
Intuition: More is always better. If bundle $x$ contains at least as much of every good as bundle $y$, and strictly more of at least one good, then $x$ is strictly preferred to $y$.
Monotonicity (Weak Monotonicity)
$\succeq$ is monotone if:
$$x \geq y \implies x \succeq y$$
Intuition: Receiving more of some goods, without losing any other good, cannot make the consumer worse off. “At least as good” allows two possibilities: the consumer strictly prefers the new bundle, or is indifferent between the two. Weak monotonicity does not require a strict improvement.
Weak vs. strict monotonicity — In words
  1. Weak monotonicity: If bundle $x$ contains at least as much of every good as bundle $y$, then $x$ is at least as good as $y$.
  2. Strict monotonicity: If bundle $x$ contains at least as much of every good as bundle $y$, and strictly more of at least one good, then $x$ is strictly preferred to $y$.
Local Non-Satiation (LNS)
$\succeq$ is locally non-satiated if, for all $x \in X$ and all $\varepsilon > 0$:
$$\exists y \in X:\quad \begin{aligned} \|x-y\| &< \varepsilon,\\ y &\succ x. \end{aligned}$$
Intuition: Starting from any bundle, it is always possible to find a strictly preferred bundle nearby, however small a change we allow. The number $\varepsilon$ specifies how close that bundle must be. LNS requires some nearby improvement; it does not say that every small change is beneficial, or that more of each good is desirable.
Monotonicity and LNS: how do the ideas differ?

Weak monotonicity describes what happens when quantities increase without any decrease elsewhere: the consumer is at least as well off. It places a restriction on all such comparisons, but allows the extra goods to leave the consumer indifferent.

Local non-satiation asks a different question: can the consumer become strictly better off through a change as small as desired? It requires an improvement near every bundle, without specifying which goods must increase or decrease.

Clarification of the implication in the slides. The slides write “Strict monotonicity ⇒ Monotonicity ⇒ Local non-satiation”. With their weak definition, the second implication needs qualification: “at least as good” allows indifference and does not guarantee a strictly better nearby bundle. Strict monotonicity does guarantee both weak monotonicity and LNS.
Course Problem · Counter-Example Monotonicity vs. LNS
Find an example of a utility function which is locally non-satiated but not weakly monotone.
Economic Interpretation
Think of $x_1$ as money (general purchasing power, where more is always strictly better) and $x_2$ as cups of coffee (where more coffee is not always better beyond 1 cup).
Utility Function
$$u(x_1, x_2) = x_1 - (x_2 - 1)^2$$
For a fixed amount of money, utility is highest at $x_2=1$. More coffee beyond this point reduces utility.
1. Locally Non-Satiated (LNS) Satisfied ✓

Keep the amount of coffee unchanged and give the consumer a little more money. The coffee term $-(x_2-1)^2$ stays the same, while the money term $x_1$ increases. Utility therefore increases.

That extra amount of money can be as small as we wish, so the improved bundle can be arbitrarily close to the original one. This works whatever the initial amount of coffee: money always provides a way to become strictly better off.

2. Weak Monotonicity Violated ✗

Compare bundle $A = (1, 1)$ and bundle $B = (1, 2)$. Clearly $B \geq A$ componentwise ($1 \geq 1$ and $2 \geq 1$).

$$u(1, 1) = 1 - (1 - 1)^2 = 1$$ $$u(1, 2) = 1 - (2 - 1)^2 = 0$$

Since $u(1, 2) = 0 < 1 = u(1, 1)$, we have:

$$(1,1)\succ(1,2)$$

Giving more coffee strictly harms the consumer, so weak monotonicity is violated!

Conclusion: $u(x_1, x_2) = x_1 - (x_2 - 1)^2$ is locally non-satiated but not weakly monotone. The distinction is that an improvement in money is always possible, even though more coffee can be undesirable.
2. Convexity
Convex Preferences
On a convex consumption set, $\succeq$ is convex if, for all bundles $x,y,z$ and every $t\in[0,1]$:
$$\begin{gathered} x\succeq z,\quad y\succeq z\\ \implies tx+(1-t)y\succeq z. \end{gathered}$$

Read the definition in order. First, fix a comparison bundle $z$. Both $x$ and $y$ are at least as good as this same $z$. Then form $tx+(1-t)y$: the weight on $x$ is $t$, the weight on $y$ is $1-t$, and the two weights add up to one. We apply these weights to the quantities of each good.

Intuition: Individuals prefer “diversity”, or prefer averages to extremes. Here this means that mixing two bundles which are each at least as good as $z$ produces a bundle which is still at least as good as $z$. Keep track of the comparison: the conclusion ranks the mixture against $z$.
Strict Convexity
For distinct bundles $x\neq y$ and a weight $t\in(0,1)$, strict convexity strengthens the conclusion:
$$\begin{gathered} x\succeq z,\quad y\succeq z\\ \implies tx+(1-t)y\succ z. \end{gathered}$$
The assumptions still compare $x$ and $y$ with the same bundle $z$. The conclusion now uses $\succ$: the mixture is strictly preferred to $z$.
An Implication of Convexity:
$$x\succ y \implies tx+(1-t)y\succeq y$$

Intuition: If the consumer strictly prefers $x$ to $y$, then any weighted average of them, with $t\in[0,1]$, is at least as good as the worse bundle $y$. Think of moving from $y$ towards $x$: the mixture does not fall below $y$ in the ranking.

3. Homotheticity
Homothetic Preferences
$\succeq$ is homothetic if, for any $\lambda > 0$:
$$x \succeq y \iff \lambda x \succeq \lambda y$$
Intuition: Preferences are scalable. Multiply every quantity in both bundles by the same positive number $\lambda$: their ranking is unchanged. In the course example, if you prefer 5 olives to 7 pretzels, you also prefer 500 olives to 700 pretzels. Both quantities have been multiplied by 100. Homotheticity says that this common change of scale preserves the preference comparison.

  • Homotheticity: Indifference curves are obtained from one another by scaling all quantities by the same positive factor.

4. Separability
Definition of Separability
$\succeq$ is separable if, for every $x, x' \in X$ and every $y, y' \in Y$:
$$\begin{gathered} (x,y)\succeq(x',y)\\ \iff (x,y')\succeq(x',y'). \end{gathered}$$
Intuition: Compare $x$ with $x'$ while holding the other component fixed. Then change that common component for both bundles. If preferences are separable, the ranking between $x$ and $x'$ must remain unchanged.

The course uses this assumption to explain why choices concerning one group of goods can be studied separately from another group, such as labour and leisure. The relevant restriction is that changing the common level of the other group does not change the ranking being studied.

5. Numéraire Properties
The Numéraire Good: Let us fix $t$ as the numéraire good. Write an alternative as $(t,y)\in\mathbb{R}\times Y$, where $t$ is money and $y$ describes the other goods. Allowing $t$ to be any real number lets us describe both payments and compensation.
The Three Numéraire Properties
A preference relation $\succeq$ satisfies the numéraire properties if:
  1. The numéraire good is valuable:
    $$(t, y) \succeq (t', y) \iff t \geq t'$$
    Intuition: When the other goods $y$ are unchanged, the consumer prefers the alternative with more money. The money amount can therefore serve as a scale for comparing otherwise identical alternatives.
  2. Compensation is possible: For every $y,y'\in Y$, there exists $t\in\mathbb{R}$ such that
    $$(0,y)\sim(t,y')$$
    Intuition: A change from $y$ to $y'$ can be offset by an appropriate amount of money. The amount $t$ makes the consumer indifferent between the two alternatives. It expresses the value of that change in monetary terms: how much the consumer is willing to pay, or needs to receive as compensation.
  3. No wealth effect: For every common money change $d\in\mathbb{R}$,
    $$\begin{gathered} (t,y)\succeq(t',y')\\ \iff (t+d,y)\succeq(t'+d,y') \end{gathered}$$
    Intuition: Adding the same amount $d$ to both alternatives preserves their ranking, even when their initial money amounts differ. The consumer’s evaluation of the trade-off between money and the other goods does not change when both alternatives become equally richer or poorer.
What is the difference between assuming No Wealth Effect and Separability?
Separability
If money is equal in both alternatives, does the ranking of $y$ versus $y'$ stay the same when that common money level changes?

Money is equal within each comparison. We change its common level for both alternatives, keeping $y$ and $y'$ unchanged. The money needed to compensate for a change in $y$ may still depend on wealth.

Graphically: The indifference curves do not cross, but they need not be parallel.

No wealth effect
If I add the same amount of money to BOTH complete alternatives, does their ranking stay the same?

The initial money amounts can be different. What must be equal is the amount added to each alternative, while $y$ and $y'$ remain unchanged.

Monetary compensation for a change in $y$ is therefore independent of the initial wealth level.

Graphically: The indifference curves are parallel shifts in the money direction $t$.

$$\begin{gathered} \text{No wealth effect}\\ \implies \text{Separability in }y \end{gathered}$$

Separability concerns comparisons made at a common money level. No wealth effect covers those comparisons too, while also covering alternatives with different money amounts. It therefore imposes a stronger restriction.

Equivalence: Numéraire Properties ⟺ Quasi-Linear Utility
For a rational preference relation on $\mathbb{R}\times Y$, the three numéraire properties hold if and only if there exists a utility representation of the form
$$u(t,y)=t+v(y),\qquad v:Y\to\mathbb{R}.$$
The utility function $u : \mathbb{R} \times Y \to \mathbb{R}$ is quasi-linear.
Starting Point / Hypotheses:
We assume rational preferences on $\mathbb{R}\times Y$ satisfying the three numéraire properties: (1) $t$ is valuable, (2) Compensation is possible, and (3) No wealth effect.
Goal: Construct $v:Y\to\mathbb{R}$ so that $u(t,y)=t+v(y)$ represents the preference relation.
1
Because compensation is possible (Property 2).
Fix an arbitrary reference bundle $\bar{y} \in Y$. Because compensation is possible, for every $y \in Y$, there exists $v(y) \in \mathbb{R}$, the money amount that makes the reference alternative $\bar{y}$ equivalent to $y$:
$$(0, y) \sim (v(y), \bar{y})$$
Similarly, for $y'\in Y$:
$$(0, y') \sim (v(y'), \bar{y})$$
2
Because there is no wealth effect (Property 3).
Indifference means that both weak comparisons hold. Apply property 3 to each comparison, adding $t$ to both alternatives:
$$(t, y) \sim (t + v(y), \bar{y})$$
Similarly, adding $t'$ preserves indifference:
$$(t', y') \sim (t' + v(y'), \bar{y})$$
3
Hence, compare the monetary equivalents.
By transitivity of $\succeq$, comparing $(t, y)$ and $(t', y')$ is equivalent to comparing their monetary equivalents at $\bar{y}$:
$$\begin{gathered} (t,y)\succeq(t',y')\\ \iff (t+v(y),\bar y)\succeq(t'+v(y'),\bar y). \end{gathered}$$
In each direction, replace the two bundles by their indifferent monetary equivalents and use transitivity.
4
Because the numéraire good is valuable (Property 1).
Because the numéraire good is valuable (Property 1), the last comparison can be rewritten purely in terms of the numéraire amounts:
$$\begin{gathered} (t,y)\succeq(t',y')\\ \iff t+v(y)\geq t'+v(y'). \end{gathered}$$
Hence $\succeq$ can be represented by the quasi-linear utility function $u(t, y) = t + v(y)$. $\blacksquare$

For the reverse direction ($\Leftarrow$), the slides state that the result is “straightforward”.
