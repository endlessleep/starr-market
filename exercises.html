Exercise 1 · Preference Relations
Exercise 1. Preference Relations
Problem Set

Prove the following statements about preference relations:

  1. If $\succeq$ is transitive, then $\succ$ is also transitive.
  2. If $\succeq$ is transitive, then $\sim$ is also transitive.
  3. If $\succeq$ is complete and transitive, then $\succeq$ is negatively transitive.
    (Note: Negatively transitive means: if $x \succeq y$, then for any $z$ either $x \succeq z$ or $z \succeq y$ or both.)
1. Transitivity of Strict Preference ($\succ$)
Goal: Show that if $\succeq$ is transitive, then $\succ$ is transitive: $$x \succ y \quad\text{and}\quad y \succ z \implies x \succ z$$
Goal. Show that if $\succeq$ is transitive, then $\succ$ is transitive.
So assume:
$$x \succ y \quad\text{and}\quad y \succ z$$
We want to prove:
$$x \succ z$$
By definition of strict preference, this means that we need to show:
$$x \succeq z \quad\text{and}\quad z \not\succeq x$$
Step 1 — Proving $x \succeq z$

From:

$$x \succ y \iff x \succeq y \text{ and } y \not\succeq x \qquad\text{and}\qquad y \succ z \iff y \succeq z \text{ and } z \not\succeq y$$

we obtain: $x \succeq y$ and $y \succeq z$.

Since $\succeq$ is transitive:

$$x \succeq y, \ y \succeq z \implies x \succeq z$$
Step 2 — Proving $z \not\succeq x$ (by contradiction)

It remains to prove: $z \not\succeq x$.

Suppose by contradiction that:

$$z \succeq x$$

Since $x \succeq y$, transitivity gives:

$$z \succeq y$$

But this contradicts: $z \not\succeq y$.

Therefore the assumption $z \succeq x$ must be false, which means:

$$z \not\succeq x$$
Conclusion

We now have:

$$x \succeq z \quad\text{and}\quad z \not\succeq x$$

Hence, by definition of strict preference:

$$\boxed{x \succ z}$$
$$\boxed{\succ \text{ is transitive}}$$
2. Transitivity of Indifference ($\sim$)
Goal: Show that if $\succeq$ is transitive, then $\sim$ is also transitive: $$x \sim y \quad\text{and}\quad y \sim z \implies x \sim z$$
Goal. Show that if $\succeq$ is transitive, then $\sim$ is also transitive.
Assume:
$$x \sim y \quad\text{and}\quad y \sim z$$
We want to prove:
$$x \sim z$$
By definition of indifference:
$$x \sim y \iff x \succeq y \text{ and } y \succeq x \qquad\text{and}\qquad y \sim z \iff y \succeq z \text{ and } z \succeq y$$
Applying transitivity in both directions

Since $\succeq$ is transitive:

$$x \succeq y, \ y \succeq z \implies x \succeq z$$

and:

$$z \succeq y, \ y \succeq x \implies z \succeq x$$

Thus:

$$x \succeq z \quad\text{and}\quad z \succeq x$$
Conclusion

Hence, by definition of indifference:

$$\boxed{x \sim z}$$
$$\boxed{\sim \text{ is transitive}}$$

Note : beaucoup plus direct que la question 1 — pas besoin de contradiction, on applique simplement la transitivité dans les deux directions.

3. Negative Transitivity of Weak Preference ($\succeq$)
Goal: Suppose $\succeq$ is complete and transitive. Given $x\succeq y$, show that for every $z\in X$: $$x\succeq z \quad\text{or}\quad z\succeq y.$$
Starting point / Hypotheses. The weak preference relation $\succeq$ is complete and transitive. Fix $x,y\in X$ with $x\succeq y$.
Goal. Prove that every $z\in X$ satisfies $x\succeq z$ or $z\succeq y$.
1. Assume the opposite

Suppose, for a contradiction, that the conclusion is false. Then there exists some $z\in X$ for which neither comparison holds:

$$\begin{gathered} x\not\succeq z,\\ z\not\succeq y. \end{gathered}$$

The negation of “$A$ or $B$” is “not $A$ and not $B$”. We keep the initial hypothesis $x\succeq y$; only the conclusion is assumed false.

2. Use completeness

Completeness requires that $x$ and $z$ can be compared:

$$x\succeq z \quad\text{or}\quad z\succeq x.$$

Step 1 rules out $x\succeq z$. Therefore the other comparison must hold:

$$z\succeq x.$$
3. Derive the contradiction

We have $z\succeq x$ from step 2 and $x\succeq y$ from the initial hypothesis. By transitivity:

$$\begin{gathered} z\succeq x,\quad x\succeq y\\ \implies z\succeq y. \end{gathered}$$

But step 1 assumes $z\not\succeq y$. We have obtained a statement and its negation: this is the contradiction.

4. Conclude

Our supposition is impossible: there is no $z\in X$ for which both comparisons fail. Hence, for every $z\in X$:

$$x\succeq z \quad\text{or}\quad z\succeq y.$$

Thus $\succeq$ is negatively transitive. $\blacksquare$

Intuition: If $x$ is at least as good as $y$, any alternative $z$ that is not weakly below $x$ must be weakly above $x$, by completeness, and therefore weakly above $y$, by transitivity. Both comparisons in the conclusion may hold when there is indifference.
Classroom method — Contradiction through strict preference.
Starting point / Hypotheses. The relation $\succeq$ is complete and transitive, and $x\succeq y$.
Goal. Show that every $z\in X$ satisfies $x\succeq z$ or $z\succeq y$ (or both).
1. Assume the opposite

Suppose there exists $z\in X$ for which the conclusion fails. Negating “or” means that both comparisons fail:

$$\begin{gathered} x\not\succeq z,\\ z\not\succeq y. \end{gathered}$$
2. Translate into strict preferences

Since $x\not\succeq z$, completeness gives $z\succeq x$. Together, these two statements mean that $z$ is strictly preferred to $x$:

$$z\succ x.$$

Similarly, $z\not\succeq y$ and completeness give $y\succeq z$ but not $z\succeq y$. By the definition of strict preference:

$$y\succ z.$$

The opposite of the desired conclusion therefore forces the strict ranking used in class:

$$y\succ z\succ x.$$
3. Apply transitivity

Question 1 established that transitivity of $\succeq$ implies transitivity of its strict part $\succ$. Applying that result to $y\succ z$ and $z\succ x$ gives:

$$y\succ x.$$
4. Contradict the initial assumption

By definition, $y\succ x$ means $y\succeq x$ and $x\not\succeq y$. The latter directly contradicts our initial hypothesis $x\succeq y$.

Thus the supposition in step 1 is impossible. For every $z\in X$, we must have:

$$x\succeq z \quad\text{or}\quad z\succeq y.$$

This proves negative transitivity. $\blacksquare$

How the two methods differ: Both are proofs by contradiction. The first derives $z\succeq y$, contradicting the assumed $z\not\succeq y$. The classroom method uses the strict chain $y\succ z\succ x$ to contradict the original hypothesis $x\succeq y$.
Exercise 2 · Joint Preference Relation
Exercise 2. Joint Preference Relation
Problem Set

Two friends, Larry and Moe, wish to go on vacation together. Individually, they are standard preference maximizers — their preferences $\succeq_{\text{Larry}}$ and $\succeq_{\text{Moe}}$ are complete and transitive. They attempt to form a joint preference relation ($\succeq^*$), as follows:

$$\boxed{x \succeq^* y \iff x \succeq_{\text{Larry}} y \quad\text{or}\quad x \succeq_{\text{Moe}} y}$$

That is, they jointly prefer $x$ to $y$ if either Larry or Moe prefers $x$ to $y$.

  1. Prove that $\succeq^*$ is complete.
  2. Show by example that it may not be transitive.
1. Completeness of $\succeq^*$
Goal: For any $x, y$, show that: $$x \succeq^* y \quad\text{or}\quad y \succeq^* x$$
Goal. For any $x, y$, show:
$$x \succeq^* y \quad\text{or}\quad y \succeq^* x$$
Since Larry's preferences are complete:
$$x \succeq_L y \quad\text{or}\quad y \succeq_L x$$
Case 1

If $x \succeq_L y$, then Larry supports $x$ over $y$. By definition of the joint relation:

$$x \succeq_L y \implies \boxed{x \succeq^* y}$$
Case 2

If $y \succeq_L x$, then Larry supports the opposite ranking:

$$y \succeq_L x \implies \boxed{y \succeq^* x}$$
Conclusion

Therefore, for every pair $x, y$:

$$\boxed{x \succeq^* y \quad\text{or}\quad y \succeq^* x}$$
$$\boxed{\succeq^* \text{ is complete}}$$
Key idea: Because the joint relation uses an OR condition, Larry alone is already enough to guarantee that every pair can be ranked!
2. Non-Transitivity of $\succeq^*$ (Counterexample)
Goal: Show by example that $\succeq^*$ may not be transitive: $$x \succeq^* y \quad\text{and}\quad y \succeq^* z \quad\not\implies\quad x \succeq^* z$$
To violate transitivity, we need to find bundles $x, y, z$ such that:
$$x \succeq^* y \quad\text{and}\quad y \succeq^* z, \qquad\text{but}\qquad \boxed{x \not\succeq^* z}$$
Counterexample Construction

Consider the following strict individual rankings on three destinations $\{x, y, z\}$:

$$\text{Larry: } \boxed{z \succ_L x \succ_L y} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \text{Moe: } \boxed{y \succ_M z \succ_M x}$$
Step 1 — Verify $x \succeq^* y$ and $y \succeq^* z$

• Larry has $x \succ_L y$, so $x \succeq_L y \implies \mathbf{x \succeq^* y}$.

• Moe has $y \succ_M z$, so $y \succeq_M z \implies \mathbf{y \succeq^* z}$.

Step 2 — Check whether $x \succeq^* z$

If $\succeq^*$ were transitive, we would require $x \succeq^* z$. But checking individual preferences:

• Larry prefers $z \succ_L x \implies x \not\succeq_L z$.

• Moe prefers $z \succ_M x \implies x \not\succeq_M z$.

Since neither Larry nor Moe supports $x \succeq z$, by definition:

$$\boxed{x \not\succeq^* z}$$
Conclusion

We have found:

$$x \succeq^* y, \qquad y \succeq^* z, \qquad x \not\succeq^* z$$
$$\boxed{\succeq^* \text{ may not be transitive}}$$
Final takeaway:
While $\succeq^*$ is complete, it need not be transitive.
Therefore, the joint preference relation is not necessarily rational.
Exercise 3 · Lexicographic Preferences
Exercise 3. Lexicographic Preferences
Problem Set

Consider lexicographic preferences on bundles $x = (x_1, x_2)$ and $y = (y_1, y_2)$, defined by:

$$\boxed{x \succeq y \iff x_1 > y_1 \quad\text{or}\quad \left(x_1 = y_1 \text{ and } x_2 \ge y_2\right)}$$

Intuition: The first coordinate has absolute priority. The second coordinate is considered only if the first coordinates are equal.

Show that lexicographic preferences are rational (i.e. complete and transitive):

  1. Prove that $\succeq$ is complete.
  2. Prove that $\succeq$ is transitive.
1. Completeness of Lexicographic Preferences
Goal: For any two bundles $x, y$, show: $$x \succeq y \quad\text{or}\quad y \succeq x$$
Goal. For any two bundles $x, y$, show:
$$x \succeq y \quad\text{or}\quad y \succeq x$$
Take $x = (x_1, x_2)$ and $y = (y_1, y_2)$. There are three mutually exclusive possibilities for the first coordinates:
Case 1 — $x_1 > y_1$

If $x_1 > y_1$, then directly by definition of lexicographic preferences:

$$x_1 > y_1 \implies \boxed{x \succeq y}$$
Case 2 — $y_1 > x_1$

If $y_1 > x_1$, then symmetrically:

$$y_1 > x_1 \implies \boxed{y \succeq x}$$
Case 3 — $x_1 = y_1$

If $x_1 = y_1$, we compare the second coordinates. Since real numbers are comparable:

$$x_2 \ge y_2 \quad\text{or}\quad y_2 \ge x_2$$

Hence:

$$x \succeq y \quad\text{or}\quad y \succeq x$$
Conclusion

In all three cases, the bundles can be compared:

$$\boxed{\succeq \text{ is complete}}$$
2. Transitivity of Lexicographic Preferences
Goal: Assume $x \succeq y$ and $y \succeq z$. Prove that: $$x \succeq z$$
Starting point / Hypotheses. Take three bundles $x=(x_1,x_2)$, $y=(y_1,y_2)$ and $z=(z_1,z_2)$ such that $x\succeq y$ and $y\succeq z$.
Goal. Prove $x\succeq z$ using the definition of lexicographic preferences.
Keep the two symbols distinct. The relation $\succeq$ compares bundles according to preferences. The signs $\geq$ and $>$ compare real-valued coordinates. We may use transitivity of numerical inequalities; transitivity of $\succeq$ is precisely what we must prove.

Expand each hypothesis into its two possible branches, as in the classroom method:

From $x\succeq y$

(i) The first coordinate is larger:

$$x_1>y_1.$$

Or (ii) The first coordinates are equal, and the second is at least as large:

$$\begin{gathered} x_1=y_1,\\ x_2\geq y_2. \end{gathered}$$
From $y\succeq z$

(iii) The first coordinate is larger:

$$y_1>z_1.$$

Or (iv) The first coordinates are equal, and the second is at least as large:

$$\begin{gathered} y_1=z_1,\\ y_2\geq z_2. \end{gathered}$$

Combining one branch from each hypothesis gives four cases. They cover every possible way in which the two hypotheses can hold.

1. Case (i) + (iii)

We have $x_1>y_1$ and $y_1>z_1$. Therefore:

$$x_1>y_1>z_1.$$

By transitivity of the numerical order, $x_1>z_1$. The first clause of the lexicographic definition then gives:

$$x\succeq z.$$

The second coordinates do not matter because the first coordinate already decides the comparison.

2. Case (i) + (iv)

We have $x_1>y_1$, while $y_1=z_1$ and $y_2\geq z_2$. Substituting $z_1$ for $y_1$ gives:

$$x_1>y_1=z_1.$$

Thus $x_1>z_1$. By the first clause of the definition:

$$x\succeq z.$$

The condition $y_2\geq z_2$ explains why $y\succeq z$, but it is not needed to compare $x$ with $z$.

3. Case (ii) + (iii)

We have $x_1=y_1$ and $x_2\geq y_2$, while $y_1>z_1$. Substituting $x_1$ for $y_1$ gives:

$$x_1=y_1>z_1.$$

Hence $x_1>z_1$, so the first clause again yields:

$$x\succeq z.$$

Although $x$ and $y$ are tied in their first coordinate, they both have a strictly larger first coordinate than $z$.

4. Case (ii) + (iv)

Both comparisons use the second clause. Their first-coordinate equalities give:

$$x_1=y_1=z_1.$$

The remaining assumptions are $x_2\geq y_2$ and $y_2\geq z_2$. By transitivity of the numerical order:

$$x_2\geq y_2\geq z_2.$$

We therefore have $x_1=z_1$ and $x_2\geq z_2$. These are exactly the two conditions in the second clause of the definition, so:

$$x\succeq z.$$
Conclusion

All four cases lead to the same conclusion whenever $x\succeq y$ and $y\succeq z$:

$$x\succeq z.$$

Thus lexicographic preferences are transitive. $\blacksquare$

Intuition: A strict advantage in the first coordinate carries through the comparison. Only when all three first coordinates are equal do we need the second coordinates.
Together with completeness, established in Question 1, this shows that lexicographic preferences are rational.
Exercise 4 · Property α
Exercise 4. Property α
Problem Set

Let $X$ be a choice set and $c$ a choice function on $X$. Show, using the following two steps, that if $c$ satisfies Property α then it can be rationalized by a rational preference relation:

  1. Define a binary relation $\succeq$ by:
    $$x \succeq y \iff c(\{x, y\}) = x$$
    Show that this relation is a rational preference relation.
    (Note: We are defining a particular preference relation from $c$ to show that $c$ is rationalizable.)
  2. Show that for every choice set $A$, $c(A) \succeq a$ for every $a \in A$. In other words, that the chosen alternative is the unique best alternative according to $\succeq$.
1. Show that $\succeq$ is a Rational Preference Relation
Goal: Show that the relation defined by $x \succeq y \iff c(\{x, y\}) = x$ is complete and transitive.
Part (a) — Completeness

Pick any $x, y \in X$. Since $c$ is a single-valued choice function on the two-element choice set $\{x, y\}$:

$$c(\{x, y\}) \in \{x, y\}$$

Therefore, either:

$$c(\{x, y\}) = x \implies x \succeq y \qquad\text{or}\qquad c(\{x, y\}) = y \implies y \succeq x$$

Hence:

$$\boxed{\succeq \text{ is complete}}$$
Part (b) — Transitivity

Assume $x \succeq y$ and $y \succeq z$. By definition of $\succeq$:

$$c(\{x, y\}) = x \qquad\text{and}\qquad c(\{y, z\}) = y$$

Consider the choice set $\{x, y, z\}$. Since $c$ selects an element from $\{x, y, z\}$:

$$c(\{x, y, z\}) \in \{x, y, z\}$$

We check the three possibilities:

• If $c(\{x, y, z\}) = y$: since $\{x, y\} \subset \{x, y, z\}$ and $y \in \{x, y\}$, Property α implies $c(\{x, y\}) = y$, which contradicts $c(\{x, y\}) = x$.

• If $c(\{x, y, z\}) = z$: since $\{y, z\} \subset \{x, y, z\}$ and $z \in \{y, z\}$, Property α implies $c(\{y, z\}) = z$, which contradicts $c(\{y, z\}) = y$.

• Therefore, we must have: $c(\{x, y, z\}) = x$.

Now consider the pair $\{x, z\} \subset \{x, y, z\}$. Since $x \in \{x, z\}$, Property α implies:

$$c(\{x, z\}) = c(\{x, y, z\}) = x$$

By definition of $\succeq$, $c(\{x, z\}) = x \implies \mathbf{x \succeq z}$.

Hence:

$$\boxed{\succeq \text{ is transitive}}$$
Conclusion

Since $\succeq$ is both complete and transitive:

$$\boxed{\succeq \text{ is a rational preference relation}}$$
2. Show that $c(A)$ is the Unique Best Alternative
Goal: For every choice set $A$: $$\boxed{c(A) \succeq a \qquad \forall a \in A}$$ In other words, the alternative chosen from $A$ must be the unique best alternative according to $\succeq$.
Goal. For every choice set $A$,
$$\boxed{c(A) \succeq a \qquad \forall a \in A.}$$
In other words, the alternative chosen from $A$ must be the unique best alternative according to $\succeq$.
Proof by Contradiction

Take any choice set $A$, with:

$$c(A) \in A.$$

Suppose by contradiction that $c(A)$ is not the best alternative in $A$.

Then there exists some:

$$a \in A, \qquad a \neq c(A),$$

such that:

$$a \succeq c(A).$$

By the definition of the preference relation:

$$a \succeq c(A) \iff c(\{a, c(A)\}) = a.$$

Hence:

$$\boxed{c(\{a, c(A)\}) = a.}$$
Contradiction via Property α

However,

$$\{a, c(A)\} \subseteq A \qquad\text{and}\qquad c(A) \in \{a, c(A)\}.$$

Since $c(A)$ is chosen from the larger set $A$, Property α implies that it must remain chosen in every smaller set that still contains it:

$$\boxed{c(\{a, c(A)\}) = c(A).}$$

This contradicts: $c(\{a, c(A)\}) = a$.

Therefore, the assumption was false: there cannot exist any $a \in A$ that is preferred to $c(A)$.

Hence:

$$\boxed{c(A) \succeq a \qquad \forall a \in A.}$$
Uniqueness & Strict Preference

Moreover, for every $a \neq c(A)$, the choice function selects only one element from the pair:

$$c(\{a, c(A)\}) = c(A) \implies a \not\succeq c(A).$$

Therefore:

$$c(A) \succ a \qquad \forall a \in A, \ a \neq c(A).$$

Thus:

$$\boxed{c(A) \text{ is the unique best alternative in } A.}$$
Conclusion

Therefore, the rational preference relation $\succeq$ generates exactly the choices made by $c$, and:

$$\boxed{c \text{ is rationalizable.}}$$
