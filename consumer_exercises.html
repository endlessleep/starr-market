Exercise 1 · Utility Representation
Exercise 1. Utility Representation

A preference relation is represented by the utility function

$$u(x_1,x_2)=x_2+g(x_1),$$

where $g$ is a continuous increasing function. A bundle is represented by $x=(x_1,x_2)$.

  1. Represent the indifference curves associated with $u$.
  2. Show that if $g$ is concave, then the preference relation is convex.

Hint 1. A function $f$ is concave if, for all $x,y\in\mathbb{R}^n$ and $\lambda\in[0,1]$:

$$\begin{gathered} f(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)\\ \geq\lambda f(x)+(1-\lambda)f(y). \end{gathered}$$

Hint 2. A preference relation is convex if, for any $t\in[0,1]$:

$$\begin{gathered} x\succeq y,\quad y\succeq z\\ \implies tx+(1-t)y\succeq z. \end{gathered}$$

1. Represent the indifference curves

Starting point: $u(x_1,x_2)=x_2+g(x_1)$.
Goal: Describe the bundles that give the same utility level.

Step 1 — Fix a utility level

Let $K$ be a fixed utility level. An indifference curve contains all bundles whose utility equals $K$. Following the class correction, we write:

$$K=x_2+g(x_1).$$

Subtract $g(x_1)$ from both sides to express the quantity of good 2 as a function of the quantity of good 1:

$$\boxed{x_2=K-g(x_1).}$$

On the nonnegative consumption set, we draw only the part of this curve where both quantities are nonnegative.

Step 2 — Read the curve

As $x_1$ increases, any increase in $g(x_1)$ must be offset by a decrease in $x_2$ to keep their sum equal to $K$. This explains the downward direction of the curve.

Each fixed value of $K$ defines an indifference curve. Its precise shape depends on $g$. The class sketch below illustrates the case where $g$ is concave, as assumed in Question 2.

2. Show that concavity of $g$ implies convex preferences

Starting point: $g$ is concave and $u(x_1,x_2)=x_2+g(x_1)$ represents preferences.
Goal: Use Hint 2: starting from $x\succeq y$ and $y\succeq z$, show that $tx+(1-t)y\succeq z$ for every $t\in[0,1]$.

Step 1 — Translate the preference comparisons into utility

Take any bundles $x,y,z$ such that $x\succeq y$ and $y\succeq z$, and any $t\in[0,1]$. Since $u$ represents preferences, these assumptions give:

$$u(x)\geq u(y)\geq u(z).$$

In particular, $u(x)\geq u(z)$ and $u(y)\geq u(z)$: both bundles give at least as much utility as $z$. We will use these two inequalities at the end of the calculation.

Also, because $u$ represents preferences, the conclusion we want is equivalent to:

$$u(tx+(1-t)y)\geq u(z).$$

Step 2 — Write the utility of the mixture

$$x=(x_1,x_2),\qquad y=(y_1,y_2).$$

The mixture is a bundle with two coordinates:

$$tx+(1-t)y=\Big( \underbrace{\color{#60a5fa}{tx_1+(1-t)y_1}}_{\color{#60a5fa}{\text{First coordinate}}}, \quad \underbrace{\color{#fbbf24}{tx_2+(1-t)y_2}}_{\color{#fbbf24}{\text{Second coordinate}}} \Big).$$
$$\begin{gathered} tx+(1-t)y\\ =\Big( \underbrace{\color{#60a5fa}{tx_1+(1-t)y_1}}_{\color{#60a5fa}{\text{First coordinate}}}, \quad \underbrace{\color{#fbbf24}{tx_2+(1-t)y_2}}_{\color{#fbbf24}{\text{Second coordinate}}} \Big). \end{gathered}$$
Attention — How to read the utility function
$$u(x_1,x_2)={\color{#fbbf24}{x_2}}+g\big({\color{#60a5fa}{x_1}}\big).$$
  • The SECOND coordinate enters directly.
  • The FIRST coordinate goes inside $g$.

For the mixed bundle, put its coordinates in these same positions.

Substitute the two coordinates:

$$u(tx+(1-t)y)={\color{#fbbf24}{tx_2+(1-t)y_2}}+g\big({\color{#60a5fa}{tx_1+(1-t)y_1}}\big).$$
$$\begin{gathered} u(tx+(1-t)y)\\ ={\color{#fbbf24}{tx_2+(1-t)y_2}}+g\big({\color{#60a5fa}{tx_1+(1-t)y_1}}\big). \end{gathered}$$

Step 3 — Apply concavity of $g$

Because $g$ is concave, Hint 1 applied to $x_1$ and $y_1$ gives:

$$g(tx_1+(1-t)y_1)\geq t\,g(x_1)+(1-t)g(y_1).$$
$$\begin{gathered} g(tx_1+(1-t)y_1)\\ \geq t\,g(x_1)+(1-t)g(y_1). \end{gathered}$$

The two terms outside $g$ stay unchanged. Replace only $g(tx_1+(1-t)y_1)$ by its lower bound, then factor out $t$ from the terms involving $x$ and $1-t$ from those involving $y$:

$$\begin{aligned} u(tx+(1-t)y)&\geq tx_2+(1-t)y_2+t\,g(x_1)+(1-t)g(y_1)\\ &=t[x_2+g(x_1)]+(1-t)[y_2+g(y_1)]\\ &=t\,u(x)+(1-t)u(y). \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{gathered} u(tx+(1-t)y)\\ \begin{aligned} &\geq tx_2+(1-t)y_2\\ &\quad+t\,g(x_1)+(1-t)g(y_1)\\ &=t[x_2+g(x_1)]\\ &\quad+(1-t)[y_2+g(y_1)]\\ &=t\,u(x)+(1-t)u(y). \end{aligned} \end{gathered}$$

The last equality uses $x_2+g(x_1)=u(x)$ and $y_2+g(y_1)=u(y)$.

Step 4 — Compare the mixture with $z$

  1. Start from the two comparisons obtained in Step 1.
    $$\begin{gathered} u(x)\geq u(z),\\ u(y)\geq u(z). \end{gathered}$$
  2. Multiply each inequality by its weight.

    Since $t\geq0$ and $1-t\geq0$, multiplying by these nonnegative weights preserves the inequality signs:

    $$\begin{gathered} t\,u(x)\geq t\,u(z),\\ (1-t)u(y)\geq(1-t)u(z). \end{gathered}$$
  3. Add the two inequalities.

    Add the left-hand sides and the right-hand sides:

    $$t\,u(x)+(1-t)u(y)\geq t\,u(z)+(1-t)u(z).$$
    $$\begin{gathered} t\,u(x)+(1-t)u(y)\\ \geq t\,u(z)+(1-t)u(z). \end{gathered}$$
  4. Factor out $u(z)$ on the right-hand side.
    $$t\,u(z)+(1-t)u(z)=[t+(1-t)]u(z).$$
    $$\begin{gathered} t\,u(z)+(1-t)u(z)\\ =[t+(1-t)]u(z). \end{gathered}$$
  5. Use the fact that the weights add up to one.
    $$t+(1-t)=1.$$

    The right-hand side is therefore $u(z)$, so:

    $$t\,u(x)+(1-t)u(y)\geq u(z).$$
  6. Combine this result with Step 3.

    Step 3 showed that the utility of the mixture is at least the weighted sum. We now know that this sum is at least $u(z)$:

    $$u(tx+(1-t)y)\geq t\,u(x)+(1-t)u(y)\geq u(z).$$
    $$\begin{gathered} u(tx+(1-t)y)\\ \geq t\,u(x)+(1-t)u(y)\geq u(z). \end{gathered}$$
  7. Return to the preference comparison.

    Since $u$ represents preferences:

    $$tx+(1-t)y\succeq z.$$

    The preference relation is therefore convex. $\blacksquare$

Are the observed choices consistent with WARP?

Hint: Is bundle A directly revealed preferred to B? To C? Check the other comparisons too.

Method from Chapter 5: Compare the cost of each alternative with the cost of the chosen bundle, using the prices at that observation. A chosen bundle is directly revealed preferred to every alternative that was affordable.

Step 2 — Read the direct revealed preferences

  • At A: A costs 5. B costs 3 and C costs 4, so both alternatives were affordable. Thus $A\ \text{DRP}\ B$ and $A\ \text{DRP}\ C$.
  • At B: B costs 2. A and C each cost 3, so both were outside this expenditure budget. This observation reveals neither $B\ \text{DRP}\ A$ nor $B\ \text{DRP}\ C$.
  • At C: C costs 5. A costs 4 and B costs 3, so both alternatives were affordable. Thus $C\ \text{DRP}\ A$ and $C\ \text{DRP}\ B$.

Step 3 — Test WARP

Apply the alternative definition of WARP from Chapter 5 to the two distinct bundles A and C. A is chosen at prices $p^A$, and C is chosen at prices $p^C$:

If, when A is chosen,
$$p^A\cdot A\geq p^A\cdot C,$$

then C was affordable. The table gives $5\geq4$, so this condition holds: A is directly revealed preferred to C.

Then it must be the case that
$$p^C\cdot C<p^C\cdot A.$$

This would require $5<4$, which is false. In fact, A costs 4 when C is chosen for 5: A is still affordable, so C is directly revealed preferred to A.

The first observation satisfies the “if” condition, but the second fails the required conclusion. Equivalently, in the course’s first formulation, it must not be the case that $p^C\cdot C\geq p^C\cdot A$; yet the data give exactly this forbidden comparison, $5\geq4$.

The comparisons involving B do not create a reversal: B is affordable at A and C, but neither A nor C is affordable at B.

The choices violate WARP. The violation is between A and C: each is chosen on an occasion when the other is strictly cheaper.
Exercise 3 · Inference from Consumption Choices
Exercise 3. Inference from Consumption Choices

Consider an economy with two goods and price-taking consumers. Alice and Bob are rational consumers with strictly monotone and strictly convex preferences. Alice has a fixed income $w$, and Bob has an income of 5.

  1. At prices $(p_1,p_2)=(1,1)$, Alice chooses $x=(1,3)$. At prices $(p'_1,p'_2)$, Alice chooses $x'=(3,1)$ and Bob chooses $x''=(5,1)$. What can you say about $(p'_1,p'_2)$?
    Hint: Consumers use all of their income to maximize their utility.
  2. Can you say anything about how Alice ranks $(2,2)$ and $(5,\tfrac12)$?

1. Find the new prices

Starting point: Strict monotonicity and the observed choices.
Goal: Use the budget equalities to identify $w$, $p'_1$ and $p'_2$.

Step 1 — Find Alice’s income

With strictly monotone preferences, extra goods are desirable. If some income were left unspent, the consumer could buy a little more and be better off. As the hint states, the chosen bundle therefore uses the entire income.

At the original prices $(1,1)$, Alice chooses $(1,3)$:

$$w=1(1)+1(3)=1+3=4.$$

Her income is fixed, so she still has 4 at the new prices.

Step 2 — Write the two new budget equalities

Alice spends 4 on three units of good 1 and one unit of good 2:

$$3p'_1+p'_2=4.$$

Bob spends 5 on five units of good 1 and one unit of good 2:

$$5p'_1+p'_2=5.$$

Both consumers face the same prices. Each buys one unit of good 2, while Bob buys two additional units of good 1.

Step 3 — Solve for the prices

Subtract Alice’s equation from Bob’s. The $p'_2$ terms cancel, and $5p'_1-3p'_1=2p'_1$:

$$\begin{aligned} 2p'_1&=5-4\\ &=1,\\ p'_1&=\frac12. \end{aligned}$$

Substitute this value into Alice’s equation:

$$\begin{aligned} p'_2&=4-3p'_1\\ &=4-\frac32\\ &=\frac82-\frac32\\ &=\frac52. \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{(p'_1,p'_2)=\left(\frac12,\frac52\right).}$$

Check: Alice’s expenditure is $\frac32+\frac52=4$, and Bob’s is $\frac52+\frac52=5$, as required.

2. Rank $(2,2)$ and $(5,\tfrac12)$

Let $x=(1,3)$ and $x'=(3,1)$, and define:

$$a=(2,2),\qquad b=\left(5,\frac12\right).$$

We want to compare $a$ and $b$.

Step 1 — Revealed preference at the original prices

At the original prices $p=(1,1)$, Alice chooses $x=(1,3)$. Her income is:

$$1(1)+1(3)=4.$$

Check $x'=(3,1)$. Its cost at the same prices is:

$$p\cdot x'=1(3)+1(1)=4.$$

So $x'$ was also affordable when Alice chose $x$. Therefore, by revealed preference, $x\succeq x'$, that is:

$$\boxed{(1,3)\succeq(3,1).}$$

Also check $a=(2,2)$. Its cost at the same prices is:

$$p\cdot a=1(2)+1(2)=4.$$

So $a$ was also affordable when Alice chose $x$. Therefore, $x\succeq a$, that is:

$$\boxed{(1,3)\succeq(2,2).}$$

Step 2 — Compare $x'$ and $b$ at the new prices

From Question 1, the new prices are:

$$p'=\left(\frac12,\frac52\right)=(0.5,2.5).$$

At these prices, Alice chooses $x'=(3,1)$ and has income 4. Now calculate the cost of $b=(5,\tfrac12)$:

$$\begin{aligned} p'\cdot b&=\frac12(5)+\frac52\left(\frac12\right)\\ &=2.5+1.25\\ &=3.75<4. \end{aligned}$$

Therefore, $b$ was strictly affordable when Alice chose $x'$. By revealed preference, together with strict monotonicity, $x'\succ b$, that is:

$$\boxed{(3,1)\succ\left(5,\frac12\right).}$$
Intuition. Since $b$ costs strictly less than Alice’s income, she would have money left over. Under strict monotonicity, she could use this remaining income to obtain a strictly better bundle, so $b$ cannot be as good as the chosen optimum $x'$.

Step 3 — Use strict convexity

Notice that $a=(2,2)$ is exactly the 50–50 mixture of $x$ and $x'$:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac12x+\frac12x'&=\frac12(1,3)+\frac12(3,1)\\ &=\left(\frac{1+3}{2},\frac{3+1}{2}\right)\\ &=(2,2). \end{aligned}$$

Therefore:

$$\boxed{a=(2,2)=\frac12x+\frac12x'.}$$

From Step 1, $x\succeq x'$. Also, by reflexivity, $x'\succeq x'$. Thus both bundles are at least as good as the benchmark $x'$:

$$x\succeq x',\qquad x'\succeq x'.$$

Since preferences are strictly convex, a true mixture of these two distinct bundles, with both weights equal to $\frac12$, is strictly better than $x'$. Hence:

$$\frac12x+\frac12x'\succ x'.$$

Since the mixture is $a=(2,2)$:

$$\boxed{(2,2)\succ(3,1).}$$
Intuition: mixture of better/worse $\succ$ worse.

Strict convexity implies that a true mixture of a better bundle and a worse bundle is strictly better than the worse bundle.

Step 4 — Use transitivity

We now have $(2,2)\succ(3,1)$ from Step 3 and $(3,1)\succ(5,\tfrac12)$ from Step 2:

$$\begin{gathered} (2,2)\succ(3,1),\\ (3,1)\succ\left(5,\frac12\right). \end{gathered}$$

Therefore, by transitivity:

$$\boxed{(2,2)\succ\left(5,\frac12\right).}$$

Alice strictly prefers $a=(2,2)$ to $b=(5,\tfrac12)$. The complete comparison is:

$$(2,2)\succ(3,1)\succ\left(5,\frac12\right).$$
