Quasilinearity, the Slutsky equation, and welfare effects of a price change.

Exercise 1

Exercise 1. Quasilinearity and Income Effect

A consumer has preferences over the single good $x$ and all other goods $z$ represented by:

$$u(x,z)=\ln(x)+z.$$

Let the price of $x$ be $p>0$, the price of $z$ be unity, and income be $y>0$.

  1. Find the Marshallian demand functions $x^*$ and $z^*$.
  2. Find the indirect utility function.
  3. Use the Slutsky equation to decompose the effect of an own-price change on the demand for $x$ into an income and substitution effect. How can you interpret your result?
  4. Suppose $y>1$, so that the consumer chooses an interior bundle. The price of $x$ rises from $p_0$ to $p_1>p_0$. Show that the change in consumer surplus gives an exact monetary measure of the welfare effect of the price change.

Setting. The budget constraint is $px+z\leq y$, with $x,z\geq0$. Because utility is increasing in $z$, the consumer spends the entire income at the optimum:

$$px+z=y.$$

1. Marshallian demands

Interior candidate

First suppose that both goods are consumed in positive quantities. At an interior optimum, the marginal rate of substitution equals the price ratio:

$$\boxed{MRS_{xz}=\frac{p_x}{p_z}}.$$

Here, $p_x=p$ and $p_z=1$. From $u(x,z)=\ln(x)+z$, the marginal utilities are:

$$MU_x=\frac{1}{x},\qquad MU_z=1.$$

Therefore:

$$MRS_{xz}=\frac{MU_x}{MU_z}=\frac{1/x}{1}=\frac{1}{x}.$$

Using the interior optimality condition:

$$\frac{1}{x}=\frac{p}{1} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad x=\frac{1}{p}.$$

Substitute this quantity into the binding budget constraint:

$$p\left(\frac{1}{p}\right)+z=y \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad z=y-1.$$

Check non-negativity

The interior candidate is feasible only when $z=y-1\geq0$, that is, when $y\geq1$.

If $0<y<1$, the interior candidate would require $z=y-1<0$. This is not feasible. The constraint $z\geq0$ then binds, so the consumer spends all income on $x$:

$$x=\frac{y}{p},\qquad z=0.$$

Marshallian demand

$$\boxed{ x^m(p,y)=\begin{cases} \dfrac{1}{p},&y\geq1,\\[.45em] \dfrac{y}{p},&0<y<1, \end{cases} \qquad z^m(p,y)=\begin{cases} y-1,&y\geq1,\\[.35em] 0,&0<y<1. \end{cases}}$$

2. Indirect utility function

For $y\geq1$, substitute the interior demands into $u(x,z)=\ln(x)+z$:

$$v(p,y)=\ln\left(\frac{1}{p}\right)+(y-1)=y-1-\ln(p).$$

For $0<y<1$, the chosen bundle is $(y/p,0)$, so:

$$v(p,y)=\ln\left(\frac{y}{p}\right)=\ln(y)-\ln(p).$$

Indirect utility

$$\boxed{v(p,y)=\begin{cases} y-1-\ln(p),&y\geq1,\\[.4em] \ln(y)-\ln(p),&0<y<1. \end{cases}}$$

3. Slutsky decomposition for an own-price change

For this one-good price change, the Slutsky equation is:

$$\frac{\partial x^m(p,y)}{\partial p} =\frac{\partial h(p,\bar u)}{\partial p} -x^m(p,y)\frac{\partial x^m(p,y)}{\partial y}.$$

The first term on the right-hand side is the substitution effect. The second term, with its minus sign, is the income effect.

Case 1: $y\geq1$

Here, $x^m(p,y)=1/p$. Hence:

$$\frac{\partial x^m}{\partial p}=-\frac{1}{p^2}, \qquad \frac{\partial x^m}{\partial y}=0.$$

The income effect is zero. Using the expenditure function from the class correction, $e(p,\bar u)=1+\bar u+\ln(p)$, Shephard’s Lemma gives:

$$h(p,\bar u)=\frac{\partial e(p,\bar u)}{\partial p}=\frac{1}{p}, \qquad \frac{\partial h(p,\bar u)}{\partial p}=-\frac{1}{p^2}.$$

Therefore:

$$\underbrace{-\frac{1}{p^2}}_{\text{total effect}} =\underbrace{-\frac{1}{p^2}}_{\text{substitution effect}} -\underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{p}\right)(0)}_{\text{income effect}}.$$

Case 2: $0<y<1$

Here, $x^m(p,y)=y/p$. Therefore:

$$\frac{\partial x^m}{\partial p}=-\frac{y}{p^2}, \qquad \frac{\partial x^m}{\partial y}=\frac{1}{p}.$$

In this corner case, the expenditure function used in the class correction is $e(p,\bar u)=p e^{\bar u}$. By Shephard’s Lemma, the Hicksian demand is therefore $h(p,\bar u)=e^{\bar u}$, so:

$$\frac{\partial h(p,\bar u)}{\partial p}=0.$$

Thus:

$$\underbrace{-\frac{y}{p^2}}_{\text{total effect}} =\underbrace{0}_{\text{substitution effect}} -\underbrace{\left(\frac{y}{p}\right)\left(\frac{1}{p}\right)}_{\text{income effect}}.$$

Interpretation. When $y\geq1$, demand for $x$ does not depend on income, so the own-price effect is entirely a substitution effect. When $0<y<1$, the own-price effect comes entirely from the income effect. In both cases, an increase in $p$ reduces demand for $x$.

4. Consumer surplus and the welfare effect

The question assumes $y>1$, so the consumer remains in the interior case and:

$$x^m(p,y)=\frac{1}{p}.$$

When the price rises from $p_0$ to $p_1>p_0$, the positive consumer-surplus loss is:

$$-\Delta CS=\int_{p_0}^{p_1}\frac{1}{p}\,dp =\ln(p_1)-\ln(p_0) =\ln\left(\frac{p_1}{p_0}\right).$$

Compare with CV and EV

Initially, utility is $u_0=v(p_0,y)=y-1-\ln(p_0)$. Compensating variation restores this utility at the new price:

$$v(p_1,y+CV)=v(p_0,y).$$

Substituting the interior indirect utility function gives:

$$y+CV-1-\ln(p_1)=y-1-\ln(p_0) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad CV=\ln\left(\frac{p_1}{p_0}\right).$$

Equivalent variation gives the same result. It is defined by:

$$v(p_0,y-EV)=v(p_1,y) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad EV=\ln\left(\frac{p_1}{p_0}\right).$$

Conclusion

$$\boxed{CV=EV=-\Delta CS=\ln\left(\frac{p_1}{p_0}\right).}$$

Because the interior demand for $x$ has no income effect, the consumer-surplus loss is an exact monetary measure of the welfare loss.
