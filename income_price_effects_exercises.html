Exercise 1. Quasilinearity and Income Effect

A consumer has preferences over the single good $x$ and all other goods $z$ represented by:

$$u(x,z)=\ln(x)+z.$$

Let the price of $x$ be $p>0$, the price of $z$ be unity, and income be $y>0$.

  1. Find the Marshallian demand functions $x^*$ and $z^*$.
  2. Find the indirect utility function.
  3. Use the Slutsky equation to decompose the effect of an own-price change on the demand for $x$ into an income and substitution effect. How can you interpret your result?
  4. Suppose $y>1$, so that the consumer chooses an interior bundle. The price of $x$ rises from $p_0$ to $p_1>p_0$. Show that the change in consumer surplus gives an exact monetary measure of the welfare effect of the price change.

Setting. As in the course, the budget set is $px+z\leq y$, with $x,z\geq0$. Since utility is increasing in $z$, the budget constraint binds at the optimum.

1. Marshallian demands

Interior candidate

Write the UMP as:

$$\max_{x,z\geq0}\ \ln(x)+z\qquad\text{s.t.}\qquad px+z=y.$$

The Lagrangian is:

$$\mathcal L=\ln(x)+z+\lambda(y-px-z).$$

The first-order conditions are:

$$\frac1x=\lambda p,\qquad 1=\lambda.$$

Substituting $\lambda=1$ into the first condition gives:

$$x=\frac1p.$$

Using the budget constraint then gives:

$$z=y-p\left(\frac1p\right)=y-1.$$

This bundle is feasible with $z\geq0$ precisely when $y\geq1$.

3. Slutsky decomposition for an own-price change

The following question concerns the interior demand, for which $y>1$:

$$x(p,y)=\frac1p.$$

Differentiate with respect to $p$ and to income:

$$\frac{\partial x(p,y)}{\partial p}=-\frac1{p^2},\qquad\frac{\partial x(p,y)}{\partial y}=0.$$

The Slutsky equation for an own-price change is:

$$\frac{\partial x(p,y)}{\partial p}=\frac{\partial h(p,\bar u)}{\partial p}-\left[\frac{\partial x(p,y)}{\partial y}\right]x(p,y).$$

Because $\partial x/\partial y=0$, the income effect is zero:

$$-\left[\frac{\partial x(p,y)}{\partial y}\right]x(p,y)=0.$$

Therefore the substitution effect equals the total effect:

$$\boxed{\frac{\partial h(p,\bar u)}{\partial p}=-\frac1{p^2}.}$$

Interpretation. In the interior region, the demand for $x$ does not change when income changes. Thus an own-price increase affects $x$ only through the substitution effect.

4. Consumer surplus and the welfare effect

For $y>1$, Marshallian demand remains:

$$x(p,y)=\frac1p.$$

The consumer-surplus change caused by the price increase is:

$$\Delta CS=-\int_{p_0}^{p_1}x(p,y)\,dp=-\int_{p_0}^{p_1}\frac1p\,dp=-\ln\left(\frac{p_1}{p_0}\right).$$

Hence the positive consumer-surplus loss is:

$$-\Delta CS=\ln\left(\frac{p_1}{p_0}\right).$$

To compare this with welfare, start from the interior indirect utility:

$$u_0=v(p_0,y)=y-1-\ln(p_0).$$

At prices $p$, the expenditure function associated with the interior solution is:

$$e(p,u)=1+u+\ln(p).$$

Therefore the compensating variation is:

$$\begin{aligned}CV&=e(p_1,u_0)-y\\&=1+\big[y-1-\ln(p_0)\big]+\ln(p_1)-y\\&=\ln\left(\frac{p_1}{p_0}\right).\end{aligned}$$

Conclusion

$$\boxed{CV=-\Delta CS=\ln\left(\frac{p_1}{p_0}\right).}$$

Because the income effect is zero, the course result gives $EV=-\Delta CS=CV$. The consumer-surplus loss is therefore an exact monetary measure of the welfare loss.
