Quasilinearity, the Slutsky equation, and welfare effects of a price change.

Exercise 1

Exercise 1. Quasilinearity and Income Effect

A consumer has preferences over the single good $x$ and all other goods $z$ represented by:

$$u(x,z)=\ln(x)+z.$$

Let the price of $x$ be $p>0$, the price of $z$ be unity, and income be $y>0$.

  1. Find the Marshallian demand functions $x^*$ and $z^*$.
  2. Find the indirect utility function.
  3. Use the Slutsky equation to decompose the effect of an own-price change on the demand for $x$ into an income and substitution effect. How can you interpret your result?
  4. Suppose $y>1$, so that the consumer chooses an interior bundle. The price of $x$ rises from $p_0$ to $p_1>p_0$. Show that the change in consumer surplus gives an exact monetary measure of the welfare effect of the price change.

1. Marshallian demands

Set up the utility-maximization problem

The Marshallian demand is the solution to the consumer’s utility-maximization problem. Here, the consumer chooses $x$ and $z$ to maximize utility for a given income $y$:

$$\max_{x>0,\ z\geq0}\ \ln(x)+z \qquad\text{s.t.}\qquad px+z\leq y.$$

Because utility is strictly increasing in both goods, it must be the case that the budget constraint binds:

$$px+z=y.$$

Interior candidate

First suppose that both goods are consumed in positive quantities. At an interior optimum, the marginal rate of substitution equals the price ratio:

$$\boxed{MRS_{xz}=\frac{p_x}{p_z}}.$$

Here, $p_x=p$ and $p_z=1$. From $u(x,z)=\ln(x)+z$, the marginal utilities are:

$$MU_x=\frac{1}{x},\qquad MU_z=1.$$

Therefore:

$$MRS_{xz}=\frac{MU_x}{MU_z}=\frac{1/x}{1}=\frac{1}{x}.$$

Using the interior optimality condition:

$$\frac{1}{x}=\frac{p}{1} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad x=\frac{1}{p}.$$

Substitute this quantity into the binding budget constraint:

$$p\left(\frac{1}{p}\right)+z=y \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad z=y-1.$$

Corner solution: $0<y<1$

The interior condition gives $x=1/p$. But substituting this candidate into the budget constraint gives $z=y-1<0$. This is not feasible because $z\geq0$. The optimum is therefore at the boundary:

$$\boxed{z=0}.$$

Marshallian demand: income $y$ is fixed. Use the binding budget constraint:

$$px+z=y.$$

Since $z=0$:

$$px=y \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{x^m(p,y)=\frac{y}{p}}.$$

Marshallian demand

$$\boxed{ x^m(p,y)=\begin{cases} \dfrac{1}{p},&y\geq1,\\[.45em] \dfrac{y}{p},&0<y<1, \end{cases} \qquad z^m(p,y)=\begin{cases} y-1,&y\geq1,\\[.35em] 0,&0<y<1. \end{cases}}$$

2. Indirect utility function

For $y\geq1$, substitute the interior demands into $u(x,z)=\ln(x)+z$:

$$v(p,y)=\ln\left(\frac{1}{p}\right)+(y-1)=y-1-\ln(p).$$

For $0<y<1$, the chosen bundle is $(y/p,0)$, so:

$$v(p,y)=\ln\left(\frac{y}{p}\right)=\ln(y)-\ln(p).$$

Indirect utility

$$\boxed{v(p,y)=\begin{cases} y-1-\ln(p),&y\geq1,\\[.4em] \ln(y)-\ln(p),&0<y<1. \end{cases}}$$

3. Slutsky decomposition for an own-price change

The main method starts from the Marshallian demand. The Slutsky equation is:

$$\frac{\partial x^m(p,y)}{\partial p} =\frac{\partial h(p,\bar u)}{\partial p} -x^m(p,y)\frac{\partial x^m(p,y)}{\partial y}.$$

$\partial x^m/\partial p$ is the total own-price effect. $\partial h/\partial p$ is the substitution effect. The term $-x^m\,\partial x^m/\partial y$ is the income effect.

Case 1: $y\geq1$

Marshallian demand is:

$$x^m(p,y)=\frac{1}{p}.$$

Therefore:

$$\frac{\partial x^m}{\partial p}=-\frac{1}{p^2}, \qquad \frac{\partial x^m}{\partial y}=0.$$

Substitute these derivatives directly into the Slutsky equation:

$$-\frac{1}{p^2} =\frac{\partial h}{\partial p} -\frac{1}{p}(0).$$

Therefore:

$$\boxed{\frac{\partial h}{\partial p}=-\frac{1}{p^2}}.$$

The income effect is zero, so the total effect equals the substitution effect: Marshallian demand for $x$ does not depend on income $y$.

$$\underbrace{-\frac{1}{p^2}}_{\text{total effect}} =\underbrace{-\frac{1}{p^2}}_{\text{substitution effect}} -\underbrace{\frac{1}{p}(0)}_{\text{income effect}}.$$

Case 2: $0<y<1$

Marshallian demand is:

$$x^m(p,y)=\frac{y}{p}.$$

Therefore:

$$\frac{\partial x^m}{\partial p}=-\frac{y}{p^2}, \qquad \frac{\partial x^m}{\partial y}=\frac{1}{p}.$$

Substitute these derivatives directly into the Slutsky equation:

$$-\frac{y}{p^2} =\frac{\partial h}{\partial p} -\frac{y}{p}\left(\frac{1}{p}\right).$$
$$-\frac{y}{p^2} =\frac{\partial h}{\partial p} -\frac{y}{p^2},$$

so:

$$\boxed{\frac{\partial h}{\partial p}=0}.$$

The substitution effect is zero. The entire own-price effect is an income effect.

$$\underbrace{-\frac{y}{p^2}}_{\text{total effect}} =\underbrace{0}_{\text{substitution effect}} -\underbrace{\frac{y}{p}\frac{1}{p}}_{\text{income effect}}.$$

Interpret the two cases

When $y\geq1$: the effect is entirely substitution. The interior Marshallian demand is $x^m=1/p$, so $x$ does not depend on income: $\partial x^m/\partial y=0$. If income changes, the consumer adjusts $z$, while the chosen quantity of $x$ stays the same. A change in $p$ therefore changes $x$ only because it changes the relative price of $x$ and $z$: this is the substitution effect.

When $0<y<1$: the effect is entirely income. The consumer is at the corner $z=0$, so all income is spent on $x$ and $x^m=y/p$. There is no $z$ left to reallocate when the price changes. At fixed utility, the consumer must keep $x^h=e^{\bar u}$, which does not depend on $p$; the substitution effect is therefore zero. The fall in $x$ comes only from lower purchasing power, so it is an income effect.

In both cases, an increase in $p$ reduces demand for $x$.

Alternative method — Derive Hicksian demand explicitly

Optional check. This route derives the Hicksian demand directly. It is not needed for the main Slutsky calculation above; Shephard’s Lemma only verifies the result.

The Hicksian demand is the solution to the expenditure-minimization problem:

$$\min_{x>0,\ z\geq0}\ px+z \qquad\text{s.t.}\qquad \ln(x)+z\geq\bar u.$$

Interior Hicksian bundle

For an interior bundle, use the MRS condition directly:

$$MRS_{xz}=\frac{p_x}{p_z} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{1}{x}=p \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{x^h(p,\bar u)=\frac{1}{p}}.$$

Use the binding utility constraint to obtain the quantity of $z$:

$$\bar u=\ln(x^h)+z^h \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad z^h=\bar u+\ln(p).$$

We already have the Hicksian demand for $x$. Shephard’s Lemma is not needed to find it.

Short check — Shephard’s Lemma. The expenditure of this directly obtained bundle is:

$$e(p,\bar u)=p\left(\frac{1}{p}\right)+\bar u+\ln(p) =1+\bar u+\ln(p).$$

Therefore:

$$\frac{\partial e(p,\bar u)}{\partial p} =\frac{1}{p} =x^h(p,\bar u), \qquad \boxed{h(p,\bar u)=x^h(p,\bar u)=\frac{1}{p}}.$$

Corner Hicksian bundle

This corresponds to the Marshallian corner above. The interior condition would give $x=1/p$, but for $0<y<1$ it would imply $z=y-1<0$. Hence the relevant corner has:

$$\boxed{z=0}.$$

For Hicksian demand, utility $\bar u$ is fixed. Use the binding utility constraint:

$$\ln(x)+z=\bar u \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \ln(x)=\bar u \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{x^h(p,\bar u)=e^{\bar u}}.$$

Again, the Hicksian demand has already been obtained directly.

Short check — Shephard’s Lemma. The expenditure of this bundle is:

$$e(p,\bar u)=p\,e^{\bar u}.$$

Therefore:

$$\frac{\partial e(p,\bar u)}{\partial p} =e^{\bar u} =x^h(p,\bar u), \qquad \boxed{h(p,\bar u)=x^h(p,\bar u)=e^{\bar u}}.$$

4. Consumer surplus and the welfare effect

Assume $y>1$, so the consumer chooses an interior bundle throughout the price change. When the price rises from $p_0$ to $p_1>p_0$, Marshallian demand is:

$$x^m(p,y)=\frac{1}{p}.$$

There are three equivalent ways to compute the compensating variation. They express the same welfare concept using the expenditure function, the indirect utility function, or Hicksian demand.

Here, $x^m(p,y)$ is the quantity chosen at given prices and income; $v(p,y)$ is the maximum attainable utility; $h(p,u)$ is the cost-minimizing quantity for a target utility; and $e(p,u)$ is the minimum expenditure needed to reach that utility.

Consumer-surplus loss

The positive loss of consumer surplus is the area under Marshallian demand between the two prices:

$$-\Delta CS=\int_{p_0}^{p_1}\frac{1}{p}\,dp =\ln(p_1)-\ln(p_0) =\ln\left(\frac{p_1}{p_0}\right).$$

Method 1 — Expenditure function (class method)

Initial utility is:

$$u_0=v(p_0,y)=y-1-\ln(p_0).$$

For the interior case, the expenditure function is:

$$e(p,u)=1+u+\ln(p).$$

It gives the minimum expenditure needed to reach utility $u$ at price $p$. At the new price $p_1$, the compensation needed to restore initial utility $u_0$ is:

$$CV=e(p_1,u_0)-y.$$

Substitute $u_0$:

$$\begin{aligned} CV&=1+u_0+\ln(p_1)-y\\ &=1+y-1-\ln(p_0)+\ln(p_1)-y\\ &=\ln\left(\frac{p_1}{p_0}\right). \end{aligned}$$
Therefore, $CV=-\Delta CS$. The consumer-surplus loss is an exact monetary measure of the welfare loss from this price increase.
Alternative method 1 — Using indirect utility

In the interior case:

$$v(p,y)=y-1-\ln(p).$$

CV is defined by restoring the initial utility at the new price:

$$v(p_1,y+CV)=v(p_0,y)=u_0.$$

Hence:

$$y+CV-1-\ln(p_1)=y-1-\ln(p_0) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad CV=\ln\left(\frac{p_1}{p_0}\right).$$

This is the same as the expenditure-function definition: $v(p_1,y+CV)=u_0$ means that $y+CV$ is the minimum income required to reach $u_0$ at $p_1$. Thus $y+CV=e(p_1,u_0)$ and $CV=e(p_1,u_0)-y$.

Alternative method 2 — Using Hicksian demand

At the initial utility level $u_0$, the interior Hicksian demand is:

$$h(p,u_0)=\frac{1}{p}.$$

Compensating variation is the extra expenditure needed to keep utility fixed while the price moves from $p_0$ to $p_1$:

$$CV=\int_{p_0}^{p_1}h(p,u_0)\,dp =\int_{p_0}^{p_1}\frac{1}{p}\,dp =\ln\left(\frac{p_1}{p_0}\right).$$

This is the expenditure-function method written in demand form. By Shephard’s Lemma, $h(p,u)=\partial e(p,u)/\partial p$; and initially, $e(p_0,u_0)=y$.

Final synthesis

$$\boxed{\begin{aligned} CV&=e(p_1,u_0)-y\\ &\Longleftrightarrow v(p_1,y+CV)=u_0\\ &\Longleftrightarrow \int_{p_0}^{p_1}h(p,u_0)\,dp \end{aligned}}$$
$$\boxed{CV=\ln\left(\frac{p_1}{p_0}\right)=-\Delta CS.}$$

These methods do not define different compensating variations. They compute the same welfare loss from three equivalent perspectives.

Exercise 2. Deadweight Loss

The government wants to raise tax revenue by imposing a per-unit tax $t>0$ on good 1. The initial price vector is $p=(p_1,p_{-1})$, where $p_{-1}$ collects the prices of all other goods.

$$\boxed{p_1'=p_1+t},\qquad p'=(p_1',p_{-1}).$$

The consumer has wealth $w$. Initial utility is $u=v(p,w)$ and utility after the per-unit tax is $u'=v(p',w)$. The quantity actually purchased after the tax is $x_1(p',w)$, so tax revenue is:

$$\boxed{T=t\,x_1(p',w).}$$

The tax is $t$ per unit, and the relevant quantity is the quantity actually purchased after the tax. Demand is therefore evaluated at the new price vector $p'$.

  1. Under which condition is the consumer worse off under the per-unit tax than under a lump-sum tax raising the same revenue $T$? Use EV.
  2. Define the deadweight loss $DW$ of the per-unit tax relative to a lump-sum tax raising the same revenue. Show that $DW>0$ and represent it graphically using Hicksian demand. Assume Hicksian demand is decreasing in its own price.
  3. Suppose the government compensates the consumer after the per-unit tax so that she remains at her initial utility $u$. Express the deadweight loss and represent it graphically using Hicksian demand.

Under a lump-sum tax raising the same revenue $T$, prices stay at $p$, while wealth becomes $w-T$. Utility would be:

$$\boxed{v(p,w-T).}$$

EV measures the welfare loss caused by the per-unit tax in monetary terms: it is the amount of wealth that could be taken away at the original prices $p$ to make the consumer exactly as well off as after the per-unit tax.

$$\boxed{v(p,w-EV)=v(p',w)=u'.}$$

At the old prices $p$, the minimum wealth required to attain the post-tax utility $u'$ is $e(p,u')$. Starting from wealth $w$, the amount that can be removed is:

$$\boxed{EV=w-e(p,u').}$$

Using $v(p',w)=v(p,w-EV)$ in the original utility comparison gives:

If $EV>T$, the per-unit tax causes a welfare loss equivalent to losing more money than the government actually collects. It is therefore worse for the consumer than a lump-sum tax collecting the same revenue.

Question 2 — Deadweight loss at post-tax utility $u'$

1. Define the welfare loss and rewrite EV

Deadweight loss is the additional welfare loss from the distortionary per-unit tax relative to a lump-sum tax raising the same revenue:

$$\boxed{DW=EV-T.}$$

From Question 1, $EV=w-e(p,u')$. After the tax, the consumer faces $p'$, has wealth $w$, and attains $u'$. Therefore $e(p',u')=w$, so:

$$EV=e(p',u')-e(p,u').$$

Only the price of good 1 changes. By Shephard’s Lemma, $\partial e(p,u')/\partial p_1=h_1(p,u')$. Hence:

$$\boxed{EV=\int_{p_1}^{p_1'}h_1(\tilde p_1,p_{-1},u')\,d\tilde p_1.}$$
Key idea. $\tilde p_1$ is just the temporary/intermediate value of the price of good 1 as it moves from $p_1$ to $p_1'$ inside the integral.

Therefore $h_1(\tilde p_1,p_{-1},u')$ changes as $\tilde p_1$ changes: EV follows the Hicksian demand curve over the entire price interval.

2. Rewrite tax revenue

At prices $p'$, the Marshallian consumer with wealth $w$ attains utility $u'$. Marshallian and Hicksian demand therefore coincide at this point:

$$\boxed{x_1(p',w)=h_1(p',u').}$$

Thus:

$$T=t\,h_1(p',u')=(p_1'-p_1)h_1(p',u').$$

Graphically, $t=p_1'-p_1$ is the height of the tax-revenue rectangle and $h_1(p',u')$ is its width. Its area is exactly $T$.

3. Why tax revenue can also be written as an integral

$$\boxed{T=\int_{p_1}^{p_1'}h_1(p',u')\,d\tilde p_1.}$$

This is valid because $h_1(p',u')$ contains the fixed final price vector $p'$, not the moving variable $\tilde p_1$. With respect to $d\tilde p_1$, it is a constant:

$$\int_{p_1}^{p_1'}h_1(p',u')\,d\tilde p_1 =h_1(p',u')(p_1'-p_1) =t\,h_1(p',u')=T.$$
Key idea. In EV, $h_1(\tilde p_1,p_{-1},u')$ varies with $\tilde p_1$. In tax revenue, $h_1(p',u')$ is fixed with respect to $\tilde p_1$.

4. Obtain and sign the deadweight loss

$$\begin{aligned} DW&=\int_{p_1}^{p_1'}h_1(\tilde p_1,p_{-1},u')\,d\tilde p_1 -\int_{p_1}^{p_1'}h_1(p',u')\,d\tilde p_1\\[.4em] &=\int_{p_1}^{p_1'}\left[h_1(\tilde p_1,p_{-1},u')-h_1(p',u')\right]d\tilde p_1. \end{aligned}$$

For every intermediate price $p_1\leq\tilde p_1

$$h_1(\tilde p_1,p_{-1},u')>h_1(p',u').$$

The integrand is positive over the price interval. Hence:

$$\boxed{DW>0.}$$

EV is the whole monetary welfare loss from the per-unit tax. One part becomes government revenue $T$; the remaining part is deadweight loss.

Question 3 — Tax plus compensation back to initial utility $u$

1. Compensation required

The tax is the same, but the government now compensates the consumer so that utility remains at its initial level $u$. Initially, $e(p,u)=w$. At the new prices, the minimum expenditure needed to reach the same initial utility is $e(p',u)$.

$$\boxed{CV=e(p',u)-e(p,u).}$$

By Shephard’s Lemma:

$$\boxed{CV=\int_{p_1}^{p_1'}h_1(\tilde p_1,p_{-1},u)\,d\tilde p_1.}$$

Here Hicksian demand is evaluated at the initial utility $u$, because the compensation explicitly returns the consumer to that level.

2. Tax revenue after compensation

After compensation, prices are $p'$ and utility is $u$. The compensated quantity of good 1 is $h_1(p',u)$, so revenue is:

$$\boxed{t\,h_1(p',u).}$$

This is again a true rectangle: height $t=p_1'-p_1$ and width $h_1(p',u)$.

3. Deadweight loss after compensation

$$DW=CV-t\,h_1(p',u).$$

Because $h_1(p',u)$ is fixed with respect to $\tilde p_1$:

$$t\,h_1(p',u)=\int_{p_1}^{p_1'}h_1(p',u)\,d\tilde p_1.$$

Therefore:

$$DW=\int_{p_1}^{p_1'}\left[h_1(\tilde p_1,p_{-1},u)-h_1(p',u)\right]d\tilde p_1.$$

Hicksian demand is decreasing in its own price, so the integrand is positive and:

$$\boxed{DW>0.}$$

Question 2 vs Question 3

The algebraic construction is almost exactly the same. The essential difference is the utility level held fixed, and therefore the Hicksian demand curve used.

$$\begin{array}{c|c|c} &\text{Question 2}&\text{Question 3}\\ \hline \text{Fixed utility}&u'\ \text{(post-tax)}&u\ \text{(initial)}\\ \text{Hicksian demand}&h_1(\cdot,u')&h_1(\cdot,u)\\ \text{Welfare measure}&EV&CV\\ \text{Deadweight loss}&EV-t\,h_1(p',u')&CV-t\,h_1(p',u) \end{array}$$
$$\boxed{\text{Q2: same construction at }u'\qquad\text{Q3: same construction at }u.}$$
